## How Does the Six-Phase Wealth Blueprint Work?

The Elite Physician Wealth Blueprint™ is presented as a six-phase financial planning process for physicians and other medical professionals. Instead of treating taxes, investments, retirement, insurance, estate planning, and practice decisions as separate projects, the process is designed to connect them through one coordinated strategy.

That structure may be helpful for physicians whose financial priorities change throughout their careers. A resident may be preparing for a major income increase. An established specialist may be balancing retirement contributions, private practice ownership, and family goals. A physician nearing retirement may be focused on creating income, managing healthcare costs, and passing wealth to the next generation.

According to the firm’s [wealth planning process](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/our-process), the six phases are Discover, Assess, Strategize, Implement, Optimize, and Review. The process is intended to create a clear framework for decision-making, but it cannot guarantee investment returns, tax savings, or retirement outcomes. Results depend on the recommendations, implementation, market conditions, tax law changes, and your own financial decisions.

### Discover: define personal, professional, and family goals

The Discover phase establishes the context for the planning relationship. The firm describes this step as a review of your goals, family priorities, income structure, employee benefits, tax situation, retirement accounts, investments, insurance, estate documents, debt, and practice ownership details when applicable.

This broader conversation matters because a recommendation that looks sensible by itself may not fit your full financial picture. A practice owner’s cash flow and business responsibilities may affect retirement contributions, insurance needs, and estate planning. A physician early in their career may need a different strategy that accounts for student debt, contract negotiations, and an expected change in income.

The goal is to create a complete starting point before recommendations are made. Ask how information is collected, who reviews it, and which professionals may need to provide additional documents.

### Assess: review cash flow, taxes, investments, protection, and retirement

During the Assess phase, the firm evaluates the areas that influence your financial position and future choices. This may include tax efficiency, retirement readiness, investment alignment, risk management, asset protection, estate planning, practice planning, and cash-flow priorities.

For physicians, cash flow deserves close attention. A high income does not automatically mean your money is being used efficiently. Bonuses, call pay, partnership income, benefits, and practice distributions can complicate decisions about spending, saving, investing, and taxes.

The assessment should also consider how different parts of your plan work together. Your investment strategy should reflect your time horizon and ability to tolerate risk, while insurance and estate documents should account for your family and professional responsibilities. Reviewing these areas together can make gaps and competing priorities easier to identify.

### Strategize: connect recommendations into one plan

The Strategize phase turns the information gathered during discovery and assessment into a personalized plan. The firm describes this plan as connecting tax planning, retirement planning, wealth management, practice decisions, and legacy objectives.

For a physician, that may mean coordinating several decisions instead of handling them one at a time. A retirement plan contribution could affect taxes and cash flow. A practice purchase could influence debt, insurance, estate documents, and investment capacity. A decision to reduce clinical hours could affect income projections and the timing of retirement.

A useful strategy should explain what to do and why each recommendation fits your goals. Ask which actions have the highest priority, what assumptions support them, and what could cause the plan to change. You can also request a clear distinction between urgent actions, longer-term priorities, and decisions that require input from a CPA or attorney.

### Implement: put the strategy into action with outside professionals

A financial plan only has practical value when the recommended actions are completed. During the Implement phase, the firm says it helps organize next steps and coordinate with your CPA, attorney, third-party administrator, payroll provider, insurance professionals, and other advisors.

This coordination can be useful when one decision affects several parts of your financial life. Setting up or revising a retirement plan, for example, may involve plan design, payroll procedures, tax reporting, employee communication, and investment decisions. Estate planning may require legal documents that a financial advisor cannot prepare.

Before moving forward, clarify who is responsible for each task. Ask whether the firm will make introductions, attend meetings, track deadlines, or simply provide recommendations. You should also understand which services are included in the engagement and when an outside professional may charge a separate fee.

### Optimize: adjust for income, markets, and life changes

The Optimize phase focuses on keeping the strategy relevant as your circumstances change. The firm describes monitoring changes in income, family priorities, practice status, tax rules, markets, and retirement projections.

Physicians may experience significant changes over the course of a career. You may change employers, become a partner, sell a practice, receive a new compensation arrangement, take parental leave, reduce clinical work, or move into an administrative role. Each change can affect savings, taxes, insurance, investment risk, and retirement timing.

Optimization should involve more than reacting to market headlines. Ask what events trigger a planning review and how often your assumptions are updated. It is also helpful to understand whether recommendations will change because of a genuine shift in your goals or because of short-term market movement. A thoughtful process should remain adaptable without encouraging unnecessary changes.

### Review: revisit goals, progress, and assumptions

The Review phase creates a regular checkpoint for your financial plan. According to the firm’s process, each year includes an Annual Elite Wealth Review™ intended to keep the plan aligned, updated, and actionable.

An annual review may cover progress toward savings and retirement goals, changes in income, investment performance, tax developments, insurance coverage, estate documents, and practice decisions. It should also revisit the assumptions behind your projections. Retirement readiness, for example, may look different after a change in spending, investment risk, desired retirement age, or expected clinical income.

Before each meeting, ask what information you should provide and which decisions need attention. Afterward, request a written list of action items, responsible parties, and target dates. This makes it easier to distinguish a meaningful planning review from a general account update.

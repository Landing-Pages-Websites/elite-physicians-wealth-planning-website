## What Physician Practice Owner Planning Covers

Employed physicians usually plan around a salary, a benefits menu, and a household budget. An owner adds a second balance sheet. The practice has its own cash, debt, staff costs, and retirement plan. The household has its own reserves, taxes, and goals. Physician practice owner planning is the habit of reading both before you change either one.

The topics worth bringing to that review are owner compensation, retirement-plan design, cash balance and defined benefit opportunities, profit sharing, employee benefits, business insurance, key-person protection, buy-sell coordination, succession, and exit planning. Treat that as a discussion list, not as a promise that every topic applies to every practice, and not as a fixed menu from one meeting. The sequence below is what this guide is for.

Start with facts you can point to, not a feeling that the year went well. Useful facts include:

* Trailing twelve-month collections, owner pay, and distributions.
* Practice cash after payroll, rent, and debt service.
* Household cash that is not sitting in the practice account.
* The current retirement plan type, who is eligible, and last year's contributions.
* Any buy-sell, loan, or lease that would matter in a sale or a partner exit.

If those numbers live in five different inboxes, the first planning step is to put them on one page. Coordination does not mean one person performs every professional service. Your CPA, attorney, third-party administrator, and insurance professionals still own their work. The plan's job is to keep their advice from colliding.

## Put Household Cash and Practice Cash on One Page

Many owners treat the practice account as a personal reserve. That works until payroll, a tax estimate, and a home project hit the same week. Separate the two pools before you decide what the practice can afford.

Household cash should cover living costs if owner pay pauses. Practice cash should cover payroll, rent, debt, and a short disruption in collections. Mixing them hides both risks. A large practice balance can look like wealth while the household has almost nothing outside the business.

Use a simple split:

1. List three months of household spending that does not depend on the practice.
2. List one payroll cycle plus one month of fixed practice costs.
3. Mark any practice cash already promised to taxes, debt, or a partner buyout.
4. Only then decide what is available for a retirement contribution, equipment, or a distribution.

This is also where personal debt and student loans belong. Paying them from a distribution is a household choice. Funding them by skipping a retirement contribution is a practice choice. Write down which one you are making. Investment decisions are more useful after that split is clear, because the portfolio should not be the emergency fund for the practice. [How physicians build a practical cash reserve](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/blog/emergency-fund-for-physicians) is the household side of that question. This section only asks whether the reserve is sitting inside the practice.

## Set Owner Pay Before You Redesign Benefits

Owner pay is the hinge. A higher salary can raise payroll tax and change what a retirement plan may contribute. A lower salary with larger distributions can look efficient and still leave the household uneven through the year. Neither choice is automatically better. The question is whether pay matches the work, the cash cycle, and the household need.

Ask three questions before you change the mix:

* Can the household pay its bills in a slow collections month without a special distribution?
* Does the salary still make sense next to what a non-owner physician would earn for similar clinical work?
* Have you and your CPA looked at how that salary feeds the retirement plan you already have?

Do not use this article to pick an entity or an S corporation election. That comparison belongs with your CPA and in a tax-focused review. Here, the point is narrower. Change pay only after you know what the household must receive every month. Then ask whether the retirement plan and the tax plan still fit.

Tax planning for an owner is not a December project. Income timing, retirement contributions, and charitable gifts all move the same taxable income. If you change pay in March and wait until November to tell the CPA, you have already spent the flexibility. [Tax planning for a physician private practice](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/blog/tax-planning-physician-practice) covers the tax tactics. This section only sets the pay number those tactics have to live with.

## Compare the Plan Designs Owners Actually Choose Between

For many owners, retirement-plan design is one of the largest financial decisions in the practice. It should be reviewed with owner goals, employee ages and pay, cash flow, tax objectives, and the work of running the plan. A plan that fit three years ago can be the wrong plan after you hire, after a partner joins, or after profits jump.

The table below is a decision screen, not a plan document and not a recommendation. Contribution room, staff cost, and funding rules depend on your census and your plan document. Your TPA and CPA have to price any change before you adopt it.

| Design.                               | What it tends to do.                                                                                | The tradeoff.                                                                        | A reason to reopen it.                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 401(k) with a match or profit sharing | Lets owners defer pay and may add an employer amount up to the annual defined contribution ceiling. | Owner room is capped. A richer formula often raises the staff cost.                  | Profits rose, but the current formula cannot use more of them for owners without a staff-cost problem.                 |
| Cash balance or defined benefit layer | Can allow a much larger owner contribution than a 401(k) alone, often for older owners.             | Funding can be required in a weak year. Administration is heavier.                   | Cash is steady, the household reserve is already funded, and the owner wants more shelter than the 401(k) can provide. |
| Profit-sharing allocation change      | Changes how the employer amount is split among owners and staff.                                    | Testing and the census decide whether the split is allowed. It is not a free choice. | A new physician, a long-tenured employee, or a partner exit changed who is eligible.                                   |
| Keep the current plan and wait        | Avoids a new required contribution while cash, debt, or a buy-in is tight.                          | You may leave tax-deferred room unused in a strong year.                             | One payroll cycle of practice cash, or three months of household cash, is not actually in the bank.                    |

Use the screen this way. If household reserves are thin, or practice cash cannot cover one payroll cycle plus a month of fixed costs, the last row wins even when profits look good. If reserves are funded and the only open question is staff cost, ask the TPA to price the 401(k) formula and any cash balance layer side by side. Do not adopt the larger design because a peer uses it.

The IRS adjusts retirement plan dollar limits each year. For 2026, the elective deferral limit is $24,500, the standard catch-up is $8,000, and the overall defined contribution limit is $72,000. A higher catch-up of $11,250 can apply for some people ages 60 to 63. The defined benefit limit is $290,000. Those figures are ceilings, not a target for your practice. Your limit depends on compensation, age, the plan document, and staff demographics. Confirm the current numbers in [IRS cost-of-living adjustment tables](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/cola-increases-for-dollar-limitations-on-benefits-and-contributions) before you budget a contribution.

A cash balance design can create a required funding obligation in a weak year. That is why the household reserve from the earlier section matters. Do not adopt a plan that assumes every future year looks like the best recent year. [Retirement plans for doctors with practice ownership](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/blog/retirement-plans-doctors-ownership) goes further into plan mechanics if this screen says the design is due for a real comparison.

## A Hypothetical Year for a Two-Physician Practice

Numbers make the sequence easier to see. The figures below are hypothetical. They are not a client result, not a projection, and not a recommendation.

Two physician owners share a practice. Trailing collections are $2,400,000. After staff, rent, supplies, debt, and other costs, about $700,000 remains before owner retirement contributions. Each owner currently takes $280,000 of salary. The practice has a 401(k) with a match. Household spending for one owner is about $180,000 a year. That owner has $40,000 in cash outside the practice.

The tempting move is to add a large cash balance contribution because the practice has the profit. Run the sequence first.

* Three months of that owner's household spending is about $45,000. Personal cash is $40,000, so the household reserve is slightly short before any new plan obligation.
* One month of fixed practice costs might be $90,000 in this example. Confirm that the operating account can hold that amount after taxes and debt, not just on the best month.
* A new plan that requires a large owner contribution also creates a staff cost. If that combined cost is $150,000 and collections drop 10 percent, the practice may need to fund the plan from cash it meant to distribute.

Put the same facts through the comparison table. The 401(k) row is the plan they already have, so the open question is whether a richer formula or a cash balance layer is worth pricing. The cash balance row fails the reserve test: personal cash is still short of three months of spending. The wait row is the honest one until that gap is closed, even though $700,000 of pre-contribution profit looks like room.

In this illustration, the first dollar is not a new plan. The first dollar fills the household reserve from $40,000 toward at least $45,000, and preferably toward a number the owner can name. The second step is a meeting with the TPA and CPA to price the staff cost of a richer design. The third step is a distribution policy that does not empty the operating account to hit a contribution target.

Change any input and the order can change. An owner with two years of personal reserves and stable collections may be ready for the plan conversation now. An owner funding a buy-in, a home, and a loan at the same time may need to wait. The point of the example is the order, not the $2,400,000.

## Time the Exit Conversation Before It Is Urgent

Sale, internal succession, an associate buy-in, or a gradual wind-down all change personal wealth. The financial plan should exist before the exit is urgent. Waiting until a buyer asks for records forces every other decision into a short window.

You do not need a valuation this month to start. You do need to know which path is even plausible:

* Who could buy, and on what rough timeline?
* Does a buy-sell already say what happens if a partner dies, becomes disabled, or wants out?
* Which personal expenses depend on owner pay that would stop or shrink after a sale?
* Is the retirement plan something a buyer would keep, freeze, or terminate?

Key-person coverage and a funded buy-sell are part of that picture, not a separate insurance shopping trip. If the practice cannot operate or pay the household after one owner leaves, the exit plan is incomplete even if the clinical work is excellent. [How to sell a medical practice](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/blog/how-to-sell-medical-practice) covers the transaction steps. This section only decides whether you should open that work in the next twelve months.

Legacy planning belongs in the same year as an exit conversation. Beneficiary forms, personal guarantees on practice debt, and a buy-sell payout can override what a will says. An attorney drafts the documents. The planning review checks that the payout, the household, and the documents describe the same outcome.

## Use a One-Page Annual Review

Physician practice owner planning fails when it happens only during a crisis or a tax deadline. A short annual review is enough if it follows the same order every time.

1. Update collections, owner pay, distributions, practice cash, and household cash.
2. Note what changed: new hires, a partner, debt, real estate, or a health event.
3. Ask the CPA what the pay mix did to taxes and what is still adjustable.
4. Ask the TPA whether the retirement plan still fits owner goals and staff facts, using the comparison table above as the agenda.
5. Name the exit path that is most likely in the next five years, even if you are not selling.
6. Write one decision for this year and one decision you are explicitly delaying.

Bring the same page to each specialist. When the CPA, the TPA, and the attorney see different versions of the year, their advice will not line up. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is physician practice owner planning?

It is a coordinated review of owner pay, household reserves, practice cash, retirement-plan design, risk coverage, and exit timing. The goal is to see how a business decision changes personal wealth before you commit.

### How is this different from a medical practice business plan?

A business plan covers market, staffing, operations, and financial projections for the practice as a company. Physician practice owner planning starts from the owner's household and asks which practice decisions the household can actually support. [Business planning for a medical practice](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/blog/business-planning-medical-practice) is the operating plan. This article is the household-and-practice sequence.

### When should owners review retirement plan design?

Review it when profits, staffing, or a partner's timeline change, and at least once a year with your TPA. A plan that maximized contributions in a strong year can become a funding problem in a weak one. Reopen the comparison if household reserves are not funded yet, because a richer design can create a bill the household cannot absorb.

### Do I need a practice valuation before I start?

No. A valuation matters for a sale, a buy-in, a dispute, or a lender. You can still organize pay, cash, and plan design without one. Get a valuation when a transaction date is real, not as a substitute for the annual review.

### Who should be in the meeting?

You and the owners who share cash decisions. Then the CPA, the TPA, and, when documents or a buy-sell are in play, the attorney. One advisor should keep the household view so the specialists are not each optimizing a different goal.

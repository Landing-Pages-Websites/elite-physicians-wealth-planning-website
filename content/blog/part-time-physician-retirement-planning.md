Part-time physician retirement planning starts with a practical question: what changes if you reduce clinical hours now, and what might that mean for the date you can stop working altogether? The answer depends on more than salary. Spending, savings, benefits, practice responsibilities, family priorities, and the kind of work you want to keep doing all belong in the same decision.

## What does part-time work mean for a physician’s retirement plan?

“Part-time” can describe very different arrangements. One physician may move from five clinical days to four while keeping the same employer and benefits. Another may leave a full-time employed role for occasional shifts. A practice owner may reduce patient hours but retain leadership duties. A surgeon may step away from call coverage but continue in clinic. A physician close to retirement may cut back gradually, while a resident or early-career attending may choose a reduced schedule for family or health reasons.

These arrangements do not have identical financial effects. A lower schedule can mean lower gross compensation, different benefit eligibility, fewer employer contributions, or altered ownership income. It may also mean lower work-related expenses, more time for family, and a pace that feels more sustainable. Some changes are temporary; others are hard to reverse. The useful starting point is to describe the actual arrangement rather than plan around the broad label “part-time.”

Write down what is changing in concrete terms:

* How many shifts, clinic sessions, procedures, or administrative days will you work each week or month?
* Will your compensation change by a fixed amount, a percentage, productivity, call coverage, or another formula?
* Will you remain an employee, become an independent contractor, keep an ownership interest, or combine roles?
* Which benefits, retirement plan access, insurance arrangements, and paid time off may change?
* How long do you expect the schedule to last, and what would make you increase or decrease hours again?

Get the proposed arrangement in writing where possible. A schedule described informally as “about three days a week” may not make clear how call, charting, meetings, vacation, or minimum coverage requirements work. The details help you estimate both the time gained and the income or benefits that may change.

For physicians deciding whether gradual retirement is a fit, the central issue is not simply whether a smaller paycheck is affordable today. It is whether the new arrangement leaves a workable path from current earnings to the income and flexibility you want later. That path can be different for a resident, a mid-career specialist, a practice partner, and a physician within a few years of leaving full-time work.

## Why might a physician reduce clinical hours?

There is no single reason for changing a schedule, and the motivation matters because it shapes what a successful outcome looks like. A physician who wants more time with children may value predictable days off. A physician reducing overnight call may care most about recovery and health. Someone preparing to leave practice may want to test a lower workload before setting a retirement date. A practice owner may be responding to a staffing or succession transition.

Separate the goal from the proposed solution. “I need to work less” describes a desired change, but not necessarily the best arrangement. Consider whether the priority is fewer total hours, fewer overnight shifts, less administrative work, a different mix of patient care, less travel, or a defined end date. If those distinctions are clear, you can compare options that may preserve some income or benefits while better addressing the reason for the change.

It can help to list the goals in order. For example:

1. Protect one day each week for caregiving.
2. Reduce call responsibilities by a specific date.
3. Keep health coverage and retirement plan access if the employment arrangement permits.
4. Maintain enough income to meet current spending and planned savings.
5. Reassess after six months rather than treating the first schedule as permanent.

This is only an example of how to make priorities specific, not a prescription. Your order may be different. The point is to avoid treating every dollar of compensation, every benefit, and every hour away from work as interchangeable. If your most important goal is a predictable recovery day, a schedule with fewer but longer shifts may not solve the problem even if annual pay is similar.

Also identify constraints that are not fully under your control. A group may require minimum coverage. A hospital may set eligibility thresholds. A partnership agreement may define voting, distributions, or exit provisions. Family care needs and health circumstances can change. The plan should acknowledge what remains uncertain instead of relying on an assumption that every part-time arrangement is available indefinitely.

## Which parts of the financial picture can change?

A reduced schedule can affect several parts of a physician household’s finances at once. Salary or clinical revenue is the most visible item, but it is not the only one. The full comparison includes take-home resources, recurring expenses, employer or practice benefits, savings contributions, debt payments, insurance, and the timing of future income.

Start with the cash that actually reaches the household, not just a headline salary figure. Compensation structures may include base pay, production-based pay, call pay, bonuses, owner distributions, or other components. Some may decline in direct proportion to hours; others may not. Ask the employer, practice administrator, or relevant professional adviser how the proposed schedule affects each component. Do not assume a 20% reduction in hours automatically produces a 20% change in total compensation or benefits.

Next, distinguish flexible spending from commitments. A lower schedule might reduce commuting, meals away from home, childcare, or other work-related costs. But housing, tuition, debt obligations, family support, and planned giving may not fall automatically. A new arrangement could also introduce different expenses, such as more unpaid leave or added coverage needs. Reviewing recent account statements and bills helps replace rough impressions with actual amounts.

Then look at the savings plan. A lower income may reduce the amount you can contribute to workplace or individual accounts. Employer contributions may have plan-specific eligibility rules, contribution formulas, hours requirements, or vesting terms. For a practice owner or partner, the impact can depend on the governing documents and how compensation or distributions are determined. Ask the plan administrator or a qualified professional to confirm the written rules for your specific arrangement.

Finally, consider the relationship between work and later income. Some physicians expect to continue part-time work after leaving a full-time role; others want a clean stop. If you rely on continuing clinical work, ask what could interrupt it: licensing or credentialing requirements, changes in health, demand for your specialty, the availability of shifts, family responsibilities, or a desire to stop sooner than planned. A plan that works only if every preferred shift remains available may need another scenario.

## How can you set a financial baseline before changing schedules?

Before comparing options, take a snapshot of where you stand. A baseline makes it easier to identify which assumptions drive the decision. Keep the first pass simple and use current, verifiable information. A one-page summary is often more useful than a complicated model built from guesses.

### 1. List household income by source

Record current annual compensation and other reliable household income, separating salary, variable pay, practice income, spouse or partner income, and any recurring benefits that materially affect the budget. Use recent pay statements, practice records, and tax documents where appropriate. Mark variable amounts as variable rather than treating a high year as guaranteed to repeat.

### 2. Separate essential, flexible, and one-time spending

Review at least several months of actual spending, then organize it into categories that are meaningful to your household. Essential recurring costs could include housing, utilities, food, insurance, and debt payments. Flexible items might include travel, dining, or discretionary purchases. One-time costs—such as a home project, tuition payment, or family event—should not be mistaken for a permanent monthly expense. This classification is not a judgment about what you should spend; it shows which assumptions can realistically change.

### 3. Inventory savings and obligations

List retirement accounts, taxable savings, cash reserves, debts, and major future commitments. For each workplace account, note the plan type, current contribution, employer contribution if any, vesting status if known, and the plan contact. Do not assume two plans with similar names have the same provisions. For a former employer’s plan or a plan connected to a practice, review its documents before making decisions.

### 4. Gather benefit and employment terms

Ask for written information about the proposed part-time role. Confirm pay calculations, eligibility thresholds, insurance coverage, paid leave, retirement plan participation, call duties, and any practice ownership implications. A question such as “Will I still receive benefits?” is too broad on its own. Ask which benefits, under what eligibility rule, starting when, and whether the answer changes if hours vary or the schedule is temporary.

### 5. Record the retirement goal you are testing

“Retire at 62” is one possible goal, but it does not explain whether you mean leaving full-time practice, ending all clinical work, selling or transferring an ownership interest, or having the option to stop. Write down the milestone that matters. Also include nonfinancial conditions: location, travel, caregiving, health, meaningful work, or the ability to volunteer. Those preferences may change the level and timing of income you want from work.

Use a shared document if more than one person is involved in the decision. A spouse or partner may have different assumptions about spending, family support, or the length of a transition. Making those assumptions visible early can prevent a spreadsheet from appearing more certain than the agreement behind it.

## How should you compare full-time, part-time, and phased-retirement options?

Compare arrangements on the same set of questions. The table below is a decision aid, not a claim about any specific employer or medical group. The actual pay, benefits, hours, and plan rules depend on your contract, employer, practice documents, and applicable plan terms.

| Decision area              | Remain full-time                                              | Reduce hours, same role                                                           | Phased or occasional clinical work                                                             |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Work pattern               | Current schedule and duties continue.                         | Specified days, shifts, or sessions are reduced; duties should be confirmed.      | Work may become intermittent or tied to available assignments.                                 |
| Compensation               | Use current written pay terms, including variable components. | Confirm whether pay changes by hours, productivity, coverage, or another formula. | Estimate only from documented rates and realistic availability; do not assume a steady volume. |
| Benefits and plan access   | Check current eligibility and plan documents.                 | Confirm thresholds and whether a change affects coverage or contributions.        | Confirm whether each assignment provides benefits; access may differ by employer.              |
| Time away from work        | Usually the least immediate schedule flexibility.             | May create regular time away, depending on the agreed schedule.                   | May offer more discretion but less predictability and continuity.                              |
| Retirement timing question | Can current savings and desired work end date align?          | Does reduced income or saving change the date or amount of future flexibility?    | How much future income is dependable, and what is the backup if work stops?                    |
| Best next evidence         | Current pay, spending, accounts, and goal date.               | Written proposal, benefit rules, new budget, and contribution estimate.           | Contract or shift terms, actual availability assumptions, and a no-work scenario.              |

The comparison should include quality-of-life factors alongside dollars. A schedule may be financially workable but not meet the reason you wanted to reduce hours. Conversely, a schedule with lower compensation might still be worth considering if it meets a high-priority personal goal and the household can accommodate the trade-off. Financial planning does not determine what is worth doing for you; it can clarify the consequences and alternatives.

## Worked example: what might a smaller schedule change?

Consider a hypothetical physician with annual gross compensation of $300,000 who currently directs $60,000 each year to savings. The physician is considering a schedule that would produce $210,000 of annual gross compensation. Assume, only for this illustration, that annual household spending stays the same and the amount directed to savings falls to $35,000. These are made-up figures for showing the arithmetic; they are not a forecast, recommendation, or representation of any typical physician’s situation.

On those assumptions, gross compensation falls by $90,000 per year, a 30% reduction from $300,000. Annual savings contributions fall by $25,000, from $60,000 to $35,000. If that contribution difference persisted for five years, the total contributions not made would add up to $125,000 before considering investment gains or losses, taxes, inflation, employer contributions, or any change in the physician’s schedule. This calculation does not say what the account balance would have been or what the physician’s retirement date should be.

The next step is not to conclude that the transition is unaffordable. Instead, test the assumptions. Does spending truly stay the same? Does the employer contribution change? Are any expenses likely to decline? Is there a planned expense that ends during those five years? Could the physician choose a different schedule, save more in some years, or work part-time for a shorter period? Each answer changes the comparison.

For example, if documented spending and benefit details show that the household can continue saving $45,000 rather than $35,000, the five-year contribution difference against the original $60,000 becomes $75,000, not $125,000. That is still a difference of $15,000 per year, but the adjusted number makes clear why a careful budget matters. The example still excludes returns and other factors; it is only arithmetic on stated contributions.

Now compare the amount with the goal. If the physician’s priority is two years of reduced work followed by a return to the current schedule, the relevant question differs from a plan to reduce hours permanently. A permanent change should be tested across a longer span and against the desired date for ending work. A temporary change may need a plan for what would make a return realistic, such as an agreed review date and a clear understanding of whether the role can be expanded again.

A useful scenario table could look like this:

| Illustrative scenario         | Annual gross compensation             | Annual savings contribution    | What to examine                                                                                            |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Current full-time arrangement | $300,000                              | $60,000                        | Current spending, benefits, and plan terms.                                                                |
| Proposed reduced schedule     | $210,000                              | $35,000                        | New take-home resources, reduced or retained benefits, and the reason for the contribution change.         |
| Alternative reduced schedule  | Use the written proposed compensation | $45,000 as a hypothetical test | Whether a different schedule or spending choice supports this contribution without undermining priorities. |

The table is not a prediction and the hypothetical $45,000 contribution is not a recommended target. It illustrates how to keep the assumptions visible. Replace each figure with numbers from your own records and confirm compensation and benefits with the employer or plan administrator.

## How can you test whether the retirement date still works?

Rather than seeking one perfect forecast, compare a few coherent paths. A baseline path might continue the current schedule for a defined period. A second path might reduce hours but preserve a target contribution if possible. A third might assume that part-time work ends earlier than hoped. Each scenario should use consistent assumptions for spending, savings, employment income, and the date you want work to stop.

Start with three questions:

* **What is the earliest date you would like the option to stop?** This is a preference to test, not a promise that the resources will support it.
* **What date would still feel acceptable if the part-time years reduce savings?** A range can be more useful than pretending the decision has a single exact answer.
* **What conditions would make you change course?** Examples include a change in health, family needs, compensation, market conditions, practice ownership, or benefits.

Then test whether each path relies on assumptions that are too fragile. Does it require continuous access to a particular schedule? Does it assume spending falls without a plan? Does it count on a specific investment result? Does it ignore costs that may rise later? If a plan works only under the most favorable assumptions, consider what changes when one of them does not occur.

Be careful with projected investment growth. Future results are uncertain, and an assumed return is not a guarantee. A projection can help organize a discussion when its assumptions are explicit, but it should not be treated as a promise or proof that a work transition will succeed. Review a range of assumptions with an appropriately qualified professional, and understand what is excluded from the calculation.

Part-time work can also shift the timing of account withdrawals or other retirement income, but the details depend on account types, plan rules, age, tax circumstances, and applicable law. Avoid making a distribution or rollover decision based only on the fact that hours have fallen. Review the relevant documents and consult qualified tax and financial professionals before acting.

Some physicians choose reduced hours because they want a longer transition rather than an immediate stop. In that case, the schedule itself is part of the retirement goal. Ask whether the arrangement gives you enough control over timing, duties, and workload to make the transition worthwhile. If “part-time” still carries full-time administrative work or unpredictable call, the actual experience may differ from the intended one.

## What should physicians verify about workplace benefits and plans?

Benefit rules are plan-specific. Never infer coverage from a colleague’s arrangement or from a general description of “part-time status.” Ask the employer’s human resources contact, plan administrator, benefits specialist, or another appropriate professional for the governing documents and a written explanation of how the proposed schedule would be treated.

Questions to put in writing include:

* What is the minimum number of hours or other eligibility measure for each benefit?
* When would a change in status take effect, and is there a transition period?
* How would employer contributions, matching, or other plan contributions be calculated?
* Would the schedule affect vesting, eligibility, or the ability to continue participating?
* Are there separate rules for paid leave, disability coverage, life coverage, or health coverage?
* If your status changes again, can you return to the prior arrangement, and under what terms?
* For owners or partners, which provisions in the governing documents address distributions, duties, voting, or a later exit?

Get clear answers about each benefit rather than a general reassurance. A health plan threshold may not match a retirement plan threshold. A contribution formula may depend on compensation or hours in a way that is not obvious from a summary. Ask when a change would appear in payroll and what happens if the schedule varies from month to month.

If you have multiple accounts from current or former employers, list them before changing work status. A schedule change is not, by itself, a reason to move money. Keep account decisions separate from the employment decision, and review the costs, tax effects, investment choices, and legal protections applicable to each choice with a qualified professional.

## What changes for practice owners and partners?

For an employed physician, the employer may define the reduced schedule and explain how compensation and benefits change. For a practice owner or partner, the arrangement can involve both clinical work and ownership. Those are related but distinct questions. A physician may reduce patient care while continuing management duties, retain an ownership interest after leaving regular clinical work, or negotiate a transition that changes both responsibilities and economics.

Do not rely on verbal assumptions about how fewer hours affect owner compensation, distributions, voting, call obligations, or the value and transfer of an interest. Review the applicable partnership, shareholder, employment, buy-sell, or other governing documents with the appropriate professional. The relevant terms differ among organizations, and a general article cannot establish what a particular agreement permits.

Before signing a change, clarify the role in writing. Identify which duties remain, which ones end, how work is measured, how coverage is arranged, and whether the transition changes ownership or only clinical activity. If an ownership transition is possible later, ask whether the proposed schedule affects that process. Give yourself time to review terms instead of letting a target date force an incomplete decision.

Practice owners may also need to distinguish household cash flow from practice cash flow. A change in distributions or responsibility for expenses should be evaluated using actual financial records and governing terms. Do not assume that the household can replace a decline in clinical income with practice proceeds unless the amount, timing, and conditions are established. For a broader look at owner-related financial questions, see [practice owner planning](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/services/practice-owner-planning); confirm the details relevant to your own organization with qualified advisers.

If a sale, buyout, or ownership transfer may be part of the future, that decision can have its own timetable and legal, tax, and financial consequences. It is sensible to raise it early enough to understand dependencies, but not to assume a particular outcome or valuation. Keep planning discussions distinct from any final agreement until the relevant parties and professionals have reviewed the terms.

## How do family priorities and health fit into the decision?

A work transition is a household decision even when one physician’s schedule changes. Discuss what the additional time is intended to make possible. Family caregiving, a partner’s career, a child’s schedule, health, travel, community commitments, and rest can all affect what “enough work” means. The right answer is not defined by a spreadsheet alone.

It is useful to make both the financial and personal expectations explicit. One person may see part-time work as a brief bridge to full retirement; another may hope to keep some clinical work indefinitely. One may expect household spending to remain stable, while another expects it to decline. Discuss those assumptions before treating them as facts.

Health and capacity deserve particular care. If a schedule change is connected to a health issue, seek appropriate medical guidance and understand relevant workplace or insurance provisions from qualified professionals. Do not make a financial plan depend on a particular health outcome. Likewise, if family care needs could change, include a scenario that recognizes that uncertainty.

Some physicians want to preserve a sense of purpose or connection to colleagues after reducing hours. Consider whether teaching, mentoring, research, administrative work, or occasional clinical shifts are possible and desirable, but verify actual opportunities rather than assuming they will be available. A plan can include meaningful work without treating that work as guaranteed income.

Your own decision may not match a conventional retirement timeline, particularly if you want to continue some work or change duties gradually.

## How should you protect flexibility during a transition?

When important details are uncertain, favor a decision process that allows review. That does not mean every schedule can be reversed or every agreement is temporary. It means asking about options before you give them up. A written trial period, a scheduled review, a clearly defined role, or a documented path to adjust hours may help, if the employer or practice agrees and the terms are appropriate.

Before the change begins, decide what you will monitor. Examples include actual monthly household spending, net income, savings contributions, benefit eligibility, hours worked, call burden, and how well the arrangement meets the original personal goal. Do not select so many measures that review becomes a second job. A short list tied to your priorities is more likely to be used.

Set a date to review the arrangement, such as after the first several months, and agree on what questions you will answer then. If the plan is temporary, define when it ends or how it will be renewed. If the arrangement is permanent, schedule periodic reviews of the retirement timeline and household assumptions. The review date should be a decision point, not a requirement to make an immediate change regardless of circumstances.

Consider a reserve for unexpected expenses or interruptions in income, but set its size based on your own obligations, access to other resources, and professional guidance. Avoid treating a generic number of months as universally correct. A physician with variable compensation, ownership responsibilities, or a household relying on one income may need to examine different contingencies than someone with other stable resources.

Keep important records together: the schedule proposal, employment or partnership documents, benefit summaries, retirement plan documents, recent statements, spending summary, and written assumptions used in any projections. When the arrangement changes, update the records. This helps you distinguish a new fact from an old estimate and reduces the chance of making a later decision from memory.

## How can you organize the transition over time?

A schedule change is easier to evaluate when the work is divided into stages. The timing will depend on your employer, practice, and personal circumstances, but a staged process helps surface missing information before a commitment becomes difficult to change. It also gives you a way to distinguish decisions that must be made now from decisions that can wait until more facts are available.

### Several months before the proposed change

Clarify your personal goal and ask for a written description of the proposed role. Gather current pay statements, benefit summaries, retirement plan documents, account statements, and a recent household spending record. If you are a partner or owner, locate the agreements that govern responsibilities and ownership. This is also a good time to identify which professionals need to review a contract, plan provision, or tax question. Avoid waiting until the final weeks to discover that an important document or approval is missing.

Use this early period to compare more than one schedule if the employer or practice is open to alternatives. For instance, a shorter workweek, fewer call duties, a defined leave period, or a gradual change in patient load may serve different goals. The options are not interchangeable, and they may not all be available. Ask what each version would mean for responsibilities and compensation, then evaluate the documented choices rather than an informal description of what might be possible.

### Before signing or confirming terms

Reconcile the written terms with your financial comparison. Check that the compensation and benefit assumptions in your analysis match the proposed arrangement. If the proposal says that a certain benefit continues only when a minimum schedule is met, reflect that condition explicitly. If compensation depends on productivity or coverage, model a conservative level that is supported by available information rather than assuming the most favorable result. Ask questions about conflicts between an offer letter, plan summary, and existing agreement before relying on any one document.

Consider a short list of “must be true” conditions. These might include a minimum take-home amount, a particular day free from clinical duties, access to a specified benefit, or a review after an agreed period. The list should reflect your own priorities and should not be used as a substitute for negotiation or legal advice. If an important condition cannot be confirmed, decide whether you can accept that uncertainty or need a different option.

### After the new arrangement begins

Compare actual results with the assumptions after the first pay cycles and again at a practical review point. Check compensation, deductions, benefit enrollment, time worked, and savings contributions. If the work schedule is nominally shorter but administrative tasks continue outside the agreed hours, record the actual pattern and consider whether it is meeting your stated goal. A discrepancy does not automatically mean the arrangement has failed; it signals that you may need clarification or a revised plan.

At the review, decide whether to continue, adjust, or reconsider the arrangement. Keep the decision tied to the original priorities: financial feasibility, time, well-being, family needs, and the retirement milestone you are testing. If circumstances have changed, update the assumptions rather than forcing the old plan to fit. A transition is not complete simply because the new schedule starts; it should be reviewed against what you intended it to accomplish.

## A practical checklist before accepting a reduced schedule: final review

Use this checklist as a starting point. It is not a substitute for reviewing contracts, plan documents, or advice specific to your situation.

1. **Define the goal.** Write down what you want less work to change and what you want to preserve.
2. **Specify the schedule.** Document shifts, duties, call, administrative work, compensation terms, and the expected duration.
3. **Build the current baseline.** Gather income, spending, savings, obligations, benefits, and retirement accounts.
4. **Request benefit details.** Verify eligibility and plan provisions directly with the appropriate administrator.
5. **Model at least three paths.** Compare the current arrangement, the proposed schedule, and a case where part-time work ends earlier or income is lower.
6. **Include household priorities.** Discuss spending, caregiving, health, and the meaning of retirement with anyone sharing the decision.
7. **Review owner or partner documents.** Confirm how clinical duties and ownership responsibilities are treated before agreeing to a change.
8. **Choose review points.** Decide what you will measure and when you will revisit the arrangement.
9. **Get qualified advice.** Coordinate questions with professionals who can assess your employment, tax, investment, legal, and benefits circumstances as relevant.

The checklist works best when each item has an owner. A physician might request plan documents, a spouse or partner might assemble household spending, and an attorney might review an agreement. That division of work can make the decision more efficient, but it does not mean that any one person has reviewed the entire plan. Bring the results together before making a final choice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will working part-time delay my retirement?

It may, but not automatically. A lower income could reduce savings or benefits, while lower work-related expenses, existing resources, or a shorter period of reduced hours could offset some of the change. The effect depends on your spending, contribution level, account and plan terms, desired retirement date, and whether future part-time work is reliable. Compare scenarios using your own figures rather than assuming every reduced schedule produces the same delay.

### How many hours should a physician work before retiring?

There is no universal number of hours. Clinical duties, specialty, employer policy, health, family priorities, compensation, and benefit eligibility vary. Define the minimum work arrangement that meets your personal goals, then confirm it is available and understand its financial and professional implications. For some physicians, a phased arrangement is attractive; others may prefer a clear end date or a different role.

### Can I keep contributing to a retirement plan after reducing my hours?

Possibly, but access and contributions depend on the plan’s written terms, your employment status, compensation, eligibility, and other applicable rules. Ask the plan administrator how your proposed schedule would be treated. Confirm both your ability to participate and the effect on any employer contribution. Do not assume a colleague’s answer applies to your plan or employment arrangement.

### Should I wait until my retirement accounts reach a particular balance?

A single balance does not answer whether reducing work is suitable. The same amount can support different choices depending on spending, other income, household needs, account types, health, and the length of the transition. Use a full review of resources, goals, and assumptions rather than relying on an arbitrary threshold. Investment performance and future income are uncertain, so no balance guarantees a particular result.

### Is phased retirement always safer than stopping work at once?

No. Phased work may provide continued income and a gradual adjustment for some people, but it can also involve uncertain shift availability, reduced benefits, lower contributions, ongoing duties, or a transition that lasts longer than intended. A full stop may offer clarity but requires its own planning. Compare both approaches, including a scenario in which continued work is not available, before deciding.

Key person insurance for medical practices is one way a practice may prepare for the financial disruption that follows the death of a person whose work, relationships, or expertise are unusually important to operations. The policy is not a substitute for a succession plan, disability coverage, or a buy-sell agreement. It is a funding tool to evaluate alongside them. The useful question is not simply how much coverage to buy; it is what financial problem the practice would need to solve, and whether insurance is an appropriate way to address it.

For a physician owner, the person in question could be a founder, a partner who anchors referrals, a specialist whose clinical work drives a substantial share of revenue, or a non-owner administrator who holds essential operational knowledge. A sound review identifies the exposure, sets a reasoned amount, clarifies who owns and benefits from a policy, and connects the insurance decision to the practice's other agreements and financial plans.

## What does key person insurance mean for a medical practice?

Key person insurance, sometimes called key employee or key man insurance, generally refers to life insurance a business owns on the life of a person whose death could create a significant business impact. The practice typically applies for and owns the policy, pays the premiums, and is named as beneficiary. If the insured person dies while the policy is in force and the claim meets the contract's terms, the practice may receive a death benefit. The owner, insured, premium payer, and beneficiary should be clearly identified in the documents; the names are not interchangeable.

The money may give a practice time and flexibility. Depending on the situation and policy, a practice might use proceeds to manage a temporary cash shortfall, recruit and onboard a replacement, cover transition costs, or support an orderly change in operations. Proceeds do not guarantee that the practice can replace the person's skills, preserve patient relationships, or continue at the same level. Nor does a policy itself set out who can make decisions, how ownership changes, or how patients will be cared for.

It helps to distinguish this business-owned coverage from personal life insurance. A physician may have individual coverage intended to support family members or meet personal obligations. A practice-owned policy has a business purpose and should be documented and administered accordingly. The two needs can coexist, but one should not be assumed to cover the other. A personal policy's owner and beneficiary arrangements, for example, may not give the practice access to funds when it needs them.

Key person coverage also differs from a buy-sell funding policy. A buy-sell arrangement addresses what happens to an owner's interest under specified events, including who may buy or receive that interest and how the value is determined. Insurance may be used to fund that transaction, but its purpose and beneficiary depend on the agreement and policy structure. Key person coverage instead aims at the operating or financial effect of losing a person's contributions. A practice may need one, both, or neither; the governing documents and facts matter.

These distinctions are especially important in a physician-owned practice. A partner's death could create a business continuity need, an ownership transition, a family or estate issue, or all three. Treating all of those needs as a single undifferentiated insurance amount can leave the practice with a mismatch between the funds available and the event the documents describe.

## Who may be a key person in a physician practice?

A person's title or ownership share alone does not establish that the person is “key.” The practice should look at the effect of that person's absence, rather than rely on seniority, compensation, or a partner's intuition. A person may be important because of a combination of clinical capacity, decision-making authority, revenue relationships, credentials, or knowledge that is difficult to transfer quickly.

Potential roles to examine include:

* **A physician owner or managing partner:** This person may coordinate clinical operations, negotiate major contracts, supervise a location, or make decisions that cannot be made easily by the remaining owners.
* **A specialist or surgeon:** A particular specialty, procedure, referral relationship, or patient panel may depend heavily on one clinician. The practice should distinguish that person's professional production from revenue the group can retain after the person is gone.
* **A clinician with a difficult-to-replace credential or schedule:** A license, certification, specialized training, or established call coverage may take time to replace. A continuity review should consider whether other clinicians can cover the work and under what constraints.
* **A practice administrator or operations leader:** A non-clinician may know how payroll, billing, staffing, systems, facilities, and vendor processes fit together. The disruption may be operational even when patient care can continue.
* **A relationship holder:** A physician or leader may maintain important referral, hospital, payer, vendor, or community relationships. The practice should ask whether the relationships belong to the organization, the individual, or both.
* **A founder with concentrated knowledge:** A founder may retain undocumented knowledge about decisions, systems, or the business's history. This can make the practice vulnerable even if the founder has reduced their day-to-day clinical workload.

For each candidate, ask what would actually stop, slow down, or become more costly if that person were suddenly unavailable. Would appointments need to be rescheduled? Would a location close temporarily? Could claims, payroll, or regulatory responsibilities be handled? Would a replacement be available locally, and how long might credentialing or recruitment take? Which duties could be redistributed, and which require a specific license or decision-maker?

Consider a person who is personally productive but whose patients and work could be absorbed by other clinicians within a few weeks. That person may create a short transition cost but not a long-term revenue loss. Another employee may produce little direct clinical revenue but be the only person who can operate essential systems. The second case could create significant interruption even though a revenue-based formula would miss it. This is why the practice should describe the specific disruption before trying to assign a coverage amount.

Do not assume that naming a person “key” will settle employee, ownership, or consent questions. Insurance applications, applicable law, company documents, and privacy requirements can affect what notices, approvals, or other steps are needed. Ask the insurer and the practice's legal adviser how those requirements apply before an application is submitted.

## What financial risk is the practice trying to cover?

Begin with a short written scenario. State whose death is being considered, what the practice expects to happen in the first weeks and months, which costs or cash needs may follow, and what resources already exist. This keeps the discussion anchored in an identifiable business exposure rather than an arbitrary round number.

Possible needs to evaluate include:

* **Temporary cash-flow pressure:** Collections may decline or arrive later while the practice continues paying rent, payroll, debt service, and other fixed expenses. The amount depends on how quickly expenses can change and what services can continue.
* **Recruiting and transition costs:** Search expenses, temporary coverage, onboarding, credentialing, and training may be material. Estimate the costs that the practice would likely incur, not an assumed industry average.
* **Operational continuity:** A practice may need temporary administrative support, systems assistance, or coverage at a location while responsibilities are reassigned.
* **Debt or contractual commitments:** Review obligations that remain due after the person's death. Do not assume an insurance benefit will be available to satisfy a particular debt unless the policy ownership, beneficiary, lender terms, and legal documents support that use.
* **Transition of ownership:** If the person is an owner, the agreement may specify a purchase or transfer. That need should be analyzed separately from operating expenses, and the insurance structure should match the agreement.
* **Time to make decisions:** A reserve may give the remaining owners time to evaluate a replacement, service changes, or a sale without making a rushed choice. It does not remove the need to make those decisions.

Then identify what should not be counted as a business need. For instance, personal household expenses of the insured's family are not automatically practice obligations. They may be relevant to a separate personal insurance review, but should not be rolled into the practice's calculation without a clear business purpose and an appropriate arrangement.

Map the practice's existing resources as well. Review unrestricted cash, a line of credit, existing business coverage, owner contributions that are realistically available, and any contractual support. Available cash should not be counted twice if it is already earmarked for payroll, taxes, equipment, or another expected expense. A borrowing facility is not the same as cash: availability, covenants, cost, and the practice's capacity to repay it all matter.

## How can a practice estimate a reasonable coverage need?

There is no universal formula that can determine the right benefit for every practice. A practical starting estimate is a transparent planning worksheet. It can organize the conversation, but it is not a policy recommendation or a substitute for underwriting, legal review, or a cash-flow forecast.

1. **Define the scenario and period.** Choose a planning period, such as the first 12 months after the person's death, and explain why it is relevant. A different period may fit a practice with a short replacement timeline or a complex ownership transition.
2. **Estimate temporary income disruption.** Estimate the amount of practice revenue that could be lost or delayed during that period. Use the practice's records and a documented assumption about how much work remaining staff can absorb. Avoid treating the insured's gross billings as revenue the practice will certainly lose.
3. **Add transition expenses.** List likely recruitment, temporary coverage, credentialing, onboarding, and operational costs. Separate one-time costs from recurring expenses.
4. **Add obligations the practice must meet.** Include relevant fixed costs or debt commitments only after reviewing the actual contracts and deciding whether the practice would remain responsible for them.
5. **Subtract resources available for this purpose.** Deduct cash reserves, existing coverage, or other funds that are truly available, after considering other planned uses and any restrictions.
6. **Test the result.** Run a lower-impact and a higher-impact case. Ask whether the amount is affordable to insure and whether the practice could manage the remaining exposure if the actual loss were larger.

**Worked hypothetical example:** Suppose a small specialty practice estimates that the loss of one physician partner could create $180,000 of temporary operating pressure during an initial transition period. It separately estimates $45,000 for recruiting and interim coverage, and $25,000 for other documented transition needs. The initial gross planning need would be $250,000 ($180,000 + $45,000 + $25,000). If the owners determine that $70,000 in unrestricted cash is genuinely available for this contingency, the preliminary uncovered amount would be $180,000 ($250,000 − $70,000).

This hypothetical arithmetic does not mean the practice should purchase $180,000 of coverage. The practice must test the underlying assumptions, whether the cash is truly available, whether coverage for that person is appropriate, and whether the policy's cost, term, exclusions, and benefit structure make sense. The estimate also excludes any buy-sell obligation unless that obligation has been separately assessed. The actual need could be lower, higher, or better addressed through another continuity measure.

Keep the worksheet auditable. Record the data source, date, and person who supplied each estimate. If the revenue impact is based on a forecast, note the assumptions about patient retention, redistributed appointments, capacity, payer collections, and any temporary closure. If the estimate changes materially when one assumption changes, that sensitivity is useful information, not a reason to hide the uncertainty.

Some businesses use a multiple of salary or revenue as a quick screening device. A multiple may be easy to calculate, but it is not a measure of a specific medical practice's cash requirement. It can overstate the need when the work is readily redistributed, understate it when there are specialized transition costs, or blur the distinction between business continuity and an owner's buyout. If a practice uses a shortcut to start a discussion, it should replace it with a fact-based review before making a decision.

## Which policy and ownership questions need answers?

Policy design depends on the practice's legal form, the insured person's role, the intended use of proceeds, the coverage period, underwriting, and the terms offered. The practice should not select a product solely because a label sounds familiar. Ask an insurance professional to explain the available structures, what event triggers payment, which exclusions or limitations apply, how premiums may change, and what happens if coverage is no longer needed.

| Who owns the policy?                    | Identify the applicant, legal owner, premium payer, insured person, and beneficiary in the actual documents.                                            | These roles determine control, payment responsibility, and who may receive proceeds; inconsistencies can frustrate the intended plan. |
| What event is covered?                  | Review the covered event, term, exclusions, contestability provisions, and any other contract limits with the insurer.                                  | The practice needs to understand when a claim may be payable and what conditions could affect it.                                     |

Term coverage and permanent coverage can have different costs, durations, and policy features. Broadly, term coverage is designed for a stated period, while permanent policies may be designed to remain in force longer if their conditions are met. Policy designs vary, and the practice should ask for a written illustration and a plain-language explanation of assumptions, guarantees, non-guaranteed elements, fees, and risks. Do not compare a term premium with a permanent-policy illustration as though they were identical offers.

For every proposal, ask for the policy form and the relevant contract language, not just a summary. Confirm the proposed insured, owner, beneficiary, face amount, premium obligations, term, and any riders. Understand the effect of missed payments, changes in the practice's ownership, and a decision to sell or close. The practice's lawyer should review whether the proposed ownership and beneficiary arrangement is consistent with the entity documents and any agreements.

Consider whether a single policy or a coordinated set of policies is being proposed for multiple people. If there are several partners, the coverage amounts may not need to be the same: roles, financial impact, and agreements can differ. On the other hand, inconsistent coverage without a documented reason may create confusion or perceived unfairness. Explain the rationale in a way the owners can revisit.

## How does key person coverage differ from disability and buy-sell coverage?

These categories address different risks. A key person life policy is generally concerned with a covered death and the business consequences of losing the person. Disability coverage is concerned with qualifying disability or inability to work under the terms of a particular policy. A buy-sell agreement addresses ownership transfer under defined events. One product should not be assumed to fill all three roles.

For example, a practice may rely on a surgeon whose absence due to an extended disability would disrupt the schedule, even though no death has occurred. A life policy may not provide a benefit for that event unless its terms provide a specific applicable feature. The practice should separately review the relevant disability protections, employment arrangements, operational backup, and how work would be covered. Policy definitions and limitations differ, so ask the insurer to explain them rather than inferring coverage from a product name.

Likewise, a life benefit paid to the practice does not automatically buy an owner's interest from an estate or transfer it to surviving partners. The governing agreement must say what is supposed to happen, how value will be determined, who has purchase rights or obligations, and how the transaction is funded. The insurance documents must then be coordinated with that arrangement. The practice should have its attorney and insurance professional review the documents together.

A practice-wide continuity review may also uncover risks that insurance alone cannot resolve: an undocumented administrative process, a single person with access to essential systems, no cross-coverage for clinical duties, or unclear authority to make urgent decisions. Documenting responsibilities, sharing knowledge appropriately, and planning backup can reduce dependence on one person. Insurance may provide funds, but it cannot perform those tasks.

## What tax and legal issues should the owners discuss?

Tax treatment depends on the policy, how it is owned and funded, the parties involved, the use of proceeds, and current law. Premiums and death benefits may be treated differently depending on the facts and applicable rules. Do not assume that business-paid premiums are deductible, that proceeds are always tax-free, or that a particular ownership structure produces a desired result. Ask a qualified tax professional to analyze the proposed arrangement before the practice commits to it.

There can also be tax, reporting, notice, or consent requirements associated with employer-owned life insurance. Which requirements apply depends on circumstances and the governing rules. The practice should ask its tax adviser and attorney to identify required documentation and confirm that it is completed before the policy is issued. This is not an area to resolve by copying another practice's paperwork or relying on a general summary.

Legal review should consider the entity's governing documents, employment or partnership agreements, any buy-sell agreement, beneficiary rights, decision authority, and the intended treatment of proceeds. If the practice has several owners, establish who can authorize an application and how a disputed or changed arrangement would be handled. If an owner dies, the estate and surviving owners may have rights or obligations that are not obvious from the insurance application alone.

Where the insured person is also an owner, make sure the plan does not confuse a payment to the business with a payment to the person's family or estate. Where the insured is an employee who does not own the practice, discuss how the coverage will be explained and administered. Privacy and respectful communication matter; insurance planning should not be treated as a judgment about a person's value or a prediction that something will happen.

Coordinate advice rather than asking one professional to answer every question outside their role. The insurer or insurance professional can explain product terms and underwriting. The attorney can review business documents and legal responsibilities. The tax professional can analyze tax reporting and consequences. The financial planning team can help the owners place the decision among business cash needs and the owners' broader financial priorities. Write down who is responsible for each follow-up and who will maintain the records.

## How should physicians compare proposals and underwriting?

Before comparing proposals, agree on the need, insured person, proposed amount, duration, and intended beneficiary. Otherwise, an attractive-looking premium may be for a different amount, duration, or set of features. Request comparable illustrations or quotes where possible, and ask the professional to show which elements are guaranteed and which depend on assumptions.

Use questions such as these:

* What is the policy's exact owner, insured, beneficiary, face amount, and proposed term?
* Which events are covered, and what exclusions, limitations, or contestability provisions may apply?
* Is the premium fixed for the stated period? If not, how can it change, and when?
* Does the proposal include optional features or riders? What do they cost, and what conditions govern them?
* What happens if the practice changes legal form, loses an owner, sells a location, or no longer needs the coverage?
* What documentation, notices, approvals, or records should be completed before coverage begins?
* How does the proposal address the specific need recorded in the practice's worksheet?

Underwriting may consider information about the proposed insured, and approval, price, terms, and availability are not guaranteed. The practice should allow for the possibility that the requested amount or structure may not be offered. Ask what alternatives exist if the proposal is declined, delayed, or issued with different terms. Do not describe an application as coverage: confirm when the policy is in force, what requirements have been satisfied, and when premium obligations start.

Be careful about replacing existing coverage or cancelling a policy before new coverage is approved and in force. A change may affect cost, insurability, policy provisions, and continuity. Any replacement decision should be reviewed with qualified professionals who can compare the actual contracts and circumstances. A proposal alone does not prove that replacement is beneficial.

For owners with a short decision window, separate urgent continuity actions from the longer-term insurance decision. The practice might document temporary authority, identify backup staff, or arrange interim coverage while underwriting is underway. Those actions do not replace insurance, but they can address operational gaps that a policy cannot solve.

## How can a practice put a decision into action?

A manageable process can keep the owners from getting stuck between a general concern and a policy application. The following sequence creates a record of what the practice is trying to accomplish:

1. **Name the exposure.** Identify the person, the duties or relationships at risk, the event being planned for, and the likely period of disruption.
2. **Gather operating facts.** Review historical financial records, capacity, staffing, contracts, cash reserves, debt commitments, and existing coverage. Keep sensitive records within appropriate channels.
3. **Model a few scenarios.** Estimate a manageable transition, a more disruptive transition, and a case where the practice cannot retain all of the person's work. Record assumptions rather than presenting estimates as predictions.
4. **Review existing protections.** Check policies, agreements, and operating procedures to see whether they address the same exposure, a different one, or only part of it. Verify policy status and terms rather than relying on memory.
5. **Set the purpose and amount.** Use the documented business need and available resources to develop an initial range. Discuss whether the residual exposure can be handled through reserves, borrowing, operating changes, or another approach.
6. **Obtain coordinated review.** Ask insurance, legal, tax, and financial professionals to review the aspects within their expertise. Share a consistent description of the intended use and structure.
7. **Approve and document.** Record owner authorization, consent or notices if applicable, the rationale, selected structure, beneficiary, and responsible person for administration. Keep copies of the policy and related agreements in a secure location.
8. **Plan for the event itself.** Specify who contacts the insurer, who handles practice operations, how the owners communicate, and how patient care and staff communications will be managed, subject to applicable privacy and professional requirements.

The practice may also use a simple comparison table when owners consider options. For example, it can compare maintaining a cash reserve, obtaining a line of credit, buying insurance, or combining approaches. The comparison should use the practice's actual terms and costs rather than generic estimates.

| Approach to evaluate | Questions for the practice                                                                                    | Important limitation                                                                                  |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cash reserve         | How much can be held without impairing planned operating needs, and who can authorize use?                    | Cash may be insufficient for a severe or prolonged disruption and may already be committed elsewhere. |
| Credit facility      | Is borrowing available under the agreement, what are the costs and conditions, and can the practice repay it? | Availability can change and borrowing creates repayment obligations.                                  |
| Key person policy    | Does the insured event, benefit, ownership, and duration match the stated business need?                      | Coverage is subject to policy terms, underwriting, ongoing premiums, and claim conditions.            |
| Operational backup   | Can duties be cross-trained, delegated, or temporarily covered, and what must be documented?                  | It can reduce disruption but does not necessarily provide cash for all resulting costs.               |
| Combined approach    | Which exposures are assigned to reserves, coverage, borrowing, agreements, and people?                        | Coordination requires clear records and periodic review as the practice changes.                      |

## When should coverage and assumptions be reviewed?

Key person planning should not end when a policy is issued. A practice can change quickly: a physician joins or leaves, a partner buys in, a location opens or closes, a specialty mix shifts, or responsibilities move to a new administrator. Each change can alter the person who is essential, the size of the possible disruption, and the appropriate owner or beneficiary.

Set a recurring review date and also revisit the plan after a material change. At minimum, confirm:

* The insured person's current duties, ownership, and importance to operations.
* The policy's in-force status, premium schedule, owner, beneficiary, term, and current face amount.
* Whether the original purpose and estimated financial need still apply.
* Whether reserves, credit, staffing, practice revenue, or obligations have changed.
* Whether the buy-sell agreement and entity documents still match the intended insurance arrangement.
* Whether required records, authorizations, notices, and contact information are current.
* Who is responsible for responding to a claim and who can access the relevant records.

Do not increase, reduce, transfer, or cancel coverage simply because a review reveals a change. First ask the insurer about contract options, then consult the practice's legal and tax advisers as appropriate. A change to ownership or beneficiary can have consequences beyond the insurance premium. Document the recommendation, decision, and reasons, including why the practice chose not to make a change if that is the outcome.

For a new practice or a physician moving toward partnership, the right review may be different from one for an established multi-owner group. Residents and fellows who are considering a future ownership role may want to understand which continuity and ownership questions to ask before signing an agreement. Established owners can focus on current concentration of duties and the reliability of backup systems. A physician approaching retirement may need to coordinate any coverage decision with a planned transition, sale, or reduction in clinical work. In every case, the policy should follow the actual role and business purpose rather than a generic career-stage checklist.

## How can owners test whether someone is truly key?

A useful review tests operational dependence rather than simply naming the most senior physician. Owners can make the discussion concrete by mapping critical functions to the people who perform them and to the people who could step in. The exercise is not a judgment about an individual's worth. It is a way to find concentrated responsibilities and decide whether the practice can operate if one person is unavailable.

Start with clinical and business functions separately. On the clinical side, list services, scheduled sessions, call responsibilities, supervision duties, and credentials that must be present for the practice to provide care. On the business side, list tasks such as payroll approval, billing oversight, hiring, payer or hospital discussions, banking authority, technology administration, and oversight of contracts. For each task, record the primary person, backup person, documentation location, and estimated time needed to transfer responsibility. A blank backup column may reveal a problem that a policy cannot solve.

Next, look at how much the practice's exposure is concentrated. Ask whether patients would follow the practice or the individual, whether other clinicians have capacity, whether referrals are tied to one person's relationships, and whether the work can be scheduled differently. The answers are not always precise, but the practice can record the basis for its assumptions. If appointment history, collections, schedules, or staffing records are used, define the period reviewed and recognize that past activity does not guarantee future collections.

Owners may find it useful to distinguish four kinds of dependence:

* **Revenue dependence:** A portion of practice income may rely on the person's work or referral relationships. Estimate the portion that could be lost after accounting for work other clinicians can reasonably absorb and timing of collections.
* **Capacity dependence:** The practice may need the person's clinical hours, specialty, or supervision to maintain appointment access. Estimate the cost and time to restore capacity, not only the billings associated with the role.
* **Decision dependence:** One person may be the only one authorized or prepared to approve contracts, respond to urgent issues, or guide owners through a transition. Identify interim authority and decision procedures.
* **Knowledge dependence:** Important procedures, passwords, vendor contacts, or billing processes may reside with one person. Appropriate documentation, access controls, and cross-training may reduce this exposure.

These categories overlap. A managing physician may be both a high-volume clinician and the person who holds critical operational knowledge. Still, separating them helps avoid double counting. If the same projected revenue decline is included once as lost production and again as a general cash-flow reserve, the worksheet may overstate the amount. Conversely, omitting recruitment, transition, or nonclinical costs because they do not appear as lost revenue could understate it.

Ask the owners to describe a plausible first week, first month, and first year without the person. Who would notify staff and patients? Who can access scheduling and billing systems? Would appointments be transferred, deferred, or referred elsewhere? Could another physician cover the work while credentialing is pending? Which obligations continue regardless of collections? Do not treat these time periods as standard estimates; use them to organize a practice-specific conversation.

## How do practice structure and career stage affect the review?

A solo or small-group practice may have little internal redundancy. The departure of one clinician can affect the ability to keep a location open, while a larger group may be able to redistribute some work but still have a unique specialty or leadership dependency. Neither size automatically determines the need for coverage. What matters is the practice's ability to absorb the particular person's duties and the resources available during the change.

In a solo practice, the owner may be both the primary clinician and the central decision-maker. Consider whether another licensed clinician could cover patients, who would handle records and staff, and whether the practice would continue, wind down, or be sold. A life insurance benefit payable to the practice may not address all family or estate needs, and it should not be assumed to provide the owner's heirs with the value of the business. The owner should coordinate personal planning and business continuity decisions rather than merge them without analysis.

In a multi-owner practice, ask whether the owners have equivalent duties and whether existing agreements distinguish a temporary operating need from a permanent ownership transfer. A partner who leads a department may have a different continuity impact from a partner whose main role is governance. Review whether decisions can be made if one owner is unavailable and whether the remaining owners have a clear process for temporary authority. If policies are proposed on multiple partners, document why each amount and beneficiary structure is appropriate to the relevant exposure.

For a practice with employed physicians, consider whether the business depends on a particular clinician's specialty, patient panel, or leadership role, and how a transition would be handled under the employment agreement. Do not assume that a policy changes employment rights, compensation, or ownership status. Those matters should be reviewed separately with legal counsel, and communications about insurance should be respectful and consistent with applicable requirements.

Career stage can affect which planning questions deserve attention, but it does not create a formula. A resident or fellow exploring future practice ownership may focus on what happens before signing an employment or partnership agreement. An established physician may need to review accumulated responsibilities and the amount of operational redundancy that now exists. A practice owner considering a sale or retirement may need to determine whether coverage is still aligned with the transition timetable and the contracts involved. Changes should be reviewed before a policy is altered or terminated.

Medical professionals beyond physicians can also have essential roles. A dentist-owner, CRNA, nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or healthcare executive may hold a credential, service-line responsibility, or operational role that is critical to a particular organization. The review should reflect the actual scope of practice, governing requirements, and employment or ownership arrangement. Do not transfer assumptions from a physician group to a different business model without checking the applicable facts.

## What records make the planning discussion more useful?

Owners do not need a perfect forecast to have a productive review, but they do need enough information to explain how they reached a conclusion. Assemble documents securely and share them only with professionals who need them. Avoid placing patient-identifying information in a general planning worksheet. Aggregate operational data where possible and follow privacy and recordkeeping requirements.

A practical preparation file may include:

* A current organization chart and list of owners, employees, roles, licenses, and backup responsibilities.
* Recent financial statements and a summary of fixed operating costs, with an explanation of which costs could change and how quickly.
* A schedule or operational summary showing clinical capacity and any dependence on a particular specialty or location, without unnecessary patient details.
* Current business insurance policies, declarations, policy contacts, renewal dates, and a record of the purpose each policy is meant to address.
* Governing documents, employment or partnership agreements, and any buy-sell or continuity provisions relevant to the person being considered.
* Current cash reserves, credit arrangements, major debt obligations, and any restrictions on access or use.
* A list of functions, system access, and relationships that would need to be transferred, plus a plan to keep critical knowledge documented appropriately.
* A dated worksheet showing the need estimate, assumptions, available resources, proposed amount range, and unresolved questions.

When information is incomplete, mark it as unknown rather than silently filling in a number. For instance, if the practice has not determined whether another physician can absorb a departing specialist's appointments, create an action to examine schedules and credentialing requirements. If an agreement is outdated, ask counsel to review it before treating an old clause as the current plan. A visible information gap is manageable; an unsupported assumption can lead owners to misunderstand what the insurance is intended to accomplish.

Close the review with a concise decision record. It can state which people were considered, what business exposure was identified, what estimates were used, which alternatives were compared, what professionals reviewed the decision, and when the next review should occur. The record should distinguish a decision to proceed from a decision to gather more information or to address the risk through operational changes instead. A decision not to purchase coverage can be deliberate if owners understand the remaining exposure and have a plan for it.

## How might the analysis differ across practice scenarios?

It can help to walk through a few hypothetical situations before estimating an amount. These examples are not predictions, and none establishes a standard benefit. Each shows how the same broad question—what happens if an important person is lost?—can point to different risks depending on the practice's structure.

**A specialty practice with concentrated clinical capacity:** Imagine a group with several physicians but only one clinician providing a particular service. The initial concern may be appointment capacity and continuity for patients, not simply the clinician's annual billings. The owners could identify the services that other clinicians can cover, any limits on their schedules, and the time needed to recruit and credential a replacement. They might estimate temporary coverage and transition costs, then consider how much cash could be available without delaying payroll or other obligations. If the remaining clinicians can absorb much of the work, a calculation based on the person's full production could exaggerate the exposure. If the service cannot continue without a qualified replacement, the interruption could last longer. The practice should document both sides of the estimate and determine whether operational backup can reduce the period.

**A group that depends on an administrator:** Suppose the administrator manages payroll, billing workflow, vendor relationships, and access to key systems, but does not generate clinical revenue. A calculation based only on collections associated with the person would show little or no need. Yet the practice could incur temporary administrative support costs, delayed billing, or disruption while it reconstructs processes. Owners should list the critical functions and access controls, identify a trained backup, and estimate any transition expense that remains after those safeguards. If cross-training and documented procedures already exist, the financial exposure may be smaller. This example shows why a key person review should include essential non-clinical roles, while avoiding an assumption that every valuable employee requires an insurance policy.

**A solo physician-owner approaching a transition:** A solo owner may have several different planning needs at once: temporary funds for staff and business obligations, a plan for patient care, and a decision about whether the practice will be sold, continued, or wound down. The owner should separate those questions. A benefit payable to the practice may support business expenses, but the owner should not infer that it will provide a personal benefit to heirs or satisfy an ownership transfer. The owner can estimate costs for each plausible path, identify who has authority to act, and ask legal and tax professionals how the entity and estate documents fit together. If a transition is already planned, the expected timing and terms should inform the review rather than defaulting to a long coverage period.

**A multi-owner practice with a buy-sell agreement:** A practice may already have an agreement that explains what happens when an owner dies. The owners should read it alongside the insurance policies. Does the agreement require or permit a purchase? How is the price determined? Who is supposed to receive the funds? Does the policy amount and owner match the intended transaction? The practice should not assume that an operating reserve and a buyout amount are the same need, or that a policy intended for one purpose can be redirected to the other without consequences. If the agreement uses a valuation method that is out of date, insurance alone will not correct the mismatch. Legal and financial review can identify what needs updating.

**A physician who is important because of relationships:** A physician may maintain referral or hospital relationships that support a service line. The practice can ask which relationships are institutional, whether other clinicians know the contacts, and how the organization would communicate if the physician were unavailable. Revenue estimates should be cautious: a relationship may continue, shift, or end, and the practice may not know in advance. A continuity plan could include shared relationship management and communication procedures. A policy might provide a reserve for a transition, but it cannot transfer trust or guarantee that referrals continue.

The point of scenario work is not to make every possibility seem insurable. It is to reveal which risks involve cash, which involve authority or expertise, and which depend on agreements or communication. If a scenario has a high estimated financial impact but little ability to replace the person's work, the owners may need to consider more than a policy amount. If the impact is primarily a knowledge gap, better documentation and cross-training may be a more direct first step. More than one response can be appropriate, but each should have a stated purpose.

## How should owners think about uncertainty in the estimate?

A forecast after a key person's death is uncertain by nature. Revenue may change as staff respond, patients choose different providers, appointments are rescheduled, or services are transferred. A precise-looking figure can give a false sense of certainty if its assumptions are weak. Owners can make the estimate more useful by showing a range and describing what would move the result toward its low or high end.

For example, the practice could record one case in which remaining clinicians absorb much of the work quickly, another in which recruitment and credentialing take longer, and a third in which a service line or location must be temporarily reduced. It can list the costs and resources that change in each case rather than simply applying a percentage increase to the first estimate. If three cases are not practical, note at least the assumptions that have the largest effect, such as the period before replacement, percentage of work retained, fixed expenses that continue, and reserves that can be used without affecting other plans.

Separate gross revenue from the practice's potential financial impact. Revenue may include amounts that would not become cash immediately, while costs to provide the service may also change if volume falls. The owners' planning worksheet should use a consistent basis and ask the practice's accountant or financial professional to help interpret its records. It should avoid counting a full year of production as a loss if the practice expects to retain some patients or reduce associated costs. Conversely, it should not assume that collections arrive on schedule just because services were performed.

Check whether there are competing priorities for the same cash. A reserve may already be needed for payroll, taxes, planned equipment, or ordinary fluctuations. A credit line could have limits or terms that make it less useful during a disruption. Existing coverage may apply to a different person, event, or beneficiary. List each resource once and label restrictions. The practice can then compare the estimated need with resources that are realistically accessible, not simply the totals appearing on a balance sheet.

Finally, distinguish uncertainty that can be reduced through better information from uncertainty that remains even after research. Owners can obtain updated financial records, confirm policy terms, assess staffing capacity, and clarify agreements. They cannot know exactly how patients, referral sources, or markets will respond to a future event. The decision record should say where estimates are based on records and where they are judgment calls. That makes it easier to update the analysis as new facts become available, and less likely that a planning number will be mistaken for a promise.

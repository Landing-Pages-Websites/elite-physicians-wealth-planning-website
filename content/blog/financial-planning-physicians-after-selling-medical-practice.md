Financial planning for physicians after selling a medical practice begins with a different question than the one that led to the sale: how should the proceeds, income, responsibilities, and risks be organized now that the practice is no longer the center of your financial life? The first step is not choosing investments. It is understanding what has changed, what obligations remain, and which decisions need coordination. This guide focuses on the period after the sale closes, rather than on preparing a practice for sale.

## What changes when the practice sale is complete?

A sale can change several financial systems at once. The practice may have generated salary, distributions, retirement-plan contributions, benefits, and business expenses. It may also have carried leases, guarantees, deferred compensation, claims-made coverage, or responsibilities for records and patient communications. When ownership ends, some items stop, some continue for a time, and others need a deliberate handoff.

The sale proceeds may look like one large number on a closing statement, but that is not necessarily the amount available for personal goals. The transaction may include cash paid at closing, funds held in escrow, an earnout, seller financing, working-capital adjustments, retained assets, or obligations that settle later. Taxes and transaction expenses may also affect the final amount. Reconcile the closing documents with the professionals who handled the transaction instead of assuming that the total is immediately spendable.

Meanwhile, your routine and professional identity may shift. Some physicians remain for a transition period or work under a new arrangement. Others stop clinical work, move to another role, teach, consult, or take time to decide. Each path creates different income, benefit, insurance, time, and family considerations. A plan that assumes the old practice income will continue may miss the point of the transition.

A coordinated plan connects the transaction’s remaining steps with household cash flow, future work, risk management, and longer-term goals. It may bring a tax professional, attorney, insurance professional, and financial adviser into the same conversation, with each retaining responsibility for their own advice. A review of [estate and business planning for retiring physicians](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6181681/) also describes the advance planning involved in selling or closing a medical practice; the details after closing depend on the specific transaction.

## Reconcile the transaction before making decisions

Start with a complete inventory of what transferred and what did not. Keep the signed purchase agreement, closing statement, amendments, escrow instructions, promissory notes, transition or employment agreements, and correspondence about unresolved adjustments together. Your transaction attorney and tax professional can interpret the documents and identify deadlines. This article cannot determine what a clause means or tell you what an agreement requires.

Break the transaction into trackable categories: cash received at closing, funds held in escrow, amounts due later, liabilities you retained, assets you kept, and contingent items. Record who is responsible for each item, the expected date, any condition that must be met, and the document that supports the estimate. If there is an earnout or seller note, identify the payment terms and who will send statements. Do not count a possible future payment as money already available for spending.

Reconcile the closing records with your personal accounts. Confirm that funds arrived in the expected account, and ask your professionals how transaction expenses should be recorded. Identify practice-related credit lines, guarantees, subscriptions, payroll arrangements, leases, and automatic payments that may remain open. Confirm the handling of final distributions, employee-related responsibilities, and amounts due to or from you. Specific duties depend on the documents and applicable rules, so ask your attorney and accountant rather than relying on assumptions.

Keep an issue log. For each open question, write the next step, the professional or party responsible, the document needed, the target date, and whether the answer affects another decision. This small habit reduces the chance that a follow-up will be mistaken for a completed obligation. It also helps if the buyer, escrow agent, lawyer, or accountant changes personnel during the transition.

Patient records and continuity responsibilities deserve a separate review. The American Academy of Family Physicians provides guidance on starting, selling, or closing a practice, including legal, financial, and patient-record issues. Its [practice transition guidance](https://www.aafp.org/practice-operations/practice-finances-and-ownership/starting-closing-practice) can prompt a discussion with counsel and the successor practice. Do not assume that a sale agreement alone answers every records, notice, or access question.

## Build a balance sheet and a dated cash map

A balance sheet shows what you own and owe at a point in time. A cash map shows when money may arrive and when it may need to leave. After a practice sale, both are useful. List personal bank and investment accounts, retirement accounts, real estate, insurance cash values if relevant, outstanding loans, credit lines, obligations identified by your tax professional, and remaining business-related items. Record ownership and access clearly, especially if an account is jointly held or linked to a former entity.

Do not combine unlike amounts under one line called sale proceeds. Cash already in your account is different from money held in escrow. A seller note is different from a bank deposit. A contingent payment differs from a receivable with confirmed terms. Show each separately with its expected timing, conditions, and confidence level. If a value is unknown, label it unknown. A conservative plan makes uncertainty visible rather than hiding it in a projected total.

For the cash map, list recurring household costs, one-time transition expenses, dates supplied by your tax professional, insurance premiums, debt payments, and expected income from employment or consulting. Note costs that may change because you no longer own the practice. Benefits or services once provided through the business may need to be replaced personally. Do not assume that every former business expense was a personal benefit or that every household expense qualifies as a business expense.

Decide who will maintain the information and when it will be updated. A monthly check during the first year may help while transaction items and new income arrangements settle, but the right schedule depends on your circumstances. Keep statements and source documents alongside the numbers. Mark estimates as estimates. This matters when purchase payments are staged or an escrow release depends on later events.

Use the cash map to separate urgent decisions from those that can wait. A deadline, a coverage gap, or a payment due soon may require prompt attention. A long-term allocation decision may have more time, if no specific condition requires action. The goal is not to delay necessary work. It is to avoid an irreversible choice made only because closing created a sense of urgency.

## Turn the cash map into a decision sequence

A balance sheet records amounts; a decision sequence clarifies what information must be known before a choice is made. Start by marking each item as confirmed, estimated, contingent, or unresolved. For example, the deposit shown in a personal account may be confirmed once it appears on a statement. A tax amount may remain an estimate pending review of the signed allocation and prior records. An escrow release is contingent until its conditions are met. A disputed expense may be unresolved until the parties document responsibility. Using labels prevents the same number from quietly changing status as it moves from one spreadsheet to another.

Then list each anticipated use of money alongside its timing and the source of the estimate. Household expenses might be based on recent statements; insurance costs might come from a current quote; a transaction payment date should come from the agreement or a written update. When two sources disagree, record both and assign someone to resolve the discrepancy. Do not average conflicting figures simply to produce a tidy plan. Ask the professional responsible for the underlying information to explain which source governs.

A practical review can proceed in a repeatable order:

1. **Protect deadlines and continuity.** Identify legal or contract dates, coverage decisions, records responsibilities, payroll or plan matters, and any payment due soon. Confirm them with the professional responsible rather than relying on a general calendar.
2. **Protect known near-term uses.** Compare confirmed cash with expenses and obligations expected before the next reliable inflow. If there is a gap, raise it promptly with your advisers and identify options without assuming a specific solution.
3. **Model uncertain events separately.** Show what changes if escrow is delayed, an earnout is reduced, a new role starts later, or household costs rise. Do not treat the less favorable case as a prediction; use it to see what decisions are sensitive to timing.
4. **Set a review point for longer-term choices.** Once key facts are established, discuss investment, retirement, giving, or major purchases in light of the entire household plan. Write down the assumptions and the date you will revisit them.

Consider two physicians who receive identical headline sale amounts. One has a signed employment agreement beginning immediately, modest transition expenses, and no contingent payments. The other plans a six-month break, has an earnout tied to future operations, and must replace benefits formerly provided by the practice. The same gross proceeds do not imply the same near-term cash needs or risk exposure. The first physician still needs a tax and liability review, while the second may need additional scenarios around income timing and the buyer’s performance. This comparison illustrates why a sale amount by itself cannot determine a spending or investing decision.

Keep a short decision record with the question, verified facts, unresolved assumptions, alternatives considered, adviser consulted, and next review trigger. If the answer depends on a future event, state that condition plainly. For example, a plan could say that a large purchase is reconsidered after an escrow release and an updated cash-flow review, not that the purchase is already affordable. Written conditions give you and your advisers a common reference and make it easier to adapt when circumstances change.

## Coordinate tax questions with the right professional

A practice sale can create tax questions whose answers depend on transaction structure, assets transferred, timing, prior elections, state and local rules, and terms of any later payment. The closing amount is not automatically the amount available after taxes. A general online estimate cannot replace a review of signed documents and your tax history. Ask a qualified tax professional to examine the transaction and explain what applies to you.

Ask which records are needed, what assumptions are being used, which dates matter, and what remains uncertain. Confirm whether sale-related reporting may span more than one year or more than one payment date. If you receive installment payments, an earnout, or escrow releases, ask how each event should be tracked and whether it changes future reporting or cash planning. Do not make an election, transfer assets, or change ownership based only on an article.

Establish a communication path among your transaction attorney, tax professional, and financial adviser. The tax professional addresses tax interpretation and filings. The attorney interprets legal obligations and transaction documents. The financial adviser may use those answers to discuss cash flow and investment decisions within the agreed scope. A written note identifying who is answering each question can prevent duplicated work and conflicting assumptions.

Where an exact answer is not available, ask for scenarios or a range, with the inputs and date clearly labeled. Revisit the estimate when the closing statement is final, a payment arrives, escrow is released, or a material assumption changes. A placeholder can help planning if it is plainly marked; it becomes risky when someone treats it as a final bill or a guaranteed amount.

Before committing all proceeds elsewhere, discuss the timing of known obligations and access to liquid resources with your professionals. This does not imply that every physician should keep the same amount in cash or use the same account. Liquidity decisions depend on timing, access, uncertainty, protection, and potential penalties or risks. Every choice has trade-offs; no cash or investment strategy removes tax, market, inflation, or counterparty risk.

## Separate short-term liquidity from long-term investment decisions

A large payment can create pressure to invest quickly. A measured process separates money according to expected use and timing. Funds that may be needed for near-term obligations raise different questions from funds intended for a long-term goal. Relevant considerations include the amount, time horizon, access needs, risk tolerance, applicable protections, costs, and professional advice.

While the broader plan is being completed, set a temporary process for handling the proceeds. It may identify who can authorize transfers, which statements will be reviewed, what transaction requires an additional check, and how uninvested funds will be monitored. This is a governance process, not an investment recommendation. It can reduce the risk of an impulsive transfer, a mistaken wire, or a decision made without understanding the terms.

Consider concentration across the whole household. A physician may still depend economically on the buyer through a seller note, earnout, retained real estate, or new employment arrangement. The former practice may no longer appear as an operating business on a personal balance sheet, yet exposure to the buyer or the same local market may remain. Review connected exposures together rather than in separate silos.

Investments carry risks after the sale. Market values can fall, inflation can reduce purchasing power, an asset may be difficult to sell when needed, rates can change, and an issuer or institution can fail. Diversification may reduce some kinds of concentration, but it does not assure profit or protect against loss. Understand costs, tax consequences, liquidity limits, restrictions, and the role of each asset before committing funds.

Be cautious with proposals built around urgency, guaranteed outcomes, vague descriptions, or pressure to move money before reviewing documents. Ask for written explanations of costs, risks, liquidity, conflicts, and how the proposal fits your complete plan. If it is complex, request plain language and time to review it with your own qualified professionals. A large proceeds balance is not a reason to accept a strategy or product you do not understand.

## Replace practice income with a household cash-flow plan

Practice income may have included compensation, distributions, benefits, and other arrangements. After a sale, cash flow may come from a new employer, a transition role, consulting, teaching, retirement accounts, portfolio assets, or several sources. Separate contracted income from income that is expected, discretionary, or contingent. Note the start date, expected duration, and confidence level for each source.

List fixed and flexible household costs, including housing, education, family support, travel, giving, insurance, debt payments, and tax amounts estimated by your professional. Compare costs with dependable income and the timing of likely payments. Use monthly or quarterly periods if the transition is uneven. A yearly total may hide a temporary gap, especially when sale payments are delayed or a new role begins later.

For illustration only, suppose a physician receives $1,200,000 at closing, while $150,000 remains in escrow and the tax professional estimates that $240,000 should remain available for tax obligations. These hypothetical figures are not a forecast or recommendation. The physician should not treat the full $1,200,000 as spendable: the escrow is not in hand, the tax estimate needs confirmation, and transaction expenses or other obligations may affect the amount. If another $120,000 is earmarked for a year of planned household spending, the remaining sum in this simplified arithmetic is $840,000 ($1,200,000 minus $240,000 minus $120,000). This calculation does not decide where the $840,000 belongs. It illustrates why separate buckets and verified assumptions matter.

Build more than one cash-flow scenario. One could assume a prompt start in a new role; another could assume a longer break, lower variable income, delayed escrow, or changed family expenses. These are planning tests, not predictions. Ask what would change if a payment arrived late or an expected income source did not materialize. If the plan works only under the most favorable assumptions, discuss alternatives.

If you continue working, determine whether the transition role is temporary or part of a longer career plan. Clarify compensation, benefits, schedule, call responsibilities, malpractice coverage, and restrictions in writing with the relevant professionals. If you stop clinical work, estimate how household income may be supported and when you will reassess the assumptions. Neither choice is inherently better. The aim is to make the trade-offs visible.

## Review retirement accounts and employee responsibilities

Plan questions can remain after the ownership transfer. Confirm the status of any retirement plan sponsored by the former practice, including who is responsible for administration, participant communications, contributions, distributions, records, and filings. The answer depends on plan documents and transaction terms. A third-party administrator and qualified legal and tax professionals can clarify what applies; do not assume that a plan automatically transfers, terminates, or continues unchanged.

List your personal retirement accounts and identify whether each is an individual account, employer-sponsored, or connected to the former practice. Check account access, beneficiaries, forms, fees, investment options, and restrictions on transfer or distribution. Avoid rolling over, withdrawing, or combining accounts until a qualified professional reviews the applicable plan and tax consequences. Each account has rules that may not be obvious from its balance.

If employees or former partners are affected, treat those matters as a separate workstream. Confirm the buyer’s and seller’s respective responsibilities for final payroll, benefits, plan administration, notices, and records as applicable to the transaction. Assign an owner and retain proof of completion. Employee matters should not be mixed with your personal cash-flow decisions or left to informal assumptions.

Consider how a new work arrangement changes the review. A physician who becomes an employee may have different benefit options from a consultant or independent professional. Availability, eligibility, limits, and deadlines depend on plan terms and current rules. Ask the plan administrator and tax professional for current information before deciding.

Keep personal planning and business cleanup distinct. A former owner can feel responsible for every task associated with the practice, but the agreement and relevant rules determine who must act. If a matter belongs to the buyer, former practice, plan administrator, or another party, document the handoff and follow up through the appropriate channel.

## Reassess insurance and liability

Insurance needs do not necessarily end on the closing date. Review professional liability, tail or extended reporting coverage if applicable, general business, property, cyber, disability, life, health, and personal umbrella coverage with qualified insurance and legal professionals. Which coverage applies, who pays, and how long it lasts depend on the policy, work arrangement, agreement, and jurisdiction.

Pay particular attention to professional liability. Determine whether the policy was claims-made or occurrence-based, whether a reporting endorsement is needed, who must arrange it, and how coverage applies to services performed before and after the sale. Do not rely only on a verbal statement that the buyer has coverage. Ask the insurance professional to explain the contract and provide written confirmation of responsibility and dates.

If you continue practicing under the buyer or another organization, confirm who provides coverage, what limits and exclusions apply, whether outside work is covered, and what happens when the employment or transition agreement ends. If you stop practicing, ask whether obligations remain for prior services and what coverage or recordkeeping requirements apply. These questions should be answered before assuming that the sale resolved them.

Personal coverage may also change. A new employer’s health, disability, or life benefits may differ from the practice’s former arrangements. A break between roles can create a gap. A change in income or family responsibilities may lead you to review existing policies, but replacing coverage can involve underwriting, cost, exclusions, or waiting periods. Review a contract before cancelling or replacing it, and compare terms rather than only premiums.

Insurance has limits. A policy may exclude events, impose conditions, or provide less coverage than expected. It is not a replacement for careful contracts or professional advice. Store the carrier, policy number, named insured, effective dates, responsible party, renewal date, and contact details securely where an authorized family member can find them.

## Update estate documents, beneficiaries, and ownership

A sale may change the assets you own, their titles, and the people involved in your financial life. Ask a qualified estate-planning attorney to review your will, trusts, powers of attorney, health-care directives, and beneficiary designations. A document that names a former business, assumes you still own practice property, or refers to an old account may no longer match your situation. Whether it needs revision is a legal question for counsel.

Review beneficiary forms separately from a will. Retirement accounts and insurance policies may have their own designations, and the form on file can control a transfer under the contract. Confirm primary and contingent beneficiaries, spelling, percentages, and contact details. Do not assume a will updates an account form, or that a change should be made without considering the broader family and legal circumstances.

Inventory assets whose ownership may need review: accounts receiving sale proceeds, real estate, retained practice property, business interests, seller notes, escrow rights, and jointly held assets. Ask an attorney whether title or trust ownership is appropriate for a specific asset. A change can have legal, tax, creditor, or operational consequences. Do not retitle property merely to make a spreadsheet look orderly.

Consider who can act if you are unavailable. Ensure authorized people know where essential documents are stored and how to contact the professionals involved. Review online and business-system access securely. Former practice systems may contain sensitive information, so do not retain or transfer access unless the sale agreement and applicable requirements permit it.

Family communication is also part of preparedness. You need not share every private detail to tell the right person whom to call and where instructions are kept. A concise contact list can identify the attorney, tax professional, insurance agent, financial adviser, and the owner of each open transaction item. Protect confidential documents and share them only with people who have an appropriate role.

## Plan for the professional and personal transition

The financial shift may overlap with a personal transition. The practice may have structured your schedule, relationships, identity, and sense of responsibility for years. A new role, reduced hours, relocation, or retirement may alter routines at the same time that income and asset decisions arise. These personal factors deserve attention because they influence what you choose next, not because there is one correct response.

Write down what you hope the next chapter includes before deciding that the proceeds must fund a particular lifestyle immediately. Consider clinical work, teaching, mentoring, volunteering, family commitments, travel, or a period of rest. These are options, not a checklist. Discuss the financial implications with family members who share them. Be clear about what is known, undecided, and scheduled for later review.

If you work for the buyer, agree on boundaries and expectations. Clarify schedule, patient-care duties, referral responsibilities, supervision, compensation, benefits, use of your name, and an end date or review point. Ask counsel to review the contract. A transition period can be useful, but it can also create uncertainty if the parties have different ideas about what helping with a handoff means.

If you stop working or reduce hours, test your plan against the intended schedule. Estimate recurring spending and likely income, and distinguish estimates from commitments. Consider effects on insurance, benefits, taxes, retirement timing, and professional obligations. You may stage decisions rather than settle everything at once, provided no deadline or coverage gap requires prompt action.

Revisit the plan after you have experienced the new routine. What seemed essential before closing may matter less later; a new responsibility or interest may emerge. Set review dates, perhaps after a quarter, six months, and a year, and also revisit when important facts change. A regular cadence helps update assumptions without reacting to every short-term market move or news story.

## Coordinate advisers without blurring their roles

Post-sale planning touches several areas. A tax professional addresses tax interpretation and filings. A transaction or estate attorney addresses legal documents, obligations, and rights. An insurance professional reviews coverage. A financial planner or investment adviser may connect cash flow, investment decisions, retirement goals, and the household picture. Other specialists may be appropriate. Coordination does not make one professional a substitute for another.

Start with a one-page summary of facts the advisers need: closing date, documents completed, payment schedule, outstanding amounts, current income arrangements, known deadlines, and open questions. Separate verified facts from estimates. Share records through secure channels and confirm who is authorized to receive them. A common fact set can reduce repeated explanations and help each professional focus on their area.

At meetings, keep an action list. For each question, record the owner, requested document, expected response date, and whether the answer affects another decision. An unresolved tax estimate may affect a cash-flow assumption; a coverage question may affect a work transition. A written list makes dependencies visible without asking a professional to work outside their role.

Ask each professional to explain scope, compensation, potential conflicts, and limitations in writing. Ask how they will coordinate with the others and who will maintain the current plan. A professional should be able to describe what they do, what they do not do, and when a referral is needed. A general phrase such as full service does not tell you the exact scope of an engagement.

Physicians in White Plains, Charles County, Prince George’s County, and the surrounding Mid-Atlantic region may prefer in-person or virtual conversations depending on the professional and subject. Location alone does not prove that a person is qualified for a specific legal or tax question. Verify experience, qualifications, and engagement scope directly before relying on advice.

## Review risks that can be easy to miss

* **Counting contingent proceeds too early.** Escrow, earnouts, and seller notes may be delayed, reduced, disputed, or conditional. Plan around confirmed cash and model uncertain amounts separately.
* **Missing timing gaps.** A tax payment, premium, household expense, or delayed paycheck may come due before a later sale payment arrives. Use a dated cash map and verify deadlines with the professional responsible.
* **Concentrating exposure in the buyer.** A seller note, earnout, continued employment, and retained property may all depend on one buyer. Consider combined exposure rather than each item in isolation.
* **Acting under pressure.** A rushed investment, distribution, asset transfer, or policy cancellation can create costs or limits. Ask which deadline is real and what should be reviewed first.
* **Assuming coverage ended or continues automatically.** Policies differ. Get written confirmation of who is insured, dates of coverage, and who must arrange any continuing protection.
* **Leaving account access or beneficiaries stale.** Old contacts, forms, or unclear authority can complicate access. Review accounts and estate documents securely with qualified professionals.
* **Ignoring wire fraud.** Independently verify payment instructions using a previously confirmed contact, use secure channels, and require an additional check for material transfers.
* **Relying on one optimistic forecast.** Future income, payments, and investment values are uncertain. Test more than one scenario and understand assumptions.

Risk management does not eliminate risk. Cash may lose purchasing power to inflation or have opportunity costs; investments may fall or become illiquid; paying debt can reduce flexibility; and a seller note can keep you exposed to the buyer. Each choice has trade-offs. Ask qualified professionals to explain the risks in your circumstances and the consequences of both acting and waiting.

## Use a phased first-year checklist

### At closing and in the first few weeks

* Store the signed agreement, amendments, and closing statement securely, with access for authorized professionals.
* Verify cash received, amounts held back, payment instructions, and remaining obligations against the closing records.
* Ask your tax professional what information is needed and which time-sensitive items require attention.
* Confirm the status of professional liability and other policies with the insurer and attorney; obtain written confirmation of responsibilities and dates.
* List recurring household costs, new benefit needs, debt, and expected income during the transition.
* Change former practice system access only as authorized; protect patient information and follow the records handoff.

### In the next one to three months

* Reconcile your balance sheet and cash map with actual statements and professional estimates.
* Review escrow, earnout, seller-note, employment, or consulting terms and note what triggers each next step.
* Discuss tax assumptions and update them when material facts change.
* Review personal insurance, health coverage, retirement accounts, beneficiaries, and estate documents with responsible professionals.
* Build an income and spending scenario around confirmed resources; separately test delayed or lower income.
* Assign an owner and follow-up date to every unresolved question.

### During the rest of the first year

* Revisit the plan as payments settle, filings are prepared, or work arrangements change.
* Check whether assigned records, employee, plan-administration, and contract responsibilities are complete.
* Review investment and liquidity decisions against goals, time horizon, risk capacity, and remaining exposure to the buyer.
* Update estate and insurance arrangements after material changes in assets, family, or work.
* Agree on a regular review cadence and refresh assumptions and open items.

This is a prompt, not a universal timeline. Your contract, policy terms, filing duties, and personal situation may call for a different order or faster action. Ask each qualified professional to identify deadlines that apply to you.

## Evaluate assets and liabilities you retained

A practice sale may leave you with more than cash and personal accounts. You may retain real estate, equipment, intellectual property, a minority interest, a seller note, a lease obligation, or rights under a continuing contract. Add each item to the balance sheet, even if it is hard to value. Describe what it is, who owns it, what agreement governs it, what income or expense it may create, how it could be transferred, and which adviser should review it. An unknown value is better than an unsupported estimate.

Retained real estate deserves a separate review. Confirm whether it is leased to the buyer, occupied by another tenant, or intended for personal use. Review the lease with your attorney. Consider maintenance, vacancies, insurance, property taxes, financing, and management costs. Rental income may be uneven, and the property may be difficult to sell quickly. Any valuation or cash-flow projection should disclose assumptions rather than imply a certain return.

A seller note or other payment right is both an asset and a risk. Review the written payment schedule, interest terms, collateral, default provisions, transfer restrictions, and notices with your transaction attorney and tax professional. Track payments against the schedule and retain evidence. The buyer’s ability to pay may change. Do not base household spending on every expected payment as though it were already cash.

List liabilities with equal care: guarantees, loans, leases, tax or payroll items under review, legal obligations, or final business expenses. Identify who is legally responsible, the amount or range if known, the due date, and the source document. Do not infer that a liability vanished because ownership changed, or that you are responsible merely because your name appears in old correspondence. Ask counsel to interpret the agreement and law.

Review the way retained items interact. A lease, seller note, and continued employment may all depend on the same buyer’s financial health. Their risks are connected even though they appear on separate balance-sheet lines. Ask advisers to consider how a delayed payment, dispute, or change in the buyer’s operations could affect your personal cash plan. A combined view is more informative than relying on each item’s stated value.

## Set practical decision rules for the transition year

Uncertainty can make every choice feel urgent. Written decision rules can sort questions into those needing immediate professional input, those that can wait for information, and those requiring an additional review before action. Make the rules fit your family and circumstances. They are not a universal formula or a replacement for legal authority over an account.

For instance, you might agree with a spouse or trusted person that a large transfer will not be made until instructions have been independently confirmed using a known contact, or that an unfamiliar proposal must be provided in writing for review. These are process safeguards, not a recommendation about where assets should be held. Confirm that any dual-approval process is supported by the institution and consistent with legal documents.

Set review triggers as well as calendar dates. A payment arriving, escrow release, new employment agreement, material insurance change, updated tax estimate, or family change may warrant a fresh look. Ask what changed, which assumptions remain valid, what choices are now possible, and which professional should be consulted. Trigger-based reviews help the plan respond to facts without requiring a reaction to every headline or market fluctuation.

Make a pause a deliberate decision rather than an accident. Record why an action is deferred, what information is needed, and when you will check again. Some choices cannot wait because of a legal deadline, coverage gap, or contract requirement; confirm those with the appropriate professional. Where timing allows, a defined pause may prevent action before the trade-offs are understood.

Include a way to handle disagreement. A physician and spouse may differ about continuing work, spending, risk, or family support. Make time to understand the concern behind each view and identify facts that can be checked. If needed, ask an appropriate neutral professional to facilitate a conversation within their scope. The aim is not identical preferences but clear consequences and an informed decision.

## Protect records and make the handoff manageable

Keep a secure record set for the period before and after the sale. It can include the final agreement, amendments, closing statement, payment records, tax documents, policy confirmations, plan correspondence, and open-item list. Separate personal records from former practice records and limit access to authorized people. Clear file names can save time when a professional requests a document months later.

Do not copy, retain, or use patient information simply because you once owned the practice. Patient records are sensitive and subject to legal, contractual, and professional requirements. Follow the agreement, applicable rules, and counsel’s direction on custody, access, retention, transfer, and patient requests. If a request reaches you after the handoff, use the agreed channel instead of creating a duplicate or sending information through an unapproved method.

Review digital and physical access. Confirm which business email accounts, payment systems, cloud drives, phone numbers, passwords, keys, and devices should be returned, transferred, disabled, or retained under the agreement. Use secure procedures and document completion. Separate personal accounts where appropriate. Ask counsel or an administrator before deleting material that may need to be preserved.

Prepare a household reference sheet with the location of essential documents, professional contacts, and recurring financial tasks. Do not put passwords, account numbers, or patient information in an unsecured file. Use secure storage and follow institutional instructions. Tell the person who may need to act where the sheet is stored and how to reach the professionals, without sharing unnecessary private details.

Archive important decisions as well as documents. Keep brief notes about why an action was taken, who advised on it, which assumptions were used, and what event would prompt review. These notes do not replace professional records but may help you and your advisers understand the sequence later. If facts change, update the record rather than treating an old plan as permanent.

## Questions to take to your advisers

Written questions can make a short meeting more useful. Select the ones that apply and send them in advance so the professional can identify needed documents.

* **For your tax professional:** Which transaction documents and prior records do you need? What assumptions underpin the current estimate? Which dates or payments could change it? When should we revisit the estimate?
* **For your attorney:** Which obligations survive closing? Who is responsible for records, notices, claims, employees, and ongoing transition duties? Should estate documents or ownership be reviewed based on what I retained?
* **For your insurance professional:** Which policies continue? Is an extended reporting endorsement or other step required? What is excluded, who pays, and when does protection begin or end?
* **For your financial adviser:** What cash is committed to known needs and what is uncertain? How do assumptions change if income or a payment is delayed? What are the costs, risks, liquidity limits, and conflicts for each proposed action?
* **For the buyer or transition contact through the agreed channel:** Which tasks remain, who owns them, what confirms completion, and where are records or patient questions routed?

After each discussion, send a short written recap of decisions and dates. Correct misunderstandings promptly. Keep the current balance sheet, cash map, contact list, and open-item log in a secure location. If a recommendation depends on an unconfirmed fact, identify that dependency before acting.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I invest all the sale proceeds immediately?

There is no universal answer. Identify confirmed obligations, timing needs, delayed or contingent payments, and personal goals first. Investment decisions depend on time horizon, risk capacity, liquidity needs, costs, and the risks of the assets. A rushed decision can be as unsuitable as an indefinite delay; discuss a deliberate process with qualified professionals.

### How long should I keep money available after the sale?

The amount and period depend on household spending, estimates from your tax professional, insurance, debt, income timing, transaction terms, and other resources. Map expected dates and test a delayed-payment scenario. Do not use a rule of thumb instead of reviewing your actual needs and obligations.

### Does selling end my professional liability responsibilities?

Not necessarily. Policy terms, coverage type, services performed, the sale agreement, and applicable rules matter. Ask your attorney and insurance professional about prior work, any reporting endorsement, responsibility, and dates. Obtain written confirmation rather than relying on an informal assurance.

### Should I update my will after selling?

A sale can change assets, ownership, and beneficiary designations. Ask an estate-planning attorney to review whether your documents reflect your current wishes and circumstances. A will may not control every account or insurance benefit, so review beneficiary forms and ownership separately with qualified professionals.

### What if I continue working for the buyer?

Review compensation, schedule, benefits, coverage, use of your name, and end-of-transition terms in writing. Have counsel review the agreement and confirm insurance and benefit details. Update your household plan to distinguish dependable income from temporary or uncertain income.

Revisit assumptions as payments, work, and family needs become clearer, and keep risks visible alongside potential benefits.

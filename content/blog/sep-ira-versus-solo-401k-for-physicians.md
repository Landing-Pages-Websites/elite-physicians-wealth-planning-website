**By Michael Epps, ChFC®, RICP®**\
Published November 11, 2026 | Last reviewed November 11, 2026

Choosing between a SEP IRA and a solo 401(k) can be a consequential decision for a physician with self-employment income. This guide to the **SEP IRA versus solo 401k for physicians** explains how the arrangements differ, which details can change the comparison, and what to check before opening or funding a plan. The short answer: there is no universal winner. The right fit depends on the business and its people, the physician’s other retirement plans, contribution goals, and the amount of administration the owner can reliably manage.

## What is the central difference between a SEP IRA and a solo 401(k)?

A SEP IRA is an employer-funded retirement arrangement. When a self-employed physician sponsors one, the physician generally acts in the employer role for purposes of the contribution. The arrangement does not use the same employee salary-deferral mechanism as a 401(k). A solo 401(k), also called an individual 401(k), can allow an eligible owner to contribute in two capacities: as an employee and as the employer. The plan document and applicable rules determine which features are available and how contributions are calculated.

That structural difference is often the first point of comparison, but it is not the whole decision. A solo 401(k)’s employee-deferral feature may be useful to someone with eligible self-employment income who has not used the available deferral amount elsewhere. If a physician already contributes through a hospital or university plan, the deferral question must be coordinated across plans. A SEP IRA may be attractive when an employer-funded contribution fits the goal and the owner values fewer plan features, but the employer’s contribution obligations and calculation still need review.

Both arrangements have limits, eligibility conditions, procedural requirements, and details that depend on the business. Both can be poorly matched if chosen from a one-line comparison or an online calculator that lacks the relevant facts. A plan that looks simple can still create work if there are eligible employees. A plan with more features can be unnecessary if the owner cannot use them or does not want the related administration.

For a broader view of how a retirement-plan decision can fit into a physician’s long-term planning, see [retirement planning for physicians](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/services/retirement-planning). The purpose here is narrower: help you frame the SEP-versus-solo-401(k) comparison and identify the facts to take to a qualified professional.

## Who is meant by “solo” in solo 401(k)?

The word “solo” can sound like a casual label for any physician who earns 1099 income. It is not a substitute for checking plan eligibility. An owner-only 401(k) is generally designed for a business owner with no eligible employees other than a spouse who works in the business. Whether a worker is eligible depends on the plan terms and applicable rules, not only on whether the physician calls the person part-time, temporary, a contractor, or support staff.

That distinction matters for physicians because a side consulting activity may have no employees, while a medical practice may have nurses, administrative employees, technicians, or staff shared with another entity. A physician may also own interests in more than one business. Related businesses and ownership relationships can affect the analysis. Do not decide that a worker is irrelevant just because payroll, supervision, or job duties are handled through another organization. Gather the ownership and worker information and have a benefits professional review it.

A spouse who genuinely works in the business may be eligible to participate under the plan’s terms. The work and compensation should reflect real business activity and comply with applicable rules. Do not create a nominal job or compensation arrangement only to obtain a retirement-plan feature. Confirm how the spouse’s participation would operate, what records are needed, and how the household’s other plan participation affects the overall result.

A SEP IRA is not a way to make employee obligations disappear. If a business has eligible employees, the employer may have to make contributions for them as well as for the owner under the plan’s formula and eligibility provisions. This can have a significant impact on cost and administration. Before adopting either arrangement, list every worker, identify the entity that employs or pays each person, and obtain a written eligibility review where the situation is not straightforward.

## How do physician work arrangements change the comparison?

Physicians often have more than one source of compensation. One person may receive W-2 wages from a hospital, 1099 compensation for consulting or locums work, and income connected to a practice or partnership. The account title does not tell you whether a particular source of income supports a particular plan contribution. The business structure, tax treatment, plan sponsor, plan document, and the physician’s role all matter.

Start by separating the activities rather than combining them into a single “income” number. Identify the payer, the entity receiving the compensation, the business expenses, the tax reporting, and the person who controls the business. Keep W-2 wages distinct from self-employment earnings. A contribution calculation for a self-employed owner is not simply a percentage of gross receipts or an amount copied from a W-2 employee’s pay statement. A qualified tax professional should determine the relevant compensation and calculation method for the specific entity and plan.

For example, a physician may receive a W-2 from a hospital and have a separate consulting business with no employees. The consulting activity might make a business retirement plan worth evaluating, but that possibility does not establish eligibility by itself. The physician still needs to review whether the activity is a qualifying business for plan purposes, whether other entities must be considered, and whether employee deferrals made at the hospital affect what may be deferred through the business plan.

Another physician may own a practice with staff. The practice’s employee roster, ownership structure, existing retirement arrangement, and business entity become central. A solo 401(k) designed for an owner-only business may not fit if there are eligible employees. A SEP IRA might require employer contributions for eligible staff. In that setting, comparing only the owner’s possible contribution misses an important part of the decision: what the arrangement means for the whole employer group.

Residents and fellows may have a W-2 training position alongside moonlighting, consulting, or other self-employment activity. Their available time, variable earnings, and existing workplace plan participation can all influence whether the extra plan is practical. Established physicians may be weighing side income against a full portfolio of workplace benefits. Practice owners and partners may need a broader plan-design review rather than an owner-only comparison. The same two account names can lead to different questions at each career stage.

## How are contributions structured?

### SEP IRA: employer-funded contributions

A SEP IRA generally uses employer contributions. For a self-employed individual, the calculation is not necessarily the same as applying a stated percentage to gross 1099 payments. Business expenses, the way self-employment earnings are determined, the applicable contribution formula, plan terms, and current limits can all matter. Where employees are eligible, the employer must also consider the plan’s participant coverage and contribution rules.

One practical implication is that a SEP IRA does not give an owner the same employee salary-deferral contribution mechanism found in a 401(k). The amount an owner may contribute is evaluated through the employer-contribution framework. This distinction can be relevant when net self-employment earnings are relatively modest, when a physician has a workplace plan, or when the desired contribution amount is not easily met through the employer-only structure. It does not automatically make the SEP inferior; it means the two arrangements are built differently.

Contribution capacity may also change with income. If a physician’s consulting receipts vary substantially from one year to the next, the business should not assume that a prior-year contribution remains appropriate. The calculation should be refreshed for the relevant year and actual business facts. A physician who owns a practice should also consider whether a contribution formula affects eligible employees, rather than treating the owner’s desired amount as the only cost.

### Solo 401(k): possible employee and employer contributions

A solo 401(k) may provide an employee salary-deferral contribution, an employer contribution, or both, subject to plan terms and the applicable limits. The two capacities can make the contribution pattern different from an employer-only arrangement. For a self-employed owner, the exact calculation is not always the familiar calculation used for an employee receiving a regular W-2 paycheck. The appropriate compensation base and contribution amount should be calculated under the business and plan’s actual circumstances.

The employee-deferral feature is not an unlimited second allowance simply because the physician has a second employer or business. Employee deferrals across workplace plans may be subject to a shared individual limit. A physician who makes deferrals through a hospital plan and a separate business plan needs to coordinate them. Employer contributions are evaluated under their own applicable rules and limits, but that does not mean every plan can be considered in isolation. Keep a record of all workplace elections and contributions before deciding on another deferral.

Plan documents can vary. A solo 401(k) does not automatically include every feature associated with a large employer’s 401(k). Roth contributions, loan provisions, investment choices, and other options depend on the provider, the plan document, and current rules. Ask for the actual terms rather than assuming a feature is present because it is familiar from another plan.

### Why a contribution estimate needs more than an income number

A headline estimate based only on gross receipts can be misleading. Consider at least the following inputs: the nature of the business income, business expenses, the owner’s entity and tax treatment, the physician’s age where relevant under current rules, employee eligibility, employer-plan participation, prior deferrals, the plan document, and the tax year. A qualified professional can calculate a permissible contribution using these facts. An online estimate may be useful for an initial question, but it should not be treated as an authorization to contribute.

Limits change. Do not rely on a remembered dollar cap, a past-year calculation, or a percentage from an article. Verify current-year limits, plan deadlines, and funding requirements with the plan provider and qualified professionals. If a contribution has already been made, do not assume it is correct or incorrect from a rough estimate; have it reviewed promptly, since the options for addressing an error can depend on the facts and timing.

## Worked example: what changes when a physician has W-2 and 1099 income?

Consider Dr. Lee, a hypothetical physician with hospital employment and a separate consulting business. After business expenses, the consulting activity has $60,000 in net self-employment earnings before any retirement-plan contribution. Dr. Lee also contributes to the hospital’s 401(k). The figures are illustrative and are not a contribution calculation, legal conclusion, or recommendation. The amount of eligible compensation and the available contribution must be computed using the current rules and complete facts.

Suppose Dr. Lee’s goal is to contribute an additional $10,000 from the consulting activity if the rules and the overall plan picture permit it. The question is not whether $60,000 multiplied by a generic rate produces $10,000. The first question is whether the consulting business and Dr. Lee’s role support the contemplated arrangement. The next question is which contribution components may be used, what the applicable compensation calculation produces, and whether any hospital deferral affects the employee contribution component.

| Decision point         | SEP IRA lens                                                                            | Solo 401(k) lens                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Contribution structure | Employer-funded contribution under the applicable formula and limits.                   | May allow employee deferral plus employer contribution, as permitted by plan terms and current rules.                   |
| Hospital plan          | Include it in the broader retirement-plan review and assess applicable rules.           | Coordinate employee deferrals across the hospital and business plans; do not treat the limit as separate for each plan. |
| What $60,000 tells us  | It is not by itself enough to calculate the permitted amount.                           | It is not by itself enough to calculate either contribution component.                                                  |
| People in the business | Check whether eligible employees affect coverage and employer contribution obligations. | Check whether eligible employees make an owner-only plan unsuitable.                                                    |
| Operating work         | Review participant records, plan terms, and funding steps.                              | Review plan document, elections, records, deadlines, and any reporting duties.                                          |

Now assume, solely to illustrate the decision process, that Dr. Lee’s tax and plan professionals determine that a $10,000 contribution is permissible under one of the arrangements after reviewing the facts. That hypothetical conclusion does not tell us which arrangement is preferable. If a SEP IRA can accommodate the goal and there are no employee obligations that change the cost, Dr. Lee might value its employer-funded design. If an employee deferral feature is important and available after taking hospital-plan deferrals into account, the solo 401(k) may deserve closer examination. If the hospital plan has already used the relevant employee deferral capacity, one of the solo 401(k)’s potential distinctions may be less useful for that year.

Dr. Lee would then compare provider fees, investment options, plan setup and maintenance responsibilities, timing, and the effects of existing IRA balances on any future transactions being considered. If the consulting activity grows, the answer may need to be revisited. If it is a one-year project with modest net earnings, the administrative burden might outweigh a feature the physician cannot use. Those are planning judgments to discuss with professionals, not conclusions supplied by the example.

The lesson is not that $60,000 produces a particular contribution. It is that the same income figure can lead to different choices depending on the other plans, the business and employee facts, and the physician’s intended use of the plan features. First verify the calculation. Then compare what each structure would actually accomplish and require.

## What is different when income comes from an S corporation or partnership?

Business entity and tax classification can change how plan contributions are determined. A self-employed sole proprietor, a partner, and an owner-employee receiving wages from a corporation may not use the same compensation base or calculation. A physician should not copy the method used by a colleague who has a different entity, even if both physicians describe their work as 1099 or practice income.

If a corporation pays the physician W-2 wages, the plan’s treatment of compensation and contributions must be reviewed with the plan document and payroll facts. Do not assume that business distributions count as plan compensation in the same way as wages. If the physician is a partner, confirm how the partnership reports relevant earnings and how the plan treats the partner’s compensation. These issues are specific enough that a generic article should not supply a formula.

A useful first step is to bring the entity’s formation and tax records, recent tax returns, payroll reports where applicable, and the plan’s adoption documents to the tax professional and plan administrator. Ask them to identify the eligible compensation source, the contribution calculation method, and the dates on which elections or contributions must be completed. Request the result in writing, including any assumptions, so the business has a record of why it used that amount.

This article is not a guide to choosing an entity or changing its tax classification. That is a separate question with legal, operational, and tax consequences. Keep the retirement-plan comparison focused on the current, verified structure and ask qualified professionals before making a change to the entity or compensation process.

## How should physicians compare eligible employees and practice staff?

Employee status can turn a personal-looking account decision into an employer plan-design question. Before opening a SEP IRA or attempting to use a solo 401(k), prepare a roster that includes every person connected to the business. Record each person’s employer, work arrangement, start date, service, compensation, ownership relationship, and whether another related business employs or pays them. These facts give a plan professional something concrete to assess.

Do not screen people out based only on labels. A worker might be described as occasional, seasonal, temporary, or a contractor, but the plan’s rules and the actual relationship determine the relevant treatment. A person paid through a staffing arrangement or a related entity may still raise a question that deserves review. The same applies when an owner has more than one practice or business. If facts are unclear, pause the plan choice until the appropriate professional resolves them.

For a SEP IRA, the employer should understand whether eligible employees must receive contributions, how the contribution formula applies to them, and what records or communications are required. The owner should compare the total employer cost, not just the amount going into the owner’s account. If contributing a certain amount for the owner also results in required amounts for eligible staff, include those costs in the decision before committing.

For an owner-only 401(k), the key first question is whether the business has any eligible employee other than a spouse who works in the business. If an eligible employee is present, the plan may need to cover the employee, and the arrangement may no longer meet the assumptions behind an owner-only plan. There may be other plan designs to consider, but that is a distinct plan-design review. A physician should not rely on a generic “solo” product description to settle eligibility.

Think through a practice with a physician-owner, two clinical staff members, and a part-time administrative employee. The owner may initially compare a SEP with a solo 401(k) based on the owner’s contribution opportunity. But the staff members’ eligibility and service, the arrangement’s terms, the practice’s payroll, and any related entity may change the analysis. The administrative employee should not be excluded from the roster simply because the owner thinks the person’s hours are low. The appropriate professional can apply the actual provisions and rules.

For a small consulting business with no staff, the roster exercise may be quick: document that there are no employees, identify whether the spouse works in the business, and verify that there are no related entities that alter the review. The process remains useful even when the answer is straightforward because it creates a defensible record of the facts used when the plan was established.

If the practice already sponsors a retirement plan, start by reviewing that plan rather than assuming another arrangement is needed. It may be possible to amend or adjust a current plan, or it may not be. The effect on staff, cost, contribution goals, and administration should be compared. For practice-level context, see [practice owner planning](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/services/practice-owner-planning).

## How do existing workplace plans affect a solo 401(k)?

A physician employed by a hospital, academic institution, health system, or medical group should gather current plan information before considering a business plan. Find the plan’s summary and recent statements, record employee deferrals already elected or contributed, and identify any employer contributions. If the physician had multiple employers during the year, include each workplace plan. Do not assume that separate payroll systems create separate personal limits.

Employee deferrals across plans may be subject to an overall individual limit. This is why the employee portion of a solo 401(k) cannot be planned independently from a hospital 401(k) or another workplace arrangement. The relevant limits and coordination rules depend on the year and facts, so ask a qualified professional to total the deferrals and confirm any remaining capacity. Preserve payroll confirmations and year-end plan statements rather than relying on memory.

Employer contributions are not identical to employee deferrals, and the distinction can matter. However, the fact that an employer contribution is evaluated separately does not establish that the owner can ignore related-plan, compensation, or business rules. A professional should review all arrangements together. Provide the names of the employers and entities, plan documents, contribution records, and information about ownership in each business.

For a physician with a 403(b), 401(k), governmental or nongovernmental 457(b), or another workplace arrangement, do not infer coordination rules from the plan name. Different plan types can be subject to different provisions. Gather the documents for each plan and ask how each contribution type is treated. The objective is a complete year-level picture, not a guess based on one account portal.

Also consider future years. A physician may change hospitals, join a group, leave employment, or increase consulting work. Plan features and contribution capacity can shift as compensation and workplace participation change. A decision that is reasonable for the current year may need review after a job transition. Keep a calendar reminder to revisit the plan after a material change, rather than treating the first choice as permanent.

## What should you compare besides contributions?

### Plan setup and paperwork

A SEP IRA is often viewed as comparatively straightforward, but it still must be established correctly and operated according to the arrangement’s terms. The employer needs to know how employees are handled, what notices or records are expected, and how contributions are documented. A solo 401(k) generally involves a plan document and operational choices. Ask the provider who prepares and maintains the document, what changes need to be reported, and what the physician remains responsible for.

Before opening an account, ask for a written list of setup steps. Which entity is the sponsor? Who signs the adoption documents? What information does the provider need about workers and ownership? When does the plan become effective? Which elections must be made, and by when? What records should the owner keep? These questions can reveal differences that a marketing page does not explain.

### Ongoing administration and reporting

Administrative responsibilities may include tracking contributions, monitoring eligibility, keeping participant information current, following the plan document, and meeting any applicable reporting requirements. Depending on the plan, its assets, and current rules, a filing obligation may apply. Ask the provider to explain what it handles and what it does not. Do not assume the custodian automatically completes every employer task.

A physician should identify a real owner for each recurring task. If the practice manager maintains records, make sure that person has the necessary information and authority. If the plan provider sends reminders, determine whether the owner must still confirm the facts or submit forms. A reminder is not a substitute for verifying compliance. Set calendar alerts for recurring reviews and keep a folder containing the plan document, contributions, employee roster, and correspondence.

### Investment choices and fees

Investment options depend on the provider and, for a 401(k), the plan terms. A SEP IRA’s available investments also depend on its custodian. Compare the actual menu, account fees, transaction costs, and any service charges that apply. Do not assume that one account type has a better menu or lower cost solely from its label. Ask for current fee information and clarify which services are included.

Keep the investment decision separate from the plan-structure decision. A broad or low-cost investment menu does not fix an eligibility problem or a contribution calculation. Conversely, an administrative feature is not necessarily valuable if it is expensive or unusable for the physician’s circumstances. Make a short comparison list and evaluate each provider’s actual terms alongside the business and plan requirements.

### Optional features

Some physicians ask about Roth contributions, loans, or other features. Their availability depends on the plan document, provider, and current rules. A solo 401(k) does not automatically provide every feature a large employer plan offers, and an advertised feature may carry conditions. Ask the provider to show the exact plan language and explain the operational steps before treating the feature as part of the decision.

When comparing Roth and pre-tax choices, the tax consequences depend on individual circumstances and current law. This article does not assess which tax treatment is appropriate. A qualified tax professional can explain how the choices apply to the physician’s situation. Similarly, a loan provision is not a reason to take on a plan without understanding repayment terms and consequences if the loan is not repaid as required.

## How can existing IRA balances affect the broader review?

A SEP IRA is an IRA for many account and tax purposes, so its balance can be relevant to other IRA-related decisions. A physician considering a Roth conversion, a rollover, or another transaction should inventory all applicable IRA balances and discuss the specific transaction with a tax professional. Do not assume that an account’s SEP label means it can be ignored or isolated from other accounts.

This point should not turn a SEP-versus-solo-401(k) comparison into a separate guide about Roth conversions. The practical takeaway is simple: include current SEP, traditional, rollover, and other relevant IRA statements in the information packet if future IRA transactions are part of the household’s plans. Ask the professional to explain how the balances are treated under the rules that apply to the specific transaction and tax year.

Likewise, do not move an account simply because one arrangement appears more convenient. Transfers and rollovers can have deadlines, eligibility rules, and tax consequences. Confirm the receiving plan’s acceptance policy, the treatment of the assets, and any effect on current and future choices before starting a transfer. Keep copies of both the sending and receiving account records.

## How do cash flow and changing income affect the decision?

A physician with 1099 work may not receive the same amount every month. Consulting, locums assignments, expert work, and practice distributions can vary with scheduling, payer timing, and business expenses. A plan contribution decision should account for cash needed to operate the business and meet household obligations, as well as the retirement goal. Do not commit a contribution based only on an optimistic estimate of annual receipts.

One method is to prepare a year-to-date cash flow snapshot before choosing a contribution schedule. List receipts received, expenses paid, expected near-term obligations, and funds reserved for business operations. Separate amounts already allocated to payroll, vendors, insurance, or other contractual responsibilities. Then ask the tax and plan professionals what contribution timing is permitted and what amount the business can responsibly fund. This is a planning worksheet, not a formula for determining the legal limit.

For a physician whose consulting income arrives in a few large payments, it may be useful to discuss whether the plan permits a single contribution near year-end or whether contributions need to be made on another schedule. For a physician with regular monthly self-employment earnings, periodic transfers may help with cash management, provided the plan rules, calculations, and provider procedures allow them. In either case, distinguish a business cash reserve from money that has been formally contributed to a retirement plan.

Variable income also makes documentation important. Save invoices, payment records, business expense summaries, payroll documents where relevant, and contribution confirmations. If actual net earnings differ substantially from the early estimate, ask the professional to revisit the amount before making a final contribution. A contribution that is too large can be difficult to correct, while a contribution that is too small may mean the physician did not achieve the intended saving goal. The remedy and available timing depend on the facts, so do not wait until a problem is discovered years later.

Career transitions can change the decision as well. A resident who takes a full-time attending position, a physician who reduces clinical work, or a practice owner who brings in a partner may face new plan and compensation facts. Review the plan whenever the business adds staff, changes ownership, starts or ends an employment relationship, or changes the way it pays the physician. A yearly check is useful even when nothing major changes, because the rules and limits can change.

## What do common physician scenarios look like?

### Scenario 1: hospital physician with a small consulting business

Dr. Patel works full time for a hospital and earns $24,000 in gross consulting fees during the year. The consulting activity has business expenses and no employees. Dr. Patel also makes employee deferrals through the hospital’s plan. A SEP IRA and a solo 401(k) are both worth discussing, but the gross fee number is not the contribution base. The first steps are to calculate the relevant net earnings, verify the business and plan facts, and obtain the hospital deferral total.

If the hospital plan already uses the available employee deferral capacity, the employee feature of a solo 401(k) may not add the contribution opportunity Dr. Patel expected for that year. The employer contribution structure may still need review, but it must be calculated under the correct rules. If the activity is likely to remain small and the physician values minimal administration, ask whether a simpler employer-funded arrangement meets the objective. If the business is expected to grow or the physician values plan features that the provider offers, compare the solo plan’s extra work and cost. No answer follows from the $24,000 gross figure alone.

### Scenario 2: physician with one year of locums income

Dr. Green accepts several locums assignments while transitioning between employed positions. The 1099 activity may be temporary, and the physician expects a new W-2 role next year. The relevant questions include whether the activity is organized as a qualifying business, whether any employees or related entities are involved, what workplace plans apply during the transition, and whether there is enough time to establish and operate a plan correctly.

A physician should compare the setup timeline and ongoing responsibility against the likely duration of the income source. If the business is short-lived, a plan with features that require continuing administration may not be worth establishing without a clear understanding of how it will be maintained or closed. If work continues into future years, a plan decision may be more valuable. Ask a professional to map the calendar for establishment, elections, and contributions instead of relying on a general deadline remembered from a prior year.

### Scenario 3: practice owner with employees

Dr. Williams owns a medical practice and employs clinical and administrative staff. The owner wants to save more through a business plan, but the staff roster includes people with different service histories and work schedules. Here, the first task is not to estimate the owner’s contribution. It is to determine who must be considered under each plan option and how the plan’s formula affects the employer’s total cost.

A SEP IRA may require the employer to contribute for eligible employees under the plan rules. An owner-only 401(k) may not fit if eligible employees are present. Dr. Williams should obtain a roster-based eligibility analysis, review any existing workplace plan, and compare full employer costs and administrative responsibilities. If another plan design is needed, a plan professional can compare alternatives. The owner should not adopt a product described as “solo” until the employee question has been resolved.

### Scenario 4: spouse works in the business

Dr. Carter’s spouse performs documented administrative work in a consulting business. The physician is interested in whether the spouse can participate in a retirement plan. The right next step is to verify the actual work, compensation, payroll and tax records, plan terms, and the spouse’s other plan participation. If the spouse is genuinely an employee, a plan may permit participation under its rules, but the details should be confirmed before making an election or contribution.

The household should also compare the administrative consequences. A spouse’s participation can change the contribution pattern, recordkeeping, and coordination needed across workplace plans. The physician and spouse should not assume that a feature is automatically beneficial because it is available. Ask the provider to explain eligibility and required documentation, then ask the tax professional to review the calculations and household interaction.

### Scenario 5: established physician with several retirement accounts

Dr. Evans has a hospital plan, an old employer account, a rollover IRA, and a new consulting business. The physician is evaluating whether the business should sponsor a SEP IRA or solo 401(k) and may consider consolidating accounts later. The immediate task is to make a complete inventory: account type, current balance, employer, plan terms, contributions for the year, and any pending transfer or conversion idea.

Existing accounts do not automatically rule a plan in or out, but they may affect the broader analysis and future transactions. Dr. Evans should ask a tax professional to review how IRA balances interact with any contemplated transaction and ask the plan provider what assets it accepts. The physician should keep the decision to open a business plan separate from a decision to move existing money, unless a qualified professional explains how the actions fit together.

### Scenario 6: resident or fellow with moonlighting income

Dr. Morgan is in training, receives W-2 compensation, and earns additional income from moonlighting. The income may change from rotation to rotation, and the physician already has access to an employer plan. The physician should begin with a realistic picture of the outside activity’s net earnings, the employer plan’s deferral record, and the time needed to maintain another arrangement.

A small side activity can still merit a review, but a plan should not be opened merely because a physician has received a 1099 form. Verify that the activity and earnings support the intended plan, identify whether employees are involved, and ask how contributions would coordinate. Then compare the potential benefit with setup, maintenance, and recordkeeping. A simple spreadsheet of receipts and expenses can help prepare the discussion, but it cannot replace the plan calculation.

## What are the main trade-offs?

**A SEP IRA may be a fit to investigate** when the business wants an employer-funded contribution structure, the owner does not need an employee salary-deferral mechanism for the goal under review, and any eligible employee obligations are understood and acceptable. The arrangement may offer a relatively straightforward experience, depending on the provider and business facts. Its limitations include the absence of the same employee-deferral feature as a 401(k), the need to calculate the employer contribution correctly, and possible cost or coverage duties for eligible employees.

**A solo 401(k) may be a fit to investigate** when the physician has qualifying self-employment income, no eligible employee issue prevents an owner-only arrangement, and an employee-deferral feature or another plan provision matters. It can offer more contribution components, subject to the plan and applicable rules. Its trade-offs include additional setup and operational decisions, shared deferral coordination with other plans, and the need to monitor eligibility and reporting requirements. A physician may not benefit from its extra features if another plan already uses the relevant capacity or if the business cannot sustain its administration.

**Neither option should be chosen by its name alone.** A plan that offers more theoretical flexibility is not automatically better if the physician cannot use it or maintain it. A simpler arrangement is not necessarily best if its employee contribution structure does not match the goal. A business with staff may need a plan-design comparison beyond the two options in this article. The decision should be based on verified eligibility, actual contribution calculations, full costs, provider terms, and the physician’s priorities.

## What questions should you take to a tax or plan professional?

Arrive with organized facts and ask questions that produce a usable answer. A professional should be able to explain what assumptions were used, what the business must do, and when the next review is due. Consider this checklist:

1. **Which entity is sponsoring the plan?** Identify the legal business, its tax classification, and which activity produces the relevant compensation.
2. **What earnings count for the calculation?** Ask how business expenses, compensation, and the physician’s role affect the plan calculation. Do not use gross receipts as a substitute.
3. **Who might be an eligible employee?** Provide a complete worker and ownership roster, including information about related businesses and the spouse’s work.
4. **Which plans already exist?** Gather plan documents and records for hospital, university, group-practice, former-employer, and business plans.
5. **How much has already been deferred?** Obtain year-to-date payroll and contribution information from every relevant workplace plan.
6. **What contribution components are available?** Ask whether the proposed plan permits employee deferrals, employer contributions, or both, and how each is computed.
7. **What is the total employer cost?** Include required amounts for eligible staff, not only the physician-owner’s desired contribution.
8. **What are the establishment and election deadlines?** Confirm the current-year calendar for the entity and plan rather than relying on a generic date.
9. **Who handles administration?** Clarify which tasks the provider performs and which remain with the owner or practice staff.
10. **What fees and investment options apply?** Request current written information and compare actual terms, not assumptions about account labels.
11. **How do existing IRA balances affect other plans?** Bring current statements if a rollover, conversion, or other IRA transaction is being considered.
12. **When should the decision be revisited?** Identify events such as hiring, a job change, a new entity, or a material income shift that should trigger a review.

Keep the answers with the plan documents and contribution records. If different professionals are involved, give each the same accurate facts and ask how their responsibilities fit together. The goal is not to make one professional answer every question; it is to make sure the tax calculation, plan terms, and business operations do not rely on conflicting assumptions.

## How can you organize the decision in a practical sequence?

A short, orderly process can prevent a rushed choice based on a contribution estimate alone. Use these steps as a preparation framework, then confirm the details with the professionals responsible for the plan and tax reporting.

1. **Define the income activity.** Write down whether the income comes from consulting, locums work, a practice, or another activity. Identify the entity receiving the income and the physician’s role.
2. **Separate receipts from earnings.** Assemble invoices, payment summaries, and expense records. Ask a qualified tax professional to determine the relevant earnings figure for plan calculations.
3. **Map the people.** Make a full roster of employees, contractors, owners, and the spouse’s business role. Note related entities and shared staffing arrangements for review.
4. **Inventory all retirement plans.** Include current and former workplace plans and individual accounts. Record employee deferrals already made and any pending transactions.
5. **State the objective.** Is the priority an employer contribution, the possibility of an employee deferral, a particular provider feature, or a workable plan for a business with employees? Rank the objectives rather than trying to optimize every feature at once.
6. **Ask for side-by-side calculations.** Have a qualified professional estimate the permitted contribution under each suitable option using the same income and employee assumptions. Ask for the assumptions and current-year limits in writing.
7. **Compare full operational costs.** Include provider charges, employer contributions for eligible staff, setup work, recordkeeping, and any filing responsibilities. Identify who will perform each task.
8. **Confirm the calendar.** Verify effective dates, elections, contribution timing, and reporting dates for the specific business and plan.
9. **Choose, document, and review.** Keep the signed plan documents, calculation, worker roster, and funding confirmations. Set a date to review the plan after year-end or any major business change.

This sequence is especially useful when a physician is busy or coordinating several advisers. It makes the decision smaller: first establish what business and people are involved; then calculate; then compare operations and features. If the first step reveals employees, multiple entities, or uncertain compensation, resolve that question before comparing account features.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a physician with a W-2 job also open a solo 401(k)?

Possibly. A physician may have a separate qualifying self-employment activity, but the activity, business structure, employee eligibility, and relationship to existing plans must be reviewed. Employee deferrals through different plans may need to be coordinated under an overall individual limit. Confirm the facts and current rules before establishing the plan.

### Can I use a solo 401(k) if my practice has employees?

An owner-only plan generally assumes there are no eligible employees other than a working spouse. If the practice has workers, determine whether any meet the plan’s eligibility criteria and whether related businesses must be included. Do not rely on informal job labels. A benefits professional can review the roster and identify suitable plan designs.

### Can I contribute to both a SEP IRA and a solo 401(k)?

Whether multiple arrangements are appropriate and how contributions interact depends on the businesses, plan sponsors, compensation, and applicable limits. Do not treat each plan’s stated limit as an independent allowance. A tax and plan professional should review all contributions together before any contribution is made.

### Does having 1099 income automatically qualify me for a solo 401(k)?

No. A 1099 form alone does not settle whether the physician has a qualifying business, what compensation is eligible, whether employees affect owner-only eligibility, or how other plans interact. Gather the business and income records and ask the plan provider and tax professional to assess the specific facts.

### What if I already have a SEP IRA?

Do not assume that you must close it or that it prevents every other option. Review the existing plan document, balance, contributions, business facts, and any proposed new arrangement with qualified professionals. If you are considering a transfer, conversion, or other IRA transaction, discuss all relevant IRA balances and tax treatment before taking action.

## Why Moonlighting Taxes for Physicians Need a Separate Review

Moonlighting is extra clinical work beyond a primary job. Residents, fellows, and attendings use it to close an income gap, pay down loans, or test a new setting. The tax question is narrower than the career question. How is this shift paid, and who is sending tax to the IRS?

Many physicians receive a W-2 from a hospital or health system and then add shifts paid on a 1099. Some extra shifts stay on a W-2 through the same employer or a second hospital. Each path changes withholding, self-employment tax, and what you can deduct.

Your main paycheck may withhold as if that job is your only income. The extra dollars can land in a higher federal bracket, and a 1099 payment may arrive with no tax taken out. Waiting until April to discover the gap is how surprise balances happen.

A useful review starts with three facts from the last pay stub and the last 1099 deposit:

* Who paid you, and was the payment W-2 wages or 1099 compensation?
* How much federal and state tax, if any, was withheld from that payment?
* Does your employment contract limit outside clinical work?

Contract limits are a planning issue, not only a tax issue. Some employers restrict moonlighting, conflicts, or use of hospital resources. Read the agreement before you build a tax plan around income you may not be allowed to earn.

## W-2 Shifts and 1099 Shifts Are Not the Same Tax Event

A W-2 moonlighting shift is still wages. The payer withholds federal income tax, Social Security tax, and Medicare tax, and it pays the employer share of Social Security and Medicare. You generally cannot deduct ordinary work costs against those wages.

A 1099 moonlighting shift is usually self-employment income if you are paid as an independent contractor. You report the activity on Schedule C and figure self-employment tax on Schedule SE. The IRS says you generally must pay self-employment tax if net earnings from self-employment are $400 or more. That threshold, the 15.3 percent rate, and the 2026 wage base below are from the IRS page on [self-employment tax](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes).

The self-employment tax rate is 15.3 percent. That rate is 12.4 percent for Social Security and 2.9 percent for Medicare. For 2026, the Social Security portion applies to the first $184,500 of combined wages and net earnings. Medicare tax does not have that wage cap.

If your W-2 wages already reach the Social Security wage base, you do not pay the 12.4 percent Social Security portion of self-employment tax on the 1099 net earnings. You still pay the 2.9 percent Medicare portion on those net earnings. An extra 0.9 percent Additional Medicare Tax can also apply above filing-status thresholds, which IRS [Topic No. 560](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc560) lists as $200,000 for most single filers and $250,000 for married filing jointly.

| Question                       | W-2 moonlighting shift                              | 1099 moonlighting shift                                                                    |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Who sends tax during the year? | The employer withholds from wages.                  | Often nobody, unless you pay estimates or raise W-2 withholding.                           |
| Social Security and Medicare   | The tax is split between you and the employer.      | You pay both halves through self-employment tax, subject to the Social Security wage base. |
| Work costs                     | These costs are usually not deducted on Schedule C. | Ordinary and necessary business costs may reduce net profit.                               |
| Typical forms                  | The payer reports wages on Form W-2.                | Expect Form 1099-NEC, Schedule C, and Schedule SE.                                         |

Do not treat a 1099 payment as extra W-2 money. The cash looks similar. The tax forms do not. If a payer offers either status, ask your CPA before you choose. Entity questions, including an S corporation, belong in a separate review. ## What to Set Aside Before the IRS Bill Arrives

Withholding on the main job does not automatically cover the side job. A flat "save 30 percent" rule is only a starting guess. Your real set-aside depends on your bracket, your state, whether Social Security tax is already maxed, and whether the shift is W-2 or 1099.

Here is an educational example, not a projection of your tax. Assume a single attending already has $220,000 of W-2 wages for 2026. Those wages are already above the $184,500 Social Security wage base on the IRS self-employment tax page. The physician then receives $20,000 of 1099 moonlighting income and has $2,000 of ordinary Schedule C costs. Net profit is $18,000.

Self-employment tax in this example is mostly the Medicare portion, because Social Security tax was already paid on the W-2 wages. The IRS method multiplies net profit by 92.35 percent before applying the rate. The Medicare portion is about 2.9 percent of $16,623, or about $482. Federal income tax is a separate layer. If that net profit is taxed around a 32 percent marginal rate, federal income tax on $18,000 is about $5,760 before the deduction for half of self-employment tax. A state income tax of 5 percent would add about $900.

Put those pieces together and the rough set-aside on this $20,000 payment is about $7,100, or roughly 36 percent of the cash received. Change the bracket, the state, or the wage base and the percentage moves. A resident whose W-2 pay is still under the Social Security wage base would also owe the 12.4 percent Social Security portion on the 1099 net earnings. That can push the set-aside higher even though the income-tax bracket is lower.

Keep the set-aside in a separate account you do not spend. The point is cash for the payment, not a promise about your final rate. Your CPA can replace this sketch with your filing status, state, and other income. Half of the self-employment tax may also be deductible when you figure adjusted gross income, which is another reason this sketch is a ceiling to discuss, not a bill.

## Withholding, Estimated Tax, and the Safe-Harbor Question

The IRS generally expects you to pay tax as you earn income. If withholding and refundable credits will not cover the bill, estimated tax is the usual tool. The IRS [estimated tax FAQ](https://www.irs.gov/faqs/estimated-tax) says you generally must make estimated payments if both of these are true:

* You expect to owe at least $1,000 after withholding and refundable credits.
* Your withholding and refundable credits will be less than the smaller of 90 percent of this year's tax, or 100 percent of last year's tax.

That second test becomes 110 percent of last year's tax if your prior-year adjusted gross income was more than $150,000, or more than $75,000 if married filing separately. The IRS states that higher-income rule on its page about the [underpayment penalty](https://www.irs.gov/payments/underpayment-of-estimated-tax-by-individuals-penalty). Many attending physicians fall in that higher-income group. Matching last year's tax, rather than 110 percent of it, may not be enough.

Estimated tax has four periods. The same IRS FAQ lists these due dates, and a weekend or holiday moves the deadline to the next business day:

1. January 1 through March 31, due April 15.
2. April 1 through May 31, due June 15.
3. June 1 through August 31, due September 15.
4. September 1 through December 31, due January 15 of the next year.

A large block of shifts in one quarter can create a penalty even if you catch up later. Annualizing income is one way to match payments to uneven moonlighting. Form 2210 is the form your CPA uses for that calculation. Do not guess your way through it on a phone between cases.

Raising withholding on the main W-2 job is another path. Withholding is generally treated as paid evenly through the year, which can help when side income arrives late. The form and the dollar change belong in a withholding review with payroll and your CPA. This article does not walk through a W-4 line by line. That adjustment is its own planning question.

One more Medicare point belongs in the same conversation. IRS Topic No. 560 says the 0.9 percent Additional Medicare Tax applies above $200,000 for single filers and above $250,000 for married filing jointly. Employers start the extra withholding only after they pay you more than $200,000, and they do not combine your spouse's wages. Two incomes can cross the joint threshold with no extra withholding at all.

## Deductions, Retirement Dollars, and Year-End Coordination

Deductions are not the main moonlighting tax question, but they change the 1099 number you set aside. For contractor income, ordinary and necessary costs of that work may reduce Schedule C profit. Think licensing tied to the moonlighting role, malpractice that you pay yourself, and travel between the side site and a qualifying business location. Personal commuting from home to a W-2 hospital is not the same thing.

Keep a simple log. Date, site, amount paid, pay type, and any cost with a receipt. A notes app is enough if you can export it for your CPA. Mixed personal and business costs need a split you can explain. This is not a full deduction catalog. Our guide to [tax deductions for doctors](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/blog/tax-deductions-for-doctors) covers W-2 and 1099 rules in more depth.

Retirement contributions can also change the tax picture, and the rules differ by plan. A 1099 shift may open a SEP IRA or solo 401(k) discussion if the moonlighting is a real trade or business and the plan terms allow it. A W-2 shift does not. Employee deferrals also have a combined annual limit across jobs. Ask the plan administrator before you contribute from two places and discover an excess later.

Before December, coordinate four items:

* Year-to-date W-2 wages and year-to-date 1099 deposits.
* Federal and state withholding already taken.
* Estimated payments already sent, and the next due date.
* Retirement contributions still available, without assuming a deduction you have not confirmed.

## A Short Checklist You Can Finish in One Sitting

Physicians rarely need another long theory piece. They need a list they can finish after a shift. Use this once when moonlighting starts, and again before the next estimated-tax date.

1. Save the contract page that allows or limits outside clinical work.
2. Label each payer as W-2 or 1099. Do not mix them in one spreadsheet row.
3. Record gross pay, federal withholding, state withholding, and any Social Security or Medicare tax withheld.
4. For 1099 pay, track costs and set aside a percentage your CPA agrees is reasonable. Revisit it if your bracket or wage base changes.
5. Compare withholding plus estimates with the safe-harbor test, including the 110 percent rule if last year's income was over the IRS threshold.
6. Put the next IRS due date on the same calendar as your call schedule.
7. Ask whether added 1099 profit should change retirement contributions or your broader tax plan before year-end.

If the list shows a gap, fix the cash plan first. Then decide whether the gap is a withholding issue, an estimated-tax issue, or both. Those are related, and they are not the same form.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do physicians pay self-employment tax on moonlighting?

Only when the moonlighting is self-employment income, which is common for 1099 independent-contractor shifts. W-2 moonlighting wages are subject to payroll Social Security and Medicare tax instead. The IRS says net self-employment earnings of $400 or more generally require Schedule SE. For 2026, the 12.4 percent Social Security portion stops once combined wages and net earnings reach $184,500. The 2.9 percent Medicare portion continues.

### Why was so much withheld from one moonlighting check?

A supplemental W-2 payment is sometimes withheld at a flat federal rate that is higher than your usual paycheck rate. That can look like half the shift disappeared. It may be withholding, not your final tax. Compare the year-to-date withholding with your expected tax before you change anything. A 1099 payment with no withholding creates the opposite problem.

### How much should a physician set aside for moonlighting taxes?

There is no single rate. In the example above, a single attending already over the Social Security wage base set aside about 36 percent of a $20,000 1099 payment after a small expense. A resident still under the wage base may owe self-employment tax that includes Social Security tax as well. Use your bracket, your state, and your pay type, then confirm the figure with your CPA.

### Can I wait until I file and pay the balance then?

Sometimes a balance due is simply the last true-up. You can still owe an underpayment penalty if you did not pay enough during the year. The IRS says the safe harbor is generally 90 percent of the current year's tax, or 100 percent of last year's tax, rising to 110 percent when prior-year adjusted gross income exceeded $150,000. Crossing that test late does not erase an earlier shortfall.

### Does moonlighting income change retirement contributions?

It can, and the plan type matters. Contractor income may allow a SEP IRA or solo 401(k) if you have a qualifying business and the plan document permits it. W-2 income uses the employer's plan. Employee deferrals are coordinated across plans. Confirm the limit with the plan administrator before you add a contribution from moonlighting pay.

Physician compensation can shape your financial life for decades. The right contract may give you dependable cash flow, meaningful retirement benefits, ownership potential, and room to pursue personal goals. The wrong one can leave you with unclear bonus calculations, excessive call, unexpected tax obligations, or expensive repayment clauses. To make a sound decision, look beyond base pay and examine how the offer works in practice. Review guaranteed and estimated income separately. Compare benefits and downside risk. Ask how production is measured and whether the targets are realistic. Then consider how your compensation fits with taxes, student loans, investing, insurance, retirement, and legacy planning. A complete review gives you a clearer path forward.

## Key Takeaways

* **Look beyond base salary**: Add bonuses, call pay, retirement contributions, insurance, malpractice coverage, equity, loan assistance, and repayment obligations to determine an offer’s real value.
* **Test the compensation formula**: Compare benchmarks for your specialty and market, request sample wRVU or collections calculations, and model conservative, expected, and high-income scenarios.
* **Connect the contract to your financial plan**: Estimate after-tax cash flow, review benefits and ownership terms, and coordinate tax, retirement, investment, insurance, and estate decisions before signing.

## What Does Physician Compensation Include?

A physician’s compensation is rarely limited to the annual salary listed in an offer letter. Your total package may include productivity pay, quality incentives, call stipends, retirement contributions, insurance, paid time off, loan repayment, equity, and other benefits. Each component can affect your cash flow, tax obligations, professional flexibility, and long-term wealth.

Review the complete compensation structure before comparing offers. A higher base salary may not be the better choice if it comes with a demanding call schedule, an unrealistic productivity threshold, limited benefits, or substantial repayment obligations. A lower guaranteed salary may be more attractive when it includes a practical bonus formula, strong retirement contributions, partnership potential, or meaningful loan repayment.

The [American Medical Association’s physician salary guidance](https://www.ama-assn.org/medical-residents/transition-resident-attending/are-physician-salaries-going-your-specialty) can help you understand broad specialty benchmarks. National averages provide context, but they cannot determine whether a specific offer is fair. You also need to evaluate your specialty, location, practice setting, workload, payer mix, and career goals.

Before signing, separate guaranteed compensation from estimated compensation. Ask how each amount is calculated, when it is paid, and what conditions could change it. This approach gives you a clearer picture of both immediate income and the financial value of the entire agreement.

### Base salary and guaranteed income

Base salary is the fixed amount your employer agrees to pay, usually in exchange for a defined schedule and specific clinical responsibilities. It provides predictable income during your first months or years in a new position, before productivity or quality bonuses become a major part of your earnings.

Ask whether the salary is guaranteed for the entire contract term or only during an initial period. Some agreements provide guaranteed pay for one or two years, then shift to a production-based formula. Clarify the expected work schedule, patient volume, call obligations, administrative duties, and conditions that could reduce your guaranteed pay.

Starting salaries vary significantly by specialty, location, and employer. The AMA reported an average starting salary of $403,000 across the physician offers it tracked. Use that figure as general context, not as a target for every physician or employment setting. Compare it with compensation data for your specialty, region, experience level, and expected workload.

### wRVUs, collections, conversion factors, and productivity bonuses

Many compensation plans connect part of your pay to productivity. Common measures include work relative value units, or wRVUs, collected revenue, or a combination of both. A wRVU-based plan typically pays you after you reach a defined threshold, using a conversion factor that assigns a dollar amount to each additional wRVU.

Ask for the complete formula and a sample calculation before signing. Find out how the practice sets your threshold, whether it can change during the contract, and how office visits, procedures, care coordination, and administrative work are counted. You should also understand when productivity is measured and when bonuses are paid.

Request regular access to your production reports. The [IM Career Source contract guide](https://careersource.im.org/career-resources/on-the-job-3/physician-contract-negotiation-a-practical-guide-for-internal-medicine-physicians-102) recommends confirming your ability to review wRVU information and understand how productivity thresholds are established. Without that transparency, you may have difficulty verifying whether your compensation reflects your work.

### Quality, call, leadership, signing, and retention incentives

Some offers include bonuses tied to quality measures, patient satisfaction, access, documentation, clinical outcomes, or other performance goals. These incentives are becoming more common as organizations place greater emphasis on value-based care. Ask which measures apply to you, who controls the data, how often results are reported, and whether the goals are realistic for your patient population.

Call pay should be listed separately from your base salary whenever possible. Confirm how weekday, weekend, overnight, and holiday call are compensated, along with how often you are expected to participate. Ask whether you receive additional pay for procedures or inpatient coverage performed during call.

Signing and retention bonuses may make an offer more appealing, but review the repayment terms carefully. The [AAFP’s physician contract guidance](https://www.aafp.org/life-and-career/employment-contracting/negotiating-physician-contracts) recommends examining performance incentives and other contract provisions closely. A bonus may need to be repaid if you leave before a specified date, and the repayment amount may be prorated or owed in full.

### Benefits, equity, relocation, and loan repayment

Benefits can represent a meaningful part of your compensation, particularly when two offers have similar salaries. Review health insurance premiums, deductibles, disability coverage, life insurance, dependent coverage, and employer retirement contributions. Confirm when each benefit begins and whether you must meet specific eligibility requirements.

For physicians joining a private practice, ask whether equity or partnership is available. A partnership track should explain the timeline, buy-in amount, valuation method, voting rights, distributions, and responsibilities. Equity is not valuable simply because it appears in an offer. You need to understand how it is earned, funded, valued, and transferred if you leave.

Relocation assistance and student loan repayment can reduce immediate financial pressure. Ask whether each payment is taxable, whether you must remain employed for a set period, and what happens if the employment relationship ends early. A [physician-focused tax strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you evaluate how these payments fit into your broader financial plan.

### Malpractice, tail coverage, PTO, CME, and retirement plans

Malpractice insurance deserves careful attention. Determine whether the policy is claims-made or occurrence-based, who pays the premiums, and whether the employer covers tail insurance when you leave. Tail coverage can cost tens of thousands of dollars in some specialties, so this provision can materially change the value of an offer.

Review paid time off separately from CME time. Confirm the number of vacation, sick, personal, and CME days, along with any limits on carrying unused time forward. Ask whether the employer pays for licensing, board certification, professional memberships, conferences, travel, and educational materials.

Retirement benefits may include a 401(k), 403(b), 457(b), pension, profit-sharing contribution, or cash balance plan. Pay attention to matching formulas, vesting schedules, contribution limits, and whether the plan accepts after-tax or Roth contributions. [CompHealth’s contract guide](https://comphealth.com/resources/physician-contract-negotiation) also recommends considering the long-term value of benefits instead of focusing only on stated salary.

### Total compensation, take-home pay, and downside risk

To compare offers accurately, calculate total compensation across a full year. Include base salary, expected bonuses, call pay, employer retirement contributions, insurance benefits, CME funding, paid leave, loan repayment, relocation assistance, and any equity or distributions. Separate guaranteed amounts from estimates, then model conservative, expected, and strong performance scenarios.

Next, estimate your take-home pay after federal and state taxes, payroll taxes, retirement contributions, insurance premiums, and other deductions. A large bonus may not improve your monthly cash flow if it arrives once a year or depends on conditions you cannot control. A signing bonus may also create a future repayment obligation.

Pay close attention to downside risk. Compensation plans can include clawbacks, delayed bonus payments, uncompensated administrative work, restrictive production thresholds, or penalties tied to early termination. The [NEJM CareerCenter’s contract guidance](https://resources.nejmcareercenter.org/article/physician-employment-contracts-strategies-for-avoiding-pitfalls) discusses why these terms deserve careful review. A clear compensation model should show what you can reasonably earn, what you must do to earn it, and what could reduce your pay.

## What Drives Physician Compensation?

Physician compensation reflects more than specialty and years in practice. Employers consider the revenue a physician may generate, the services they provide, local demand, and the financial structure of the practice. The same physician can receive very different offers based on location, call schedule, payer mix, ownership model, productivity expectations, and contract terms.

Compensation surveys offer useful benchmarks, but they do not determine the value of a specific position. To compare offers fairly, look at the complete arrangement: base pay, incentives, benefits, malpractice coverage, retirement contributions, partnership opportunities, schedule, and the risk of variable income. A higher salary may come with heavier call, aggressive production targets, or fewer protections, while a lower salary may include stronger benefits and more predictable cash flow.

### Specialty demand, procedures, and clinical revenue

Specialty demand is one of the strongest influences on physician compensation. Surgical and procedural specialties often generate significant clinical revenue because physicians perform high-value procedures, manage complex cases, or support services that require specialized equipment and staff. The [Doximity 2025 Physician Compensation Report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025) lists neurosurgery, thoracic surgery, and orthopaedic surgery among the specialties with the highest average compensation.

Still, specialty alone does not determine income. Compensation may depend on procedure volume, operating room access, referral patterns, payer contracts, and the practice’s staffing capacity. A specialist joining a busy group with established referrals may have a different earning opportunity than one entering a smaller market with limited infrastructure.

### Experience, credentials, and leadership duties

Experience can strengthen a physician’s negotiating position, but it does not automatically lead to a higher salary. Employers typically assess the value a physician brings through clinical productivity, specialized training, patient retention, teaching, quality work, and the ability to develop a service line.

Additional credentials may support higher pay when they address a specific organizational need. Fellowship training, board certification, advanced procedural skills, or expertise in an underserved area can make a candidate more valuable. Leadership duties may also warrant additional compensation when a physician manages a department, oversees quality initiatives, recruits clinicians, or takes on administrative accountability.

The [White Coat Investor’s guide to complex physician contract negotiations](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/anatomy-of-a-complex-physician-contract-negotiation) emphasizes the importance of showing how an established physician creates value. Before requesting a compensation review, document measurable contributions instead of relying only on tenure.

### Clinical hours, call, patient volume, and workload

A salary means little without the workload attached to it. Two physicians may earn similar amounts while working very different schedules. Compare clinic hours, operating room time, inpatient duties, weekend coverage, overnight call, response expectations, and daily patient volume.

Call can materially change the value of an offer, especially when it is frequent, unrestricted, or included in base pay without a separate stipend. Ask how often you take call, whether you receive post-call relief, and whether you must remain on-site or respond remotely. Patient messages, documentation, open-access scheduling, and care coordination can also add substantial work outside scheduled visits.

Productivity-based pay requires close review. A high wRVU target may be reasonable for one specialty and unrealistic for another. Examine the threshold, conversion factor, attribution rules, and payment timing. [Doximity’s compensation data](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025) also shows that compensation varies by practice setting, making workload and workplace structure important parts of the comparison.

### Geography, payer mix, referrals, and practice economics

Location affects compensation through physician demand, practice revenue, and operating costs. Rural and underserved communities may offer signing bonuses, loan repayment, relocation assistance, or other incentives to attract clinicians. However, a higher gross salary does not always produce more usable income after housing, taxes, insurance, commuting, and childcare costs.

Payer mix also influences revenue potential. A practice with more commercial insurance may have a different financial profile from one serving mostly Medicare, Medicaid, or uninsured patients. Referral networks, local competition, hospital contracts, and access to operating rooms or diagnostic facilities can affect how quickly a physician builds a patient panel.

When considering a move, compare an offer with local conditions rather than relying on a national average. [Doximity’s geographic compensation data](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025) provides useful context, but local market information can help clarify how a benchmark applies to a particular opportunity.

### Employment model, ownership, and negotiating leverage

The employment model shapes both compensation and financial risk. Hospital-employed physicians often receive a defined salary, benefits, and productivity incentives. Private practice physicians may have greater operational control and a path to ownership, but they may also share responsibility for overhead, staffing, equipment, and collections. Locum tenens and independent arrangements can offer flexibility and higher short-term rates, though they often provide fewer benefits and less income stability.

Ownership may create income through practice distributions or the eventual sale of an ownership interest. It may also require a buy-in, personal guarantees, or a period of reduced cash flow. Review financial statements, overhead allocations, debt obligations, and distribution history before treating a partnership track as part of the offer.

Negotiating leverage can come from a scarce specialty, strong production history, leadership experience, or willingness to work in a difficult-to-staff location. The [American Medical Association’s physician contracting guidance](https://www.ama-assn.org/medical-residents/transition-resident-attending/physician-contracting-do-s-and-don-ts-interviews) recommends reviewing employment agreements carefully and addressing important terms before signing.

### Why seniority alone does not set compensation

Years in practice can contribute to expertise, referrals, and efficiency, but seniority alone does not establish market value. Many organizations use formulas based on wRVUs, collections, quality measures, leadership responsibilities, or a combination of these factors. A newer physician in a shortage specialty may earn more than a veteran physician with a lighter schedule.

Experience still matters when it leads to measurable contributions. Senior physicians may warrant higher compensation when they manage complex cases, mentor colleagues, lead departments, improve access, or strengthen the practice’s reputation. The important step is connecting experience to specific responsibilities and results.

For a compensation review, gather production reports, patient access data, referral growth, quality scores, teaching duties, committee work, and leadership outcomes. This evidence creates a stronger discussion than simply pointing to the number of years you have worked for the organization.

### Why higher pay does not indicate higher care quality

Compensation generally reflects a role’s financial and operational value. It does not directly rank clinical skill, professionalism, or patient care quality. Procedural services may generate more revenue than cognitive care, even though both are essential. Physicians serving complex or underserved populations may also carry demanding workloads that compensation surveys do not fully capture.

Quality incentives can connect payment to patient experience, safety, access, or clinical outcomes, but those measures require careful interpretation. Results may depend on patient population, staffing, available resources, documentation, and factors outside a physician’s control. A high-paying offer may include demanding productivity expectations, while a lower-paying role may provide stronger support, flexibility, or long-term stability.

Assess compensation and care conditions separately. Review staffing, scheduling, clinical autonomy, care coordination, administrative support, and quality expectations. As [CompHealth’s physician contract guide](https://comphealth.com/resources/physician-contract-negotiation) explains, the long-term compensation structure should be clearly defined in the contract, not judged by the headline salary alone.

## How Does Compensation Differ by Specialty?

Physician compensation varies widely by specialty, but salary alone does not explain the difference. A surgeon’s offer may reflect procedure volume, call coverage, production expectations, and the revenue generated by operating rooms. A primary care offer may place more weight on panel size, visit volume, care coordination, and quality measures. Academic positions may provide less guaranteed clinical income while offering protected research time, teaching opportunities, or a path to leadership.

Compensation benchmarks can help you assess whether an offer appears competitive, but they cannot replace a careful contract review. Compare the salary with the schedule, staffing, call burden, benefits, location, payer mix, and opportunity to earn productivity incentives. The [Doximity 2025 Physician Compensation Report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025), for example, analyzes full-time physicians working at least 40 hours per week across 60 major metropolitan areas. That makes it useful for identifying broad patterns, but not for pricing every individual offer.

Career stage matters, too. A first attending contract, partnership track, medical director role, and late-career reduced schedule may use entirely different compensation structures. Before comparing numbers, confirm that you are comparing similar jobs with similar responsibilities.

### Medical, surgical, primary care, and subspecialty benchmarks

Surgical and procedural specialties generally report higher average compensation than primary care and many cognitive specialties. The Doximity report lists neurosurgery as the highest-paid specialty at an average of $749,140, followed by thoracic surgery at $689,969 and orthopaedic surgery at $679,517. These figures reflect broad survey data, not a guaranteed salary for every physician in those fields.

Primary care compensation may look lower on paper, even though demand remains strong. Internal medicine, family medicine, and pediatrics physicians can face very different patient volumes, panel expectations, quality incentives, and call schedules depending on the employer. Subspecialists also see wide variation. A procedure-heavy role may pay differently from a consultative role within the same broader field.

Use specialty benchmarks as a starting point, then adjust for the actual position. A hospital-employed specialist, private practice partner, and academic physician may have different income potential despite completing similar training.

### Highest-paying specialties and procedural revenue

The highest average compensation is often concentrated in specialties with substantial procedural revenue, complex cases, technical expertise, and demanding call schedules. Neurosurgery, thoracic surgery, orthopaedic surgery, plastic surgery, cardiology, and other procedural fields may rank near the top of national surveys.

That pattern does not mean procedures automatically produce a better financial outcome for the physician. High gross compensation can come with long hours, expensive malpractice coverage, frequent call, operating room delays, and significant physical or administrative demands. A contract with a large production component may also require enough referrals, equipment, staffing, and operating time to make the income target realistic.

When reviewing a procedural offer, ask how the employer calculates production. Request the expected case volume, historical physician productivity, wRVU threshold, conversion factor, payer mix, and bonus schedule. Also ask whether staffing shortages or scheduling limits could make the advertised compensation difficult to reach.

### Cognitive versus procedural specialties

Cognitive specialties generate revenue primarily through evaluation, diagnosis, chronic disease management, care coordination, and follow-up rather than high-cost procedures. Compensation may therefore depend on patient panel size, visit complexity, documentation, quality scores, risk adjustment, and access targets.

The Doximity report found that surgical specialists earned 87% more than primary care physicians in 2024. Although that gap narrowed from 100% in 2022, it remains substantial. The difference highlights how reimbursement models can value procedures differently from ongoing cognitive care, even though both are essential to patient outcomes.

For physicians in cognitive specialties, look beyond base salary. A fair offer should account for daily visit volume, inbox responsibilities, after-hours work, same-day access requirements, care management duties, and staffing support. If compensation depends on quality or panel measures, ask whether those metrics are within your control and how the employer calculates them.

### Starting salaries, demand, and recruitment pressure

Starting compensation can differ significantly from the income of an established physician. Employers may offer a guaranteed salary during the first year, followed by a productivity-based formula once you build a patient panel or referral base. Signing bonuses, relocation assistance, loan repayment, and retention payments can also make a first offer appear more generous than its recurring compensation.

The [American Medical Association’s review of physician salaries by specialty](https://www.ama-assn.org/medical-residents/transition-resident-attending/are-physician-salaries-going-your-specialty) reports an average starting salary of $403,000 for the physicians tracked, slightly below the previous year’s $406,000 average. That figure offers a useful reference point, but it combines different specialties and does not describe every market or employment model.

Demand can strengthen your negotiating position, particularly in rural areas, shortage areas, and specialties with limited local coverage. Still, recruitment pressure does not guarantee a strong contract. Ask when guaranteed income ends, what happens if volume develops slowly, and whether bonus targets are based on realistic assumptions.

### Specialty-specific call, staffing, and production expectations

Two offers with the same base salary can create very different work lives. One may include frequent overnight call, weekend coverage, and responsibility for new consults. Another may offer a lighter call schedule but require high clinic volume, extensive inbox work, or aggressive wRVU targets.

Review the call schedule in writing. Ask how often you will take weekday, weekend, and holiday call, how coverage is handled, and whether you receive additional compensation. Clarify whether call is paid separately, included in the salary, or tied to a production formula. You should also understand the expected number of patients, procedures, operating days, consults, and administrative hours.

A practical [physician contract negotiation guide for internal medicine physicians](https://careersource.im.org/career-resources/on-the-job-3/physician-contract-negotiation-a-practical-guide-for-internal-medicine-physicians-102) emphasizes reviewing workload and staffing expectations before signing. That advice applies across specialties. Insufficient nursing, advanced practice, anesthesia, or administrative support can affect patient care and your ability to meet compensation targets.

### Mean, median, and percentile compensation

A compensation survey may report a mean, median, or percentile figure, and each measure tells you something different. The mean is the mathematical average, which can be influenced by unusually high or low values. The median is the midpoint, meaning half of the reported physicians earned more and half earned less. Percentiles show where an individual figure falls within the surveyed group.

Percentile data is often more useful than one national average. A physician with several years of experience, a busy subspecialty practice, and significant call responsibilities may reasonably compare an offer with a higher percentile. A new attending in an academic role with protected teaching time may be better matched to a lower percentile.

Read the methodology before using any figure. Check the survey year, geographic coverage, specialty definitions, sample size, hours worked, and whether the figures include bonuses or benefits. Doximity’s data, for example, focuses on full-time US physicians who work at least 40 hours per week. Those boundaries may not match your schedule or employment model.

### Why one average salary can misrepresent an offer

An average salary cannot show whether an offer is fair for your specific role. A $400,000 salary may be attractive for a physician with limited call, strong staffing, generous retirement benefits, and a manageable patient schedule. It may be less appealing if it requires frequent nights, a large unpaid administrative workload, high malpractice exposure, or a production threshold that few physicians reach.

Location and practice setting also change the comparison. A hospital-employed physician, private practice owner, academic faculty member, and locum tenens physician may report compensation differently. Ownership distributions, retirement contributions, paid time off, and health insurance can materially affect the value of the package.

When comparing an offer, review guaranteed pay, realistic incentive income, benefits, expenses, and downside risk together. [Weatherby Healthcare’s contract negotiation guidance](https://weatherbyhealthcare.com/blog/physician-contract-negotiation) recommends considering workload, location, benefits, and practice conditions rather than focusing only on salary. A complete compensation review can then inform your tax strategy, retirement planning, and [wealth management for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

## How Does Location Affect Compensation?

Location can shape physician compensation as much as specialty, experience, or workload. Two physicians with similar training may receive different offers because of local demand, patient demographics, payer mix, practice economics, and competition among employers.

When comparing locations, look beyond the advertised salary. State taxes, housing, malpractice premiums, commuting time, call expectations, and referral patterns all affect what your income can support. A compensation package that appears modest may provide more purchasing power in a lower-cost market, while a larger salary may leave less available for saving and investing after local expenses.

### Regional, state, and metropolitan pay differences

Physician compensation varies widely among regions, states, and metropolitan areas. According to the [Doximity 2025 Physician Compensation Report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025), the metropolitan areas with the highest average physician compensation in 2024 included Rochester, Minnesota, at $495,532; St. Louis, Missouri, at $484,883; and Los Angeles, California, at $470,198.

The report also found that average compensation increased in 54 of the 60 metropolitan areas studied. These figures show why a national specialty average cannot determine whether a specific offer is competitive. Compare data for the state, metropolitan area, specialty, and practice setting you are actually considering.

A local benchmark may reveal whether an offer reflects prevailing market conditions or simply a strong recruiting pitch. Ask how the employer established the salary, productivity expectations, and incentive opportunity.

### High-paying markets versus cost-adjusted income

A high salary does not automatically create greater financial flexibility. Housing, childcare, transportation, taxes, and everyday expenses can consume much of the difference between a high-paying metropolitan area and a lower-cost location. The Doximity report notes that expensive coastal cities often rank lower after compensation is adjusted for cost of living.

For example, a physician earning more in a major coastal city may have less available for investing, debt repayment, or home ownership than a physician earning less in a more affordable market. Your priorities matter, too. A higher salary may not compensate for a longer commute, more expensive housing, or less time with family.

When comparing offers, estimate your after-tax income and recurring expenses in each location. The [American Medical Association’s salary guidance](https://www.ama-assn.org/medical-residents/transition-resident-attending/are-physician-salaries-going-your-specialty) also emphasizes the importance of considering salary alongside purchasing power and the complete employment package.

### Rural, underserved, and shortage-area incentives

Rural and underserved communities may offer additional incentives because recruiting and retaining physicians can be difficult. These incentives may include signing payments, relocation assistance, loan repayment, housing support, retention bonuses, enhanced benefits, or higher rates for call coverage.

Federal and state programs can also affect an offer’s value. Physicians serving eligible communities may qualify for National Health Service Corps loan repayment, depending on the site, specialty, contract, and service requirements. Treat these programs as part of total compensation, not as an extra benefit separate from your financial analysis.

Incentive payments often come with conditions. Review the required service period, repayment obligations, eligible loans, worksite rules, and termination provisions before including the funds in your plan. Also assess staffing, call coverage, patient volume, and professional support. A rural offer may provide meaningful financial value, but the workload and resources should fit your long-term goals.

### State taxes, malpractice, housing, and commuting costs

Two offers with the same gross salary can produce very different results after local expenses. State and local taxes affect take-home pay, while malpractice premiums and tail coverage can change the financial risk of an employment agreement. Housing costs, property taxes, insurance, childcare, and commuting expenses also affect monthly cash flow.

Review who pays malpractice premiums and whether the policy includes tail coverage. If you leave the practice, an uncovered tail policy could create a substantial expense. The same applies to relocation benefits, signing bonuses, and loan repayment. Some payments may be taxable, subject to repayment, or tied to a specific employment period.

Create a location-specific budget before comparing offers. Include housing, transportation, family expenses, professional costs, and estimated taxes. Then calculate what remains for debt repayment, retirement contributions, emergency reserves, and taxable investing. This produces a more useful comparison than salary alone.

### Local demand, competition, payer mix, and referrals

Compensation reflects the economics of the local practice, not just a physician’s credentials. A community with a shortage of specialists may offer stronger recruitment incentives, while a market with many physicians may limit salary growth. Local demand, competition, referral relationships, and patient access all influence the revenue a practice can generate.

Payer mix matters, too. A practice serving more commercially insured patients may have different revenue patterns than one serving a larger Medicare or Medicaid population. Referral volume can affect productivity for procedural specialists, surgeons, and subspecialists. Primary care compensation may depend more heavily on panel size, visit volume, quality measures, and care coordination.

Specialty growth can vary by location. The [Doximity compensation report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025) noted notable compensation increases in specialties including pediatric nephrology and preventive medicine. Use these figures as context, then ask how local demand affects your proposed schedule, patient volume, referral access, and incentive formula.

### Maryland and Mid-Atlantic compensation factors

Maryland and the broader Mid-Atlantic region have distinct compensation considerations. The area includes major academic medical centers, community hospitals, private practices, specialty groups, and competitive urban markets. Compensation may differ significantly among the Washington, DC, and Baltimore areas, suburban communities, and more rural parts of the region.

Primary care demand is strong throughout many Mid-Atlantic communities, yet compensation may not always reflect that need. The Doximity report highlights a mismatch between high demand for primary care services and comparatively lower pay in Maryland and the Mid-Atlantic. That gap can affect recruitment, retention, call coverage, and negotiating leverage.

Physicians considering an offer near White Plains, Maryland, or elsewhere in the region should compare the full package against local housing costs, taxes, commuting patterns, and malpractice expenses. A physician-focused [wealth management plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) can help connect the offer to debt repayment, investing, insurance, and long-term cash flow decisions.

### Gross salary versus purchasing power and cash flow

Gross salary is the starting point for evaluating an offer, not the final answer. The AMA reports that the average starting salary across tracked physicians was $403,000, slightly below the previous year’s average of $406,000. National figures provide useful context, but they do not show what remains after taxes and living expenses.

To evaluate purchasing power, estimate your net income under the proposed compensation formula. Include base salary, expected bonuses, retirement contributions, health insurance costs, student loan payments, housing, and other recurring expenses. If the offer includes productivity pay, model conservative, expected, and strong production scenarios rather than assuming the highest possible bonus.

Also consider how stable your cash flow will be. A larger variable component may create planning challenges if patient volume, payer rates, or referral patterns change. Coordinating your offer review with [tax planning for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you estimate the income you are likely to keep and use each year.

## How Do Practice Setting and Career Stage Affect Compensation?

Physician compensation depends on more than specialty and location. Your practice setting, role within the organization, and career stage can change both your total income and the way you earn it. A hospital-employed physician may receive a predictable salary with productivity incentives, while a private practice owner may have greater long-term earning potential alongside responsibility for overhead, staffing, and business decisions.

Career stage matters, too. Residents and fellows are usually reviewing their first attending contract. Established physicians may be negotiating productivity formulas, medical director pay, or partnership terms. Physicians approaching retirement may place greater value on schedule flexibility, income stability, and a smooth transition than on the highest possible salary. To compare opportunities fairly, review the entire compensation structure rather than focusing only on the advertised base pay.

### Employed hospital and health system pay

Hospital and health system employment commonly combines a guaranteed salary with productivity incentives, quality bonuses, and benefits. This model can provide predictable cash flow and administrative support, with the employer handling many billing, staffing, compliance, and office management responsibilities. It may suit physicians who prefer to focus on patient care without managing a separate business.

The [Doximity 2025 Physician Compensation Report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025) lists average compensation of $439,319 for hospital-employed physicians and $438,983 for physicians working within a health system, integrated delivery network, or accountable care organization. These figures offer useful context, but they do not show how an individual offer is calculated. Review clinical hours, call expectations, wRVU thresholds, conversion factors, bonus timing, and repayment provisions before making a comparison.

### Academic medicine, research, and teaching pay

Academic compensation often reflects a broader role than clinical care alone. Your package may include protected time for research, teaching, committee work, publications, mentoring, and program development. Because these responsibilities can reduce clinical hours, a lower clinical salary may not tell the full story of the position’s value.

Academic packages may also include productivity incentives, quality bonuses, and restrictive covenants that vary by institution and practice setting. The [American College of Physicians contract negotiation guide](https://careersource.im.org/career-resources/on-the-job-3/physician-contract-negotiation-a-practical-guide-for-internal-medicine-physicians-102) recommends reviewing the complete agreement, including expectations that may not appear in the base salary. Ask how protected time is defined, whether research funding is guaranteed, and what happens if grant support or departmental priorities change.

### Private practice, partnership, and owner income

Private practice compensation can follow several paths. An associate may receive a salary and production bonus, while a partner may earn income from personal collections, practice profits, ancillary services, or distributions. Ownership can provide more control and long-term value, but it also introduces financial obligations that do not appear in an employed offer.

The share of physicians working in private practice fell from 60.1% in 2012 to 42.2% in 2024, according to the [Doximity compensation report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025). This shift makes it especially important to examine ownership opportunities carefully. Ask how profits are allocated, how expenses are assigned, and whether your income depends on personal collections, practice-wide performance, or both. Review the practice’s financial statements before assuming partnership will produce higher earnings.

### Locum tenens, telemedicine, and hybrid pay models

Locum tenens work may offer attractive hourly or daily rates, scheduling flexibility, and the chance to evaluate different practice environments. However, physicians should account for unpaid travel time, gaps between assignments, licensing costs, housing, benefits, and malpractice coverage. A higher hourly rate does not necessarily produce higher annual income after these costs and interruptions are included.

Telemedicine and hybrid roles may use hourly pay, per-visit compensation, productivity measures, or a salaried structure. Review how no-shows, documentation time, technology problems, and follow-up care are handled. When comparing opportunities, include salary, bonuses, benefits, and other incentives, as recommended in this [physician contract negotiation guidance](https://weatherbyhealthcare.com/blog/physician-contract-negotiation). Then compare expected annual work hours and after-tax cash flow, not only the stated rate.

### Residents, fellows, and first attending offers

Residents and fellows often review their first attending offer with limited experience interpreting compensation formulas. Base salary matters, but so do the ramp-up period, expected patient volume, call obligations, benefits, and timing of productivity incentives. An offer with a lower starting salary may become more attractive if it includes a reasonable wRVU threshold, adequate onboarding time, and a clear path to higher earnings.

Start the process early. Beginning compensation discussions nine to 18 months before your anticipated start date can give you more time to compare offers and negotiate important terms, according to this [guide to preparing for compensation discussions](https://www.michaeljohnsonlegal.com/are-you-prepared-for-compensation-discussions). Request the full contract, bonus formula, benefits summary, and restrictive covenant before accepting a verbal offer. An attorney who regularly reviews physician agreements can identify terms that may affect your income after the first year.

### Established physicians, medical directors, and leadership roles

Established physicians may have more negotiating leverage, but compensation increases usually require a documented connection between added value and added pay. That value may come from higher patient volume, improved access, program growth, quality outcomes, referral development, teaching, or administrative leadership.

Medical director and leadership roles should include clearly defined responsibilities and protected time. Confirm whether the leadership stipend is separate from clinical compensation and whether the employer can change it at its discretion. Review meeting expectations, reporting duties, travel, and performance measures. As [White Coat Investor explains](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/anatomy-of-a-complex-physician-contract-negotiation), established physicians often need a specific plan to demonstrate how their work creates value before seeking higher compensation.

### Practice ownership, buy-ins, overhead, and distributions

A partnership offer should be evaluated as both a compensation agreement and a business investment. A buy-in may provide access to future profits, voting rights, real estate, equipment, or ancillary revenue, but the financial terms must be transparent. Ask what you are purchasing, how the value was determined, and whether the practice can require additional capital contributions later.

Overhead is another major factor. Rent, staff compensation, supplies, billing, insurance, technology, and marketing can reduce the amount available for distributions. The agreement should explain how collections, expenses, and profits are calculated. [CompHealth’s physician contract guidance](https://comphealth.com/resources/physician-contract-negotiation) emphasizes the importance of defining long-term compensation in writing. Request several years of financial statements, understand the buyout terms, and have your accountant review the practice before committing to ownership.

### Late-career transitions, reduced schedules, and retirement income

Late-career compensation planning often shifts from maximizing income to creating flexibility and protecting accumulated wealth. A reduced clinical schedule, part-time arrangement, consulting role, or medical director position may allow you to continue working while reducing call and administrative demands. The right structure depends on your health, goals, retirement assets, and preferred level of patient care.

Some physicians accept lower pay in exchange for greater autonomy or work-life balance. The [Doximity 2025 report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025) found that 77% of physicians said they had accepted, or would accept, lower compensation for those benefits. Before changing schedules, model the effect on retirement contributions, health insurance, professional expenses, and pension benefits. A physician-focused [retirement planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can coordinate reduced earned income with portfolio withdrawals and tax planning.

## How Should You Use Compensation Data?

Compensation reports can help you assess whether a physician offer is competitive, but they should not make the decision for you. A national salary average rarely reflects the details that shape your actual income, including specialty, location, call schedule, productivity expectations, benefits, and ownership opportunities.

Use compensation data as a starting point for research and negotiation. Compare several reputable sources, review each methodology, and adjust the results to match your career stage and work setting. Then evaluate the complete offer, including taxes, benefits, risk, and long-term earning potential. A physician-focused financial planner and an attorney who regularly reviews physician contracts can help you determine how the compensation structure fits your broader financial plan.

### Doximity and Medscape compensation reports

Doximity and Medscape are well-known sources for physician compensation research. Their reports can provide useful information about specialty averages, geographic differences, compensation trends, and factors affecting physician earnings. The [Doximity 2025 Physician Compensation Report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025), for example, examines compensation by specialty and metro area while addressing gender pay differences and reimbursement pressure.

These reports are most useful for identifying broad market patterns. They can help you prepare questions before discussing an offer or requesting a compensation review. However, a national average should not be the sole basis for accepting or rejecting a contract. Use the report to establish a preliminary range, then compare it with data for your specialty, region, practice setting, and expected workload.

### MGMA, AMGA, AMN Healthcare, and specialty surveys

MGMA, AMGA, AMN Healthcare, and specialty-specific organizations offer different perspectives on physician compensation. Each source may focus on a distinct group, such as employed providers, medical group members, or candidates involved in recruitment. Reviewing multiple surveys can help you identify a reasonable range instead of relying on one result.

Pay attention to whether a report includes base salary, total cash compensation, productivity pay, or benefits. These figures are not interchangeable. AMN Healthcare’s physician recruitment data, for example, reflects search engagements and starting offers, while other surveys may report compensation for physicians already established in a role. The [AMA’s review of physician salary trends](https://www.ama-assn.org/medical-residents/transition-resident-attending/are-physician-salaries-going-your-specialty) provides useful context when comparing starting pay across specialties.

### BLS wage data and public sources

The Bureau of Labor Statistics offers a public reference point for physician wages by occupation and location. Its data can help you compare broad pay patterns across states and metropolitan areas, especially if you are considering relocation. You can review occupation and geographic information through the [BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics program](https://www.bls.gov/oes/).

BLS data has limits when you are evaluating a contract. It may group physicians into broad occupational categories and may not capture wRVU formulas, partnership distributions, call burden, or signing incentives. Public sources may also fail to distinguish between academic medicine, private practice, hospital employment, and ownership income. Use BLS information to frame your research, then supplement it with specialty and setting-specific data.

### What AMN data shows about starting pay and demand

AMN Healthcare data can be especially helpful for residents, fellows, and physicians comparing their first attending offers. Its reported sample includes 1,420 physician search engagements conducted from April 1, 2024, through March 31, 2025. The average starting salary across the physicians tracked was $403,000, compared with $406,000 during the prior period.

That figure is a reference point, not a universal benchmark. Starting compensation can vary significantly by specialty, location, call expectations, and recruitment difficulty. A rural hospital may offer a substantial signing package because it has struggled to recruit, while an academic position in a competitive metro area may offer less cash but include teaching, research, or loan repayment benefits. Review AMN findings alongside the [AMA’s discussion of physician recruitment and starting salaries](https://www.ama-assn.org/medical-residents/transition-resident-attending/are-physician-salaries-going-your-specialty).

### Compare survey year, sample size, and methodology

Before comparing two compensation reports, check when the data was collected and how many physicians participated. Recent responses may reflect market changes more accurately than older data, especially when reimbursement, inflation, or recruitment demand has shifted. A larger sample can provide a more stable view, although sample size alone does not guarantee that the results match your situation.

Methodology matters just as much. Determine whether the report measures base salary, total compensation, starting offers, or actual income. The Doximity report used more than 37,000 physician compensation surveys from 2024 and more than 230,000 surveys collected over six years. That depth offers valuable context, but you still need to check whether respondents resemble you in specialty, setting, geography, and workload.

### Match benchmarks to location, setting, experience, and workload

A compensation benchmark becomes more useful when you adjust it for the conditions of the job. Start with location, then consider whether the role is in a single-specialty group, multispecialty group, solo practice, hospital, academic institution, or another setting. Account for years in practice, subspecialty training, leadership duties, clinical hours, call frequency, and patient volume.

For example, Doximity reported average compensation of $476,807 for single-specialty groups, $461,671 for multispecialty groups, and $457,562 for solo practices. Those figures show why setting matters, but they do not tell you whether a specific offer is fair. Compare the benchmark with production expectations, staffing levels, referral support, and the resources available to help you meet those expectations.

### Separate employed, owner, academic, and locum data

Do not compare income from different career models as if it represents the same type of compensation. An employed physician may receive a salary, productivity bonus, benefits, and malpractice coverage. A practice owner may report higher total income, but that figure could include distributions after overhead while also carrying payroll, staffing, equipment, and compliance responsibilities.

Academic compensation may include protected research or teaching time, while locum work often pays a higher hourly rate without the same long-term benefits or stability. Ownership income can also fluctuate with collections and operating expenses. When comparing offers, determine whether the survey measures personal earnings, employer-paid compensation, or practice revenue. The [AMA’s physician contracting guidance](https://www.ama-assn.org/medical-residents/transition-resident-attending/physician-contracting-do-s-and-don-ts-interviews) can help you identify questions for your contract review.

### Use percentiles instead of one national average

A single average can hide a wide range of physician compensation. Percentiles provide more useful context by showing where an offer falls compared with other reported compensation levels. An offer near the 25th percentile may be reasonable for a limited schedule or early-career role, but less competitive for a high-volume position with frequent call.

Ask which percentile applies to your specialty, region, and practice setting. Confirm whether the figures reflect base pay or total compensation. A high percentile does not automatically mean an offer is excessive or inappropriate. As [VMG Health explains in its discussion of survey data and fair market value](https://vmghealth.com/insights/blog/debunking-provider-compensation-myths-correctly-using-survey-data-for-fmv), compensation above the 90th percentile does not automatically exceed fair market value. The full contract and business arrangement still matter.

### Distinguish fair market value from survey benchmarks

Survey data and fair market value are related, but they are not the same. A survey benchmark describes compensation reported by a group of physicians. Fair market value considers the specific services, responsibilities, market conditions, and business arrangement involved in a contract.

This distinction matters for employed physicians, medical directors, practice owners, and arrangements involving hospitals or other healthcare organizations. A compensation plan should connect pay to documented duties and measurable work instead of relying only on a percentile target. Review the wRVU formula, conversion factor, collections assumptions, administrative time, and call responsibilities. Because compensation structures can be complex, consider having an experienced healthcare attorney assess the agreement and related compliance concerns. [NEJM CareerCenter’s physician contract guidance](https://resources.nejmcareercenter.org/article/physician-employment-contracts-strategies-for-avoiding-pitfalls) offers additional questions to consider.

### Account for growth, inflation, and purchasing power

Compensation data should show how earnings change over time, not just what physicians earn in one survey year. Inflation can reduce the practical value of a salary even when the dollar amount increases. Compare compensation growth with changes in housing, insurance, education, childcare, and other expenses in the location where you will live and work.

Also consider how reimbursement changes may affect future income. Medicare payment pressure, payer mix, staffing costs, and procedure volume can influence an employer’s ability to maintain its compensation plan. Doximity has reported physician compensation growth despite reimbursement cuts and periods of high inflation, but that trend does not guarantee that every specialty or employer will follow the same pattern. Model several scenarios, including stable income, reduced productivity, and delayed bonus payments, before treating a compensation figure as dependable cash flow.

## What Causes Gender Disparities in Physician Compensation?

Gender disparities in physician compensation rarely come from one decision or one point in a career. They can develop through specialty selection, clinical workload, contract terms, access to leadership, partnership opportunities, and time away for caregiving. Even when physicians have similar training and experience, differences in how employers measure productivity and distribute revenue can affect total pay.

The gap appears across specialties and employment models. According to the [Doximity 2025 Physician Compensation Report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025), women physicians earned less than men in every specialty examined. After adjusting for specialty, location, and years of experience, women earned an average of $120,917 less than men. These statistics describe an industry-wide pattern, not the value or ability of any individual physician.

Physicians can respond by examining the full compensation structure, documenting their contributions, and asking for objective standards. Employers can help by publishing salary ranges, using consistent formulas, and reviewing pay across comparable roles. A clear compensation process also makes it easier to connect career earnings with [physician-focused wealth management](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians), including taxes, retirement savings, and long-term financial goals.

### The gender pay gap and compensation transparency

The physician gender pay gap widened to 26% in 2024, up from 23% in 2023, according to Doximity. Men’s average compensation increased by 5.7%, while women’s increased by 1.7%. These figures do not explain the circumstances of an individual offer, but they show why physicians should examine compensation beyond the headline salary.

Transparency is especially important when an offer includes wRVUs, collections, conversion factors, quality bonuses, call pay, or clawbacks. Physicians may not know how their terms compare with colleagues in similar specialties, locations, or roles.

Employers can make compensation easier to evaluate by publishing salary ranges, defining incentive formulas, and reviewing pay by specialty, experience, workload, and employment setting. Physicians can request the assumptions behind an offer and compare them with reliable market data before signing.

### Specialty, hours, productivity, and workload differences

Specialty mix affects compensation, but it does not fully explain gender disparities. Surgical specialists earned 87% more than primary care physicians in 2024, according to Doximity. Yet women physicians earned less than men in every specialty included in the report, suggesting that specialty selection alone cannot account for the difference.

Clinical hours and productivity measures can create additional variation. A physician who handles more call, procedures, new patients, or complex cases may generate more measurable revenue. However, production systems can overlook care coordination, inbox management, mentoring, teaching, and uncompensated patient communication.

Physicians should review whether the compensation formula reflects their actual workload. Employers should define how clinical, administrative, quality, and leadership work is measured and paid, especially when those responsibilities affect a physician’s schedule but do not produce wRVUs.

### Leadership, partnership, and revenue opportunities

Access to leadership and ownership opportunities can affect lifetime earnings as much as annual salary. Medical directors, department chairs, service-line leaders, and practice owners may receive administrative pay, equity, profit distributions, or greater influence over future revenue.

When these opportunities are assigned informally, some physicians may have fewer chances to build wealth through ownership or leadership. Partnership tracks also require careful review. A contract may mention a future buy-in without explaining eligibility, valuation, voting rights, distributions, or the timeline for ownership.

Contract negotiation is standard in physician employment. The [American College of Physicians’ contract guidance](https://careersource.im.org/career-resources/on-the-job-3/physician-contract-negotiation-a-practical-guide-for-internal-medicine-physicians-102) emphasizes reviewing compensation structure, non-compete terms, and tail coverage. Physicians should ask how leadership roles are selected, how administrative work is paid, and what measurable steps lead to partnership.

### Parental leave, flexibility, and career interruptions

Parenthood and caregiving can affect compensation when physicians reduce clinical hours, take leave, or choose schedules with fewer call responsibilities. These choices may also affect access to leadership roles, high-revenue cases, partnership opportunities, and future raises. The financial effect can continue after a physician returns to full-time work.

Flexible arrangements can help physicians remain in practice, but flexibility should not automatically mean lower pay. Employers can create fairer systems by defining how part-time work, telemedicine, call coverage, parental leave, and return-to-practice periods affect compensation and advancement.

Career timing also matters. The American Medical Association’s physician contracting guidance notes that specialty demand and physician shortages affect recruiting conditions. Physicians returning from leave or changing schedules should research their current market value and negotiate based on their responsibilities, not assumptions about their availability.

### Use objective measures and consistent pay formulas

A compensation plan is easier to evaluate when it uses consistent, written measures. These may include wRVUs, collections, quality scores, patient access, call coverage, leadership duties, and specific bonus thresholds. The agreement should explain how each measure affects pay and when results are reviewed.

Objective standards do not guarantee fairness if the measures overlook important work. A formula based only on procedure volume, for example, may disadvantage physicians who provide complex cognitive care or spend significant time coordinating treatment. A fair plan should reflect the services a physician performs and the outcomes the organization values.

Employers should apply the same methodology to physicians in comparable roles and investigate unexplained differences. Physicians can ask how targets were set, whether the conversion factor is negotiable, and how quality incentives or clawbacks affect final compensation. The [CompHealth physician contract guide](https://comphealth.com/resources/physician-contract-negotiation) recommends defining the long-term compensation structure clearly in the contract.

### Document your value and negotiate with market data

A strong negotiation starts with evidence. Keep records of clinical volume, collections, wRVUs, quality results, patient access improvements, leadership responsibilities, teaching, recruitment, and other work that supports the practice. Include contributions that may not appear in a production report, such as developing a service line or improving referral relationships.

Use several sources to establish a reasonable range for your specialty and location. The [Weatherby Healthcare contract guidance](https://weatherbyhealthcare.com/blog/physician-contract-negotiation) recommends researching compensation packages for physicians in the same specialty and geographic area. Compare the survey year, percentile, practice setting, workload, and payment methodology before using a figure in a negotiation.

Connect each request to a specific business reason. Explain how your experience, call schedule, clinical production, or leadership duties support a higher salary, conversion factor, bonus, or benefit. This gives both sides a clearer basis for discussion and creates a written record of the value you bring.

### Schedule regular compensation reviews

A physician’s responsibilities can change long before a contract is formally renewed. Patient volume may increase, call coverage may expand, or a physician may take on teaching, quality, recruitment, or administrative work. A compensation review should account for these changes rather than relying on the original offer.

Ask for a regular review schedule, such as an annual assessment, and identify the data the employer will use. The review may include production, collections, quality measures, access, call, leadership, and market benchmarks. It should also explain how changes take effect and whether prior underpayment can be corrected.

Compensation plans often include more than base salary and an incentive bonus. [NEJM CareerCenter’s physician contract resources](https://resources.nejmcareercenter.org/article/physician-employment-contracts-strategies-for-avoiding-pitfalls) notes that methodologies may include quality measures and clawbacks. Regular reviews help physicians identify unclear terms, address inconsistencies, and keep compensation aligned with their actual work.

## What Trends Shape Physician Compensation?

Physician compensation continues to change as healthcare organizations respond to inflation, reimbursement pressure, staffing shortages, consolidation, and new care delivery models. These forces affect more than the salary listed in an employment offer. They can change productivity thresholds, bonus formulas, call schedules, benefits, leadership opportunities, and the amount of financial risk a physician carries.

A compensation package that looks competitive at first glance may produce a different result after accounting for inflation, taxes, required patient volume, unpaid administrative work, and the likelihood of earning incentive pay. Physicians should review how each part of the package works together instead of focusing on guaranteed salary alone.

Market data offers useful context, but national averages do not tell the full story. The [Doximity 2025 Physician Compensation Report](https://www.doximity.com/reports/physician-compensation-report/2025), for example, examines compensation trends, reimbursement pressure, practice ownership, and specialty differences. Use reports like this as a starting point, then compare the findings with data for your specialty, location, practice setting, and career stage.

These trends also have a direct effect on your broader financial plan. Changes in income can influence tax strategy, retirement contributions, insurance needs, investment decisions, and the timing of a practice purchase. A physician-focused planning team can help connect compensation decisions with your long-term goals.

### Inflation-adjusted pay and purchasing power

Average U.S. physician compensation increased 3.7% from 2023 to 2024, according to Doximity. That was slower than the 5.9% increase reported the previous year. A higher salary does not necessarily mean you are financially ahead when housing, insurance, education, childcare, and other household costs are also rising.

Purchasing power varies significantly by location. A salary that supports a comfortable lifestyle in a rural market may feel much tighter in a major metropolitan area. State and local taxes, housing costs, commuting expenses, malpractice premiums, and school tuition can change the value of the same compensation package.

When comparing offers, estimate after-tax cash flow rather than focusing only on the stated increase. Include recurring living expenses, debt payments, retirement savings, and moving costs. A [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can also help you understand how compensation changes may affect your net income and long-term savings capacity.

### Guaranteed salary, productivity, and hybrid pay models

Many physician contracts combine guaranteed salary with productivity incentives. Common formulas may use work relative value units, collections, patient volume, quality scores, or a blend of these measures. Some agreements also include signing bonuses, retention payments, quality incentives, and repayment provisions.

A hybrid model can provide income stability while rewarding higher production, but the details matter. Ask when the productivity formula begins, whether the threshold is prorated, how extended leave affects the calculation, and whether the organization can change the formula. Confirm who controls scheduling, staffing, referrals, and billing, since each factor can affect your ability to produce.

The [NEJM CareerCenter’s guidance on physician employment contracts](https://resources.nejmcareercenter.org/article/physician-employment-contracts-strategies-for-avoiding-pitfalls) recommends reviewing compensation methodologies carefully. Before signing, request examples showing what you would earn at different production levels. These examples can reveal whether the incentive is realistic and how much downside risk exists if patient volume falls short.

### Value-based care and quality incentives

Value-based care places greater emphasis on outcomes, access, patient experience, care coordination, and cost management. As a result, more contracts include incentives tied to quality measures rather than procedure volume alone. These measures may include readmission rates, preventive care, patient satisfaction, documentation, or performance within a specific payer program.

Quality incentives can be meaningful, but they are not always fully within a physician’s control. Results may depend on staffing, patient complexity, referral patterns, technology, social factors, and the organization’s systems. Ask whether the practice adjusts for patient risk and whether targets apply to you individually, your department, or the entire organization.

The [American Academy of Family Physicians explains physician contract considerations](https://www.aafp.org/life-and-career/employment-contracting/negotiating-physician-contracts) related to value-based payment models. Review the exact metrics, payment timing, historical payout rates, and any provision that allows the employer to revise the measures. Treat a bonus as variable income unless the contract clearly guarantees it.

### Telemedicine, administrative work, and clinical leadership

Telemedicine can expand patient access and create scheduling flexibility, but the compensation model may differ from in-person care. A contract may pay by encounter, hour, wRVU, panel size, or a blended formula. Clarify whether virtual visits receive the same credit as comparable office visits and who handles technology, documentation, licensing, and patient follow-up.

Administrative and leadership responsibilities also deserve specific compensation. Medical directors, department chairs, quality leaders, and physicians managing teams may spend substantial time in meetings, chart review, protocol development, recruiting, and compliance work. If those hours reduce clinical availability, a production-based formula may not reflect the full scope of the role.

Benefits and schedule flexibility can have real financial value. [Weatherby Healthcare’s contract negotiation guidance](https://weatherbyhealthcare.com/blog/physician-contract-negotiation) highlights the importance of reviewing leave, health coverage, retirement benefits, and other non-salary terms. Ask whether leadership time is paid separately, protected on the schedule, or simply added to your existing workload.

### Reimbursement changes and Medicare pressure

Reimbursement trends can affect physician compensation even when a contract does not change immediately. Doximity reports that Medicare physician payment fell 33% from 2001 through 2024 after adjusting for inflation. Organizations facing lower reimbursement may respond by changing productivity thresholds, reducing staffing, delaying hiring, or placing more emphasis on outpatient volume.

The effect varies by specialty and payer mix. A practice that depends heavily on Medicare may face different pressures than one with a larger share of commercial insurance, self-pay, or specialty procedures. Review how the organization calculates collections and whether payer-specific rates affect your incentive compensation.

Ask how often the employer reviews the compensation formula and what happens if reimbursement rules change. You should also understand whether the contract permits unilateral changes to the payment model. A change in reimbursement can affect your income, savings rate, retirement contributions, and ability to meet major financial goals, making [wealth management for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) an important part of long-term planning.

### Consolidation, physician demand, and changing offers

Healthcare consolidation continues to reshape employment options for physicians. Hospitals, health systems, private equity groups, and larger medical organizations may offer more resources, broader referral networks, and administrative support. They may also use standardized contracts and centralized compensation formulas that leave less room for customization.

Doximity reports that the share of physicians working in private practice declined from 60.1% in 2012 to 42.2% in 2024. The report also found that nearly 38% of physicians said reimbursement concerns caused their organization to delay or cancel hiring or service expansion plans. These conditions can affect staffing, call coverage, equipment purchases, and the long-term stability of an offer.

Do not assume that a large organization automatically provides greater security or that private practice automatically offers greater upside. Compare financial condition, turnover, staffing, governance, and the history of compensation changes. If you are considering ownership, review financial statements, overhead, debt, and distribution history before treating future equity as part of your compensation.

### Compensation transparency, equity, and governance

Compensation transparency is becoming more important as physicians seek clearer information about pay formulas, benchmarks, and advancement opportunities. Clear policies can help physicians identify inconsistent treatment, understand incentive calculations, and prepare for productive compensation discussions.

Doximity reported that the physician gender pay gap widened to 26% in 2024, compared with 23% in 2023. After adjusting for specialty, location, and years of experience, women physicians earned an average of $120,917 less than men. Differences can accumulate through lower retirement contributions, smaller bonuses, reduced ownership opportunities, and slower wealth accumulation.

Organizations can address these concerns with consistent formulas, documented performance measures, regular compensation reviews, and clear promotion criteria. Physicians should keep records of clinical production, leadership work, teaching, research, patient access, and quality contributions. If a committee or governance group sets compensation, ask who participates, what data they review, and how you can challenge an inaccurate evaluation.

### Specialty shortages, recruitment competition, and starting pay

Recruitment demand can influence starting salaries, signing bonuses, call arrangements, relocation assistance, and loan repayment. According to Doximity, the most recruited specialties included internal medicine, family medicine, psychiatry, pediatrics, and obstetrics and gynecology. Primary care specialties represented three of the four most in-demand fields.

A shortage does not guarantee that every physician receives an exceptional offer. Demand may be strongest in a particular county, hospital service line, or patient population. The organization may also have limited revenue, inadequate staffing, or strict compensation policies. Still, strong recruitment needs can provide useful leverage when several employers are competing for the same physician.

Residents and fellows should compare more than the headline signing bonus. Review the guaranteed salary period, productivity ramp, call schedule, relocation repayment terms, loan assistance, malpractice coverage, and timing of the first compensation review. Established physicians should compare the complete package with their current opportunity cost, including retirement benefits, referral sources, schedule flexibility, and ownership potential.

## How Can You Evaluate and Negotiate a Fair Offer?

A fair physician offer involves more than comparing base salaries. You need to understand how compensation is calculated, how much control you have over your schedule, which benefits are included, and what financial risks come with the agreement. An offer with a high salary may be less attractive if it includes demanding call obligations, an unrealistic productivity threshold, expensive tail coverage, or limited paid time off.

Request the complete employment agreement and compensation documents before negotiating. Ask the employer to explain unfamiliar terms, calculations, and assumptions in plain language. Then compare the offer with compensation data for your specialty, location, experience level, and practice setting. [Weatherby Healthcare’s contract negotiation guidance](https://weatherbyhealthcare.com/blog/physician-contract-negotiation) recommends researching comparable physician compensation before beginning discussions.

It can also help to review the offer alongside your broader financial plan. A signing bonus, partnership opportunity, or relocation package may affect taxes, retirement contributions, insurance needs, and cash flow. ### Build a market-based baseline from multiple sources

Begin with several compensation benchmarks instead of relying on one salary report or recruiter estimate. Compare figures for your specialty, geographic area, experience, practice setting, and expected workload. An employed cardiologist in a large health system may receive a very different package from a private-practice cardiologist with an ownership path, even within the same state.

Review median and percentile figures, then adjust for call, procedures, patient volume, clinical hours, and administrative responsibilities. Public data, specialty surveys, and physician recruitment reports can provide useful context, but their survey years and methods may differ. Use the information to establish a reasonable range, not one supposedly correct salary.

A market baseline also helps you identify whether the offer reflects the work you are expected to perform. If the role includes teaching, leadership, supervision, or outreach clinics, compare it with positions carrying similar responsibilities.

### Set target, minimum, and negotiation priorities

Before responding to an employer, define three points: your preferred outcome, your minimum acceptable terms, and the items you are willing to trade. This keeps the discussion focused and reduces the chance that you will make a decision based only on the headline salary.

Your priorities may include base pay, a lower productivity threshold, a specific call schedule, additional paid time off, or an ownership timeline. If burnout or family responsibilities are concerns, scheduling flexibility and limits on administrative work may matter more than a modest salary increase. The [AAFP’s physician contract guidance](https://www.aafp.org/life-and-career/employment-contracting/negotiating-physician-contracts) recommends identifying your most important goals before negotiating.

Write your priorities down and rank them. You may accept a smaller signing bonus in exchange for better call coverage, or trade a higher base salary for a shorter initial contract term. Knowing what matters most gives you flexibility while keeping the negotiation aligned with your professional and personal goals.

### Request the full compensation formula and assumptions

Ask for the complete compensation formula in writing before evaluating the offer. It should explain how base salary, productivity pay, quality incentives, collections, call pay, leadership stipends, and other bonuses are calculated. Request the assumptions behind the offer, including expected annual work relative value units, patient volume, payer mix, and ramp-up time.

Pay attention to when incentives begin and whether they are paid monthly, quarterly, or annually. Ask what happens if you take parental leave, become ill, reduce your schedule, or start partway through a measurement period. Clarify whether compensation depends on individual performance, department performance, or both.

A clear formula lets you estimate potential earnings instead of treating variable compensation as guaranteed income. [CompHealth’s contract negotiation guide](https://comphealth.com/resources/physician-contract-negotiation) emphasizes the importance of defining long-term compensation in the agreement. If the formula is difficult to follow, request a sample calculation using realistic production figures.

### Analyze wRVU thresholds, collections, bonuses, and conversion factors

If the offer includes productivity pay, identify the exact wRVU threshold and conversion factor used to calculate compensation. Ask how the practice determines wRVUs, whether the benchmark can change, and whether the threshold is adjusted for part-time work, leave, or a midyear start.

Understand the relationship between billed services, collected revenue, and your compensation. A collections-based model may depend on payer mix, billing practices, denial rates, and the time required for insurers or patients to pay. Ask whether you receive credit for services billed under another clinician, performed by advanced practice providers, or affected by global surgical periods.

Review bonus conditions carefully. Some plans include quality scores, patient satisfaction measures, documentation requirements, or departmental targets. Others require repayment if you leave before a specified date. The [NEJM CareerCenter’s contract resource](https://resources.nejmcareercenter.org/article/physician-employment-contracts-strategies-for-avoiding-pitfalls) explains why physician compensation formulas often contain several components beyond base salary.

### Compare pay, benefits, incentives, and downside risk

Evaluate the entire package, not just the guaranteed salary. Include health insurance, disability coverage, retirement contributions, paid time off, CME funding, professional dues, malpractice insurance, relocation assistance, and loan repayment. These benefits can significantly affect your total financial value.

Then consider the downside risk. Is the salary guaranteed for the full contract term, or can the employer reduce it after an introductory period? Can the productivity threshold increase? Are bonuses discretionary? Could you owe back a signing bonus, relocation payment, or educational benefit if you leave?

A lower salary may still be competitive if it includes strong retirement contributions, reliable bonus terms, disability coverage, and paid tail insurance. A higher salary may not compensate for heavy call or substantial repayment obligations. Model both expected income and a conservative scenario before deciding. ### Negotiate call, schedule, PTO, CME, relocation, and loan repayment

Compensation discussions should include the practical terms that shape your workweek. Ask how many call shifts you will cover, whether weekend and holiday call is separate, and how additional coverage is paid. Clarify response expectations, backup coverage, rounding duties, and the process for changing the schedule.

Review paid time off, CME days, CME funding, professional memberships, and conference travel. Confirm whether PTO and CME time are separate or combined, and ask how unused time is handled. If you are relocating, negotiate moving expenses, temporary housing, licensing fees, and repayment conditions.

Loan repayment can be especially valuable early in your attending career. Ask whether the benefit is paid directly to your lender, whether it is taxable, and whether you must remain employed for a specific period. Support each request with a clear reason, such as local relocation costs, additional call responsibilities, or the clinical value you bring to the practice.

### Review malpractice, tail coverage, restrictive covenants, and termination terms

Review malpractice coverage carefully. Determine whether the policy is claims-made or occurrence-based, who pays the premiums, and who is responsible for tail coverage when employment ends. Tail insurance can be expensive, so it should not be treated as a minor contract detail.

Read restrictive covenants closely. Look at the geographic area, duration, covered services, and whether the restriction applies if the employer terminates you without cause. Also review nonsolicitation, confidentiality, intellectual property, and patient-record provisions. These terms may affect where you can work next and whether you can continue serving established patients.

Termination language should address notice periods, termination without cause, termination for cause, bonus eligibility, benefit continuation, and repayment obligations. Ask what happens to unpaid compensation after termination. A health care attorney should review these provisions because their impact depends on the contract language and applicable state law.

### Assess partnership tracks, equity, buy-ins, and distributions

An ownership opportunity can be valuable, but a “partnership track” does not guarantee future equity. Ask when you become eligible, how the decision is made, and whether the terms are documented in a separate agreement. Request financial information about the practice, including revenue, expenses, debt, distributions, and outstanding liabilities.

For a buy-in, clarify what you are purchasing and how the price is determined. The agreement should explain voting rights, ownership percentages, capital contributions, distributions, and what happens if you leave. Ask whether partners receive income from ancillary services, real estate, or other business lines.

Separate income from clinical work from income generated through ownership. A practice owner may have greater long-term earning potential, but also takes on overhead, payroll, compliance, equipment, and capital risks. Elite Physician Wealth Planning’s [practice owner planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning) helps medical professionals coordinate these decisions with business cash flow, tax planning, and a future exit strategy.

### Support requests with documented value

A negotiation is stronger when each request connects to a measurable contribution or specific market condition. Prepare a concise summary of your training, certifications, procedural skills, referral relationships, leadership experience, research, teaching, and expected clinical responsibilities.

Use objective support whenever possible. You might reference additional call shifts, higher patient volume, an underserved location, a new service line, or procedures you can perform that the practice currently sends elsewhere. If you are requesting a higher conversion factor, explain how your production or collections history supports the request.

Avoid presenting a request as a personal preference without context. Instead, explain the business reason and propose a workable term. [CompHealth recommends providing a clear justification](https://comphealth.com/resources/physician-contract-negotiation) for each request. A concise, professional explanation gives the employer useful information and makes your proposal easier to evaluate.

### Put agreements in writing and seek legal review

Do not rely on verbal promises about future compensation, partnership, schedule changes, or bonus eligibility. Ask for every agreed change to appear in the contract or an executed addendum. Before signing, confirm that the final document matches the term sheet, email discussions, and negotiation notes.

Have an attorney who regularly reviews physician employment agreements examine the contract. Legal review is especially important for restrictive covenants, malpractice and tail coverage, termination provisions, repayment clauses, intellectual property, and ownership agreements. The [NEJM CareerCenter recommends legal review](https://resources.nejmcareercenter.org/article/physician-employment-contracts-strategies-for-avoiding-pitfalls) because physician compensation structures and contract obligations can be complex.

Give yourself enough time to ask questions and request revisions. You do not need to address every issue in one conversation. A careful review can identify terms that affect your income, professional mobility, and financial obligations well beyond the first year of employment.

## How Does Compensation Support Wealth Planning?

Physician compensation is more than the salary shown on an offer letter. Base pay, productivity formulas, bonuses, benefits, equity, retirement contributions, and ownership opportunities all affect how much you can save, invest, protect, and eventually pass on to your family. An offer that looks attractive on paper may provide less usable income after taxes, professional expenses, loan payments, and lifestyle costs.

A wealth plan helps connect those moving parts. Instead of evaluating compensation only by its gross annual amount, you can compare offers based on after-tax cash flow, long-term earning potential, benefits, risk, and flexibility. This is especially important when part of your compensation depends on work relative value units (wRVUs), collections, quality metrics, or discretionary bonuses.

Before signing, review the contract with the professionals who can evaluate its legal, tax, and financial consequences. The [AAFP’s physician contract negotiation guidance](https://www.aafp.org/life-and-career/employment-contracting/negotiating-physician-contracts) recommends understanding the tax implications of a salary offer and researching compensation averages in your specialty and location. Those details can help you make better decisions about savings, debt repayment, investing, insurance, and future career moves.

### Model gross compensation after taxes

A six-figure salary does not tell you how much money will reach your bank account. Federal and state income taxes, payroll taxes, retirement contributions, health insurance premiums, student loan payments, and professional expenses can reduce available cash flow. Modeling compensation after taxes gives you a more realistic basis for comparing employment offers.

Ask for projections that include base salary, expected productivity pay, signing or retention bonuses, and employer benefits. Review conservative, expected, and optimistic scenarios if part of your income depends on wRVUs, collections, or discretionary bonuses. A financial planner can help translate those projections into a spending, savings, and debt repayment plan.

### Coordinate bonuses, equity, and deferred compensation with tax strategy

Bonuses, restricted equity, partnership distributions, and deferred compensation can create valuable opportunities, but each may involve different tax rules and timing. A large bonus could increase your taxable income, while equity may create a tax obligation before you receive cash. Deferred compensation may also involve vesting schedules, distribution elections, and employer-credit risk.

Review these details before accepting the offer. The [NEJM CareerCenter’s physician contract guidance](https://resources.nejmcareercenter.org/article/physician-employment-contracts-strategies-for-avoiding-pitfalls) notes that compensation structures are becoming more complex and may require legal review. Your tax strategy should account for when compensation is earned, when it is taxed, and how it fits with deductions, charitable giving, and retirement contributions.

### Evaluate retirement contributions and employer benefits

Retirement benefits can add significant value to a physician compensation package. Compare the employer match, profit-sharing contributions, plan limits, vesting rules, and availability of supplemental plans. Depending on the employer, a 401(k), 403(b), cash balance plan, or 457(b) may provide different savings and tax-planning opportunities.

Also review health insurance, disability coverage, life insurance, paid time off, CME funding, relocation assistance, and student loan repayment. Benefits can reduce your household expenses and the amount you need to save independently. [Weatherby Healthcare’s contract negotiation guidance](https://weatherbyhealthcare.com/blog/physician-contract-negotiation) recommends confirming how benefits are structured and when they become available. Include each item when calculating total compensation.

### Connect practice income, overhead, buy-ins, and ownership

For practice owners and physicians considering partnership, personal compensation cannot be separated from practice economics. Revenue, staffing, equipment, rent, billing, insurance, and other overhead determine how much income the practice can distribute. Strong collections may not translate into dependable personal cash flow if expenses are rising or accounts receivable are delayed.

A buy-in also deserves careful analysis. Review the purchase price, valuation method, financing terms, ownership rights, distribution policy, and expected return on investment. Compare projected owner income with what you could earn as an employee. [White Coat Investor’s analysis of physician contract negotiations](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/anatomy-of-a-complex-physician-contract-negotiation) discusses how efficiency, new locations, expanded hours, and outside funding can affect practice value.

### Plan for compensation changes during career transitions

Physician income often changes during major career decisions. You may move from residency to your first attending role, change specialties, accept a leadership position, reduce clinical hours, join a partnership, or transition to locum tenens work. Each choice can affect taxes, benefits, insurance, debt payments, and retirement savings.

Build a transition plan before the new compensation begins. Estimate one-time costs, maintain an appropriate cash reserve, and decide how to handle insurance and retirement contributions during any employment gap. Starting negotiations early gives you more time to compare offers and address unresolved terms. [Michael Johnson Legal recommends beginning compensation discussions well before a physician’s intended start date](https://www.michaeljohnsonlegal.com/are-you-prepared-for-compensation-discussions).

### Integrate physician income with investing and cash flow

A high income creates meaningful planning opportunities, but consistent cash flow decisions determine how much of that income becomes wealth. Separate fixed obligations from flexible spending, then assign each pay period to taxes, debt repayment, emergency savings, retirement accounts, and taxable investments. Treat variable compensation cautiously until you understand how reliable it is.

Your investment strategy should reflect your career stage, liquidity needs, time horizon, and risk tolerance. A resident may focus on building reserves and repaying high-interest debt, while an established physician may prioritize tax-efficient investing, education funding, and financial independence. Comparing your offer with [specialty and geographic compensation data](https://weatherbyhealthcare.com/blog/physician-contract-negotiation) can help you set a savings rate that reflects your earning potential.

### Protect wealth with insurance and risk planning

Your ability to earn may be one of your most valuable assets, particularly if you have substantial education debt, dependents, or a practice loan. Review employer-provided disability coverage carefully, including the definition of disability, benefit limits, exclusions, waiting period, and whether the policy is portable when you change jobs.

Life insurance, umbrella liability coverage, business insurance, and malpractice protection may also be important. Pay close attention to who pays for malpractice coverage and whether tail coverage applies after employment ends. [CompHealth’s physician contract guide](https://comphealth.com/resources/physician-contract-negotiation) recommends defining long-term compensation and contract terms clearly. Your risk plan should address both household finances and professional responsibilities.

### Connect career earnings to estate and legacy planning

Your compensation can support more than your own retirement. Over a long career, it may help fund education, charitable giving, family support, and a lasting legacy. Estate planning should reflect how your assets are titled, who depends on your income, and what you want to happen if you die or become unable to manage your affairs.

Review beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and insurance policies alongside your will and trust documents. Business ownership may require a succession strategy, valuation terms, and instructions for transferring or selling your interest. Career earnings can also create estate tax considerations, so coordinate investing, insurance, gifting, and estate decisions. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**What should I consider besides a physician’s base salary?**\
Review the entire compensation package, including productivity pay, call stipends, quality bonuses, retirement contributions, insurance, paid leave, malpractice and tail coverage, loan repayment, relocation assistance, and partnership opportunities. Separate guaranteed income from amounts that depend on production or other conditions.

**How can I tell whether a physician’s compensation offer is competitive?**\
Compare the offer with current data for your specialty, location, practice setting, experience level, schedule, and workload. Look at the survey methodology and whether the figures represent base salary, total cash compensation, or starting pay. Use multiple benchmarks as a reference, then assess the contract’s benefits, risks, and earning potential.

**What questions should I ask about a wRVU or productivity-based plan?**\
Request the full formula, including the production threshold, conversion factor, attribution rules, payment schedule, and bonus examples. Ask whether the target can change, how leave affects the calculation, and whether staffing, referrals, scheduling, or payer mix could limit your ability to meet it. Regular access to production reports is also important.

**Which physician contract terms deserve the closest review?**\
Pay close attention to termination rights, repayment clauses, restrictive covenants, malpractice and tail coverage, call obligations, bonus conditions, benefit eligibility, and partnership terms. If the offer includes ownership, review the buy-in, valuation method, distributions, voting rights, and exit provisions before treating equity as part of your compensation.

**How does physician compensation fit into financial planning?**\
Your compensation affects taxes, retirement savings, insurance needs, debt repayment, investment decisions, and estate planning. Modeling after-tax income under conservative and expected scenarios can show how much cash you can reliably save. A physician-focused financial planning team can help coordinate these decisions with your employment agreement and long-term goals.

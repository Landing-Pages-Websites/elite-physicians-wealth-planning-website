A contribution made in March and a conversion completed in April might sound like one simple transaction. For tax purposes, however, they can fall into different years. That distinction matters for physicians who are coordinating IRA contributions with demanding work schedules, multiple retirement accounts, and changing income. A backdoor Roth IRA may help high earners add Roth savings, but the pro-rata rule and reporting requirements deserve careful attention. If you’re researching how physicians can do a backdoor Roth IRA, start by checking your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances and gathering past tax forms. We’ll explain the steps, timing, and tax considerations to review before you act.

## Key Takeaways

* **Review every IRA before converting**: Traditional, rollover, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances can affect the taxable share of a Roth conversion.
* **Keep contribution and conversion records**: Track the tax year and transaction date for each step, then report the activity and after-tax basis on Form 8606.
* **Estimate the tax cost before acting**: Weigh potential Roth benefits against your income, cash needs, workplace or practice plans, and long-term goals.

## What Is a Backdoor Roth IRA, and Why Do Physicians Use It?

A backdoor Roth IRA is a strategy that may allow a high-income physician to put money in a Roth IRA when they can’t contribute directly. It involves making a nondeductible contribution to a traditional IRA, then converting that money to a Roth IRA. Before starting, review your other IRA balances, since they can affect the tax cost.

### Contribute to a traditional IRA on a nondeductible basis, then convert it to a Roth IRA

A backdoor Roth IRA begins with a contribution to a traditional IRA. You make the contribution with after-tax dollars and don’t claim it as a tax deduction. Then, you convert the contribution to a Roth IRA. The conversion may be taxable if the account has investment gains or if you hold other pre-tax funds in traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRAs. The IRS uses a pro-rata calculation to determine the taxable share, so converting only the after-tax contribution may not mean the entire conversion is tax-free. You’ll also need to report the nondeductible contribution and conversion on your tax return, typically using Form 8606. The [IRS guidance on individual retirement arrangements](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/individual-retirement-arrangements-iras) explains key IRA rules.

### Consider potential benefits, including tax-free qualified withdrawals

Roth IRA investments can grow tax-free, and qualified withdrawals are generally tax-free. Roth IRAs also don’t require the original account owner to take required minimum distributions during their lifetime. These features may appeal to physicians who expect to have substantial retirement income or want more flexibility in deciding when to withdraw funds. Roth assets may also support estate-planning goals, although beneficiaries must follow applicable distribution rules. The benefits depend on your situation: taxes due when converting, your current and future tax rates, and how long the money can remain invested all matter. A backdoor Roth isn’t automatically the best choice for every physician. [Empower’s overview of the strategy](https://www.empower.com/the-currency/money/backdoor-roth-ira-good-move) reviews potential benefits and trade-offs.

### See how the strategy can help physicians exceed direct Roth IRA income limits

Physicians may find that attending compensation, bonuses, or practice income puts their household above the income range for direct Roth IRA contributions. A backdoor Roth can offer another path to Roth IRA savings: income limits that apply to direct contributions generally don’t apply to Roth conversions. That distinction can make the strategy worth considering for high-earning physicians. However, a conversion can add taxable income, particularly if you have pre-tax IRA funds. Review the tax impact alongside your workplace retirement plan contributions, practice income, and other savings goals. [SDT Planning’s article for physicians](https://www.sdtplanning.com/blog/why-doctors-should-consider-a-backdoor-roth-ira) explains why some doctors use this approach.

### Separate Roth contribution limits from conversion income limits

A backdoor Roth doesn’t allow you to contribute more than the annual IRA limit. Traditional and Roth IRA contributions share that limit, and you also need eligible compensation. The limit can change each year, so check the [IRS’s current IRA contribution limits](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-ira-contribution-limits) when planning a contribution. A conversion is different: it moves money already held in a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA, so it doesn’t count as a new contribution. There’s generally no income limit on conversions, but the converted amount may be taxable. Keeping contributions and conversions distinct can help you stay within the annual limit while planning for any tax bill.

## Who Can Make a Backdoor Roth IRA Contribution?

A backdoor Roth IRA may be an option for physicians whose income is too high to contribute directly to a Roth IRA. Before using the strategy, check that you have eligible compensation, stay within the annual contribution limit, and understand which tax year applies to each transaction. Your other traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRA balances can also affect the tax consequences, so eligibility is only one part of the decision.

### Confirm eligible compensation and the annual IRA contribution limit

To contribute to an IRA, you generally need eligible compensation, such as wages, salary, or net earnings from self-employment. Income from investments, pensions, or rental property generally does not count as compensation for this purpose. Your combined contributions to traditional and Roth IRAs cannot exceed the annual limit or your eligible compensation, whichever is lower.

For 2026, the IRA contribution limit is $7,500, plus a $1,100 catch-up contribution if you are age 50 or older by the end of the year. The limit applies across your traditional and Roth IRAs, not separately to each account. Since limits can change, check the IRS’s [IRA contribution rules](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-ira-contribution-limits) before contributing.

### Review traditional IRA deduction rules and Roth IRA income limits

There is no income limit for contributing to a traditional IRA. However, whether you can deduct that contribution may depend on your income, filing status, and whether you or your spouse are covered by a workplace retirement plan. A backdoor Roth strategy typically begins with a nondeductible contribution, which you report to the IRS so your after-tax basis is documented.

Direct Roth IRA contributions have income limits that vary by filing status and modified adjusted gross income. Check the IRS’s [Roth IRA guidance](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/roth-iras) for the current thresholds. These limits apply to direct contributions, not Roth conversions. Keep in mind that converting pre-tax IRA money may still create taxable income, even when your income is too high for a direct Roth contribution.

### Check age-based limits and spousal IRA eligibility

You can contribute to a traditional IRA at any age if you have eligible compensation. Roth IRA contributions also have no maximum age limit, though income limits apply to direct contributions. If you are 50 or older by the end of the tax year, you may qualify for an additional catch-up contribution within the annual limit.

A spouse with little or no compensation may be eligible to contribute to an IRA based on the working spouse’s compensation, provided you file a joint federal tax return and have enough eligible compensation to cover both contributions. Each spouse must have an IRA in their own name, and your combined contributions must stay within the applicable limits. A spousal contribution does not change the Roth income limits for direct contributions.

### Identify the contribution tax year and deadline

When you make an IRA contribution, specify the tax year it applies to. You can generally contribute for a tax year until that year’s federal income tax filing deadline, typically in April. For example, a contribution made in the following calendar year may count toward the prior year if you designate it correctly and meet the deadline. Check the IRS’s [contribution limit and deadline guidance](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-ira-contribution-limits) for the applicable year.

The contribution deadline and conversion timing are separate. A Roth conversion is reported for the calendar year in which the conversion occurs. If you contribute for one tax year and convert the money the next year, you will report the contribution and conversion on different tax-year returns.

### Track contribution and conversion years when they differ

Keep a record of the tax year assigned to each contribution and the date of each conversion. For instance, you might contribute in March and designate the deposit for the previous tax year, then convert the funds later that spring. The contribution is reported for the year you selected, while the conversion is reported for the calendar year it took place.

This distinction matters when you file Form 8606, which reports nondeductible contributions and calculates the taxable portion of a Roth conversion. Save confirmation statements from your IRA custodian and note both the contribution year and conversion date. The IRS [Form 8606 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report transactions that fall in different tax years.

## What Should Physicians Check Before Starting?

Before making a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution or converting it to a Roth IRA, take inventory of your retirement accounts and tax records. Existing IRA balances can affect how much of a conversion is taxable, even if you intend to convert only your recent after-tax contribution. The IRS generally considers your traditional, rollover, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances together when calculating the taxable portion.

This review is especially important for physicians with multiple employers, practice income, or retirement plans from different stages of their careers. A rollover IRA from a former employer or a SEP IRA connected to practice income may affect the calculation. Workplace plans, such as 401(k)s and 403(b)s, are generally treated separately, but their rollover rules may offer an option worth discussing.

Gather account statements, contribution records, and prior Forms 8606 before you act. Confirm any transfer or conversion details with your custodian, and consult a tax professional if your account history is unclear. Coordinating the decision with your broader [tax planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you understand how it fits with your income, practice, and retirement plans.

### Review traditional, rollover, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances

List every traditional, rollover, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA you own, including accounts held at different custodians. For the pro-rata calculation, these balances are generally considered together. A rollover IRA from a former employer counts, as do SEP and SIMPLE IRA assets. You can’t avoid the calculation by converting from just one account.

Review the source of the money in each account, not only its current value. Records can help identify pre-tax funds and nondeductible contributions, also known as after-tax basis. The IRS explains how to report IRA basis and distributions in its [Form 8606 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606). If you can’t determine how part of an account should be treated, ask a tax professional to review it before converting.

### Include IRAs tied to practice income or a former employer’s plan

Physicians may have SEP or SIMPLE IRAs connected to practice income, as well as rollover IRAs created after leaving a hospital, group practice, or other employer. Include these accounts in your review, even if they’re inactive or held at another financial institution. Their balances may affect the pro-rata calculation.

Ask each custodian or plan administrator for current statements and details about the account’s funding history. If you own a practice, check how the business retirement plan is structured and where employer contributions are held. Keep in mind that a workplace plan and an IRA are different account types, even when both came from a former employer. Mapping out your accounts before contributing or converting can help you identify which balances matter and which records you still need.

### Check whether a 401(k) or 403(b) accepts eligible pre-tax IRA rollovers

Ask your 401(k) or 403(b) plan administrator whether the plan accepts eligible pre-tax funds from an IRA. Some plans allow these rollovers, while others don’t. Moving eligible pre-tax IRA funds into a workplace plan may reduce the pre-tax IRA balance included in the pro-rata calculation, but the transfer must be handled and reported correctly.

Confirm which types of funds the plan accepts, what documents it requires, and when the rollover must be completed. Don’t assume the plan will accept every IRA asset. The IRS [rollover chart](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-tege/rollover_chart.pdf) offers general guidance on which retirement funds may be rolled into different plans. Your plan’s rules and your account’s tax history also matter, so review both with the administrator and, if needed, a tax professional before initiating a transfer.

### Confirm plan rules and the SIMPLE IRA two-year rollover restriction

SIMPLE IRAs have a special two-year rule. During the first two years after you begin participating in a SIMPLE IRA plan, a rollover generally must go to another SIMPLE IRA to avoid the additional tax that can apply to an early distribution. Once the two-year period has passed, other rollover options may be available, subject to the rules for the receiving account and the transaction.

Check when you first participated in the plan, rather than relying only on when the account was opened or funded. Ask the custodian or plan administrator how the restriction applies before moving or converting the money. The IRS provides details in its [SIMPLE IRA guidance](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plans-faqs-regarding-simple-ira-plans). If you’re unsure about the start date or rollover treatment, get tax advice before taking action.

### Gather account statements, contribution records, and prior Forms 8606

Collect year-end statements for all your IRAs, records of contributions and conversions, and copies of relevant tax returns. Pay particular attention to prior Forms 8606. This form tracks nondeductible contributions and any after-tax basis carried forward from earlier years. If you made nondeductible contributions but can’t find the forms, don’t assume the basis was reported correctly.

A tax preparer may need to review past returns and supporting documents to determine what was reported or whether a correction is needed. Keep confirmations for contributions and conversions, along with year-end account values. These records help support accurate reporting and can help prevent paying tax again on after-tax dollars. The IRS [Form 8606 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report nondeductible contributions and Roth conversions.

### Check the process with custodian instructions, IRS guidance, tax software, or a tax professional

Contact your IRA custodian before moving money. Ask how to make a nondeductible contribution and request a Roth conversion, then confirm any forms, processing times, transfer methods, and transaction reporting. Make sure the contribution is assigned to the intended tax year, and save confirmation of each step.

IRS guidance and tax software can help with general reporting, but they may not resolve gaps in your records or unusual account histories. A tax professional can review the pro-rata calculation, SIMPLE IRA timing, and possible workplace-plan rollovers before you proceed. ## How Do Physicians Complete a Backdoor Roth IRA?

A backdoor Roth IRA generally involves contributing after-tax money to a traditional IRA, then converting it to a Roth IRA. The process has a few steps, and existing IRA balances can affect how much of a conversion is taxable. Before moving money, review your accounts, confirm the tax year for your contribution, and keep records of each transaction.

### Open traditional and Roth IRAs, if needed

If you do not already have the accounts, open a traditional IRA and a Roth IRA with a custodian that allows Roth conversions. Keeping both accounts at the same institution may simplify the transfer, but check the custodian’s process and timing first.

Before contributing, review your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances. These accounts are generally combined when calculating the taxable portion of a Roth conversion, so pre-tax money in any of them may affect your tax bill. Workplace plans such as 401(k)s and 403(b)s are treated separately for this calculation. Vanguard’s guide to [setting up a backdoor Roth IRA](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/article/how-to-set-up-backdoor-ira) offers an overview of the account setup and balance considerations.

### Make a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution and designate its tax year

Contribute to your traditional IRA with after-tax money, and make sure you do not claim a deduction for that contribution. When making the deposit, tell your custodian which tax year it applies to. This is especially important when contributing early in the year or near the tax-filing deadline, when a contribution may be assigned to the previous year.

Your contribution must meet the annual IRA limit and be supported by eligible compensation. The contribution deadline and conversion date are separate: you may be able to make a prior-year contribution after that year ends, but a conversion is reported for the calendar year in which it happens. [Fidelity’s overview of the backdoor Roth process](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/personal-finance/backdoor-roth-ira) explains how the nondeductible contribution fits into the strategy. Save the deposit confirmation showing its amount and tax year.

### Convert the contribution to a Roth IRA, generally without withholding taxes

After the contribution settles, request a conversion from your traditional IRA to your Roth IRA. Your custodian may offer an online transfer or require a form. Ask about processing times and confirm the date the conversion is completed, since that date determines the tax year in which it is reported.

You can generally elect not to have taxes withheld from the conversion. If the account earned interest or investment gains before the transfer, those amounts may be taxable when converted. Pre-tax IRA funds can also make part of the conversion taxable under the pro-rata rule. Paying any taxes due from non-IRA funds may leave more money in the Roth IRA to invest, but plan for the tax cost before initiating the conversion.

### Invest the Roth IRA funds and save transaction confirmations

A conversion transfers money into your Roth IRA, but it does not automatically invest it. Once the funds arrive, review your account and select investments that fit your time horizon, risk tolerance, and overall portfolio. If you do not make an investment selection, the money may remain in the account’s settlement fund.

Keep confirmations and statements for the contribution and conversion, as well as records of any earnings before the transfer. These documents can help you and your tax preparer report the transactions accurately. The [IRS instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report nondeductible IRA contributions and Roth conversions. Retain a copy of the completed form with your tax records.

### Track contribution and conversion dates, especially across tax years

Record the contribution amount, the tax year it was assigned to, the deposit date, the conversion date, and the amount converted. A contribution and its conversion may belong to different tax years. For example, you might make a contribution early in one calendar year for the previous tax year, then convert it during the current year. The contribution is reported for the year it was designated for, while the conversion is reported for the year it occurred.

Share this timeline with your tax preparer, along with account statements and prior Forms 8606. Keeping your records organized can help prevent reporting errors and preserve your after-tax IRA basis. Physicians balancing salary, practice income, and multiple retirement accounts may also want to coordinate the steps with a broader [tax strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians).

## How Does the Pro-Rata Rule Affect a Backdoor Roth IRA?

The pro-rata rule can make part of a Backdoor Roth IRA conversion taxable, even when you contribute after-tax money to a traditional IRA. Physicians may have IRA savings from previous employers, practice ownership, or retirement plan rollovers, so it’s important to review all relevant accounts before converting. The calculation considers your after-tax basis alongside your IRA balances and transactions for the year. Understanding how those pieces fit together can help you estimate the tax impact and decide whether a conversion makes sense for your broader financial plan.

### Understand how the IRS combines traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances

For the pro-rata calculation, the IRS generally considers your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances together. It does not treat each account as a separate pool, so you generally can’t convert only the after-tax dollars while leaving pre-tax money in another IRA untouched.

For example, you might make a nondeductible contribution to a traditional IRA while holding pre-tax savings in a rollover IRA from a former employer. The calculation accounts for those balances together, which may make part of your conversion taxable. Before converting, gather statements for each traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA, including accounts connected to previous jobs or practice ownership. A coordinated [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you assess the conversion alongside your other income and tax considerations.

### Use the combined IRA value on December 31 to calculate the taxable share

The value of your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs on December 31 of the conversion year is one part of the pro-rata calculation. Form 8606 also takes into account certain distributions and conversions made during that year, so the year-end balance alone does not determine the taxable share. Keep records of account values and transactions, then review the [IRS instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) to understand how to report your basis and calculate the taxable amount.

Pay close attention to timing if you contribute for one tax year but convert during the next. The contribution and conversion may be reported on different tax returns. Keep track of the tax year assigned to each contribution, the conversion date, and your December 31 balances so you and your tax preparer have a complete picture.

### Account for taxes on pre-tax IRA funds included in a conversion

If your combined IRA assets include pre-tax contributions or investment earnings, a portion of your Roth conversion may be taxable as ordinary income. The calculation considers your after-tax basis in relation to the total IRA value and relevant transactions reported on Form 8606. Making a nondeductible contribution does not, by itself, make the entire conversion tax-free.

The taxable amount adds to your income for the year, which may already include physician salary, bonuses, partnership income, or practice earnings. Estimate the tax impact before converting, and consider whether you have cash outside your retirement accounts to pay any resulting tax. Withholding taxes from the conversion can leave less money in your Roth IRA and may have other tax consequences. A tax professional can help you assess the conversion based on your complete income picture.

### Separate IRA balances from assets in 401(k), 403(b), and other workplace plans

The pro-rata calculation generally includes traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs, but not assets held directly in employer-sponsored plans such as 401(k) or 403(b) accounts. This distinction may be useful if you have pre-tax IRA funds and your current employer’s plan accepts eligible rollovers. Moving eligible funds into a workplace plan may reduce the IRA balance considered in the calculation, depending on the timing and details of the transaction.

Before taking action, confirm that the plan accepts incoming rollovers and ask which funds qualify. Review the plan’s fees, investment options, and procedures as well. Physicians with both employment and practice income may have several retirement plan options, so consider how a rollover fits into your broader [wealth management strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

### Consider eligible rollovers to an employer plan before converting

If you have pre-tax money in an IRA, check whether your 401(k) or 403(b) accepts eligible rollovers. Moving qualifying funds into a workplace plan may reduce the IRA balance included in the pro-rata calculation. First review the plan’s requirements, fees, investment options, and rollover process. A rollover may not be available, and it should make sense for your overall retirement strategy, not just for one Roth conversion.

SIMPLE IRAs have a specific restriction during the first two years of participation. During that period, rollovers generally can go only to another SIMPLE IRA. After two years, other rollover options may be available, subject to the receiving plan’s rules. Review the [IRS rollover guidance](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc413) and confirm how the rules apply to your accounts with a tax professional before moving funds.

## How Do Physicians Report a Backdoor Roth IRA on Form 8606?

Form 8606 records nondeductible traditional IRA contributions, Roth conversions, and any after-tax basis carried forward. Accurate reporting helps ensure that money you have already paid tax on is not taxed again. It also helps reconcile your IRA activity with Form 1040 and your custodian’s tax documents. Keep a copy of each year’s form so you can verify your basis when you file future returns.

### Report the nondeductible contribution and establish after-tax basis

Report a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution in Part I of Form 8606 for the tax year assigned to that contribution. You can make a contribution after the calendar year ends and before the tax filing deadline, then designate it for the prior year. The contribution year, rather than the date you transferred the funds, determines which year’s Form 8606 reports it.

Do not deduct the contribution if you intend it to be nondeductible. The amount becomes your after-tax basis, which helps identify the portion of future conversions or distributions that has already been taxed. Save the custodian’s contribution confirmation and record the tax year you selected. The [IRS Form 8606 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report contributions and track basis.

### Report the Roth conversion and calculate its taxable portion

Report a Roth conversion in Part II of Form 8606 for the calendar year the conversion occurred. The contribution and conversion may fall in different tax years. For example, if you make a contribution for one tax year in the following January and convert it later that year, report the contribution on the form for the contribution year and the conversion on the form for the conversion year.

Form 8606 helps calculate the taxable portion of the conversion. Pre-tax funds in traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRAs may affect that calculation under the pro-rata rule, even if you convert only your recent contribution. Keep the Form 1099-R from your custodian and your December 31 IRA account values. Review the [IRS instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) for the applicable calculation.

### Carry forward remaining basis and coordinate the return with Form 1040

If your after-tax basis is not fully used in a conversion or distribution, Form 8606 carries the remaining amount forward. Check the prior year’s form when preparing your current return, especially if you have made nondeductible contributions or completed conversions in multiple years. Keeping the basis consistent from one return to the next helps document which IRA dollars have already been taxed.

A Roth conversion also needs to be reported on Form 1040. Form 8606 calculates the taxable portion, while Form 1040 reports the IRA distribution and its taxable amount. Compare the figures across your forms and custodian documents before filing. Use the [current Form 1040 and instructions](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1040), since reporting details and line numbers can change.

### File Form 8606 even when the conversion creates no taxable income

You may owe no tax on a conversion if it consists entirely of after-tax basis and no other IRA balances affect the calculation. You still generally need to file Form 8606 to report the nondeductible contribution and document your basis. A zero taxable amount does not mean the transaction can be left off your return.

This matters when your contribution and conversion occur in different years or when you repeat the strategy over time. Without the form, it may be harder to substantiate your basis when you take a distribution or make another conversion. Check the [IRS filing instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) for the rules that apply to your situation, and retain a copy with your tax records.

### Correct missing or inaccurate forms and keep supporting records

If you discover that a prior Form 8606 is missing or inaccurate, review the instructions for that tax year to determine how to correct the record. If the error also changes other items on your tax return, you may need to amend the return. If the issue is limited to Form 8606, follow the IRS instructions for submitting the form. A tax professional can help assess the correction, particularly if multiple years of contributions or conversions are involved.

Keep Forms 8606, Forms 1099-R, contribution confirmations, year-end IRA statements, and conversion records together. These documents support your basis calculation and make future filings easier to verify. Physicians coordinating IRA activity with practice retirement plans or other tax decisions may also benefit from professional [tax planning for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians).

## What Tax Costs and Timing Rules Should Physicians Consider?

A Backdoor Roth IRA can involve taxes even when you make a nondeductible contribution. Before converting, review your IRA balances, income, and timing. These details can affect the taxable amount and how much you may owe.

### Account for investment gains between contribution and conversion

If your contribution earns interest or investment gains before you convert it, that growth is generally taxable when converted. Even a small amount earned in a cash or money-market account can create a taxable portion. Converting soon after contributing may limit growth, but it does not make any earnings tax-free.

Keep records of the contribution amount, conversion amount, and transaction dates. You’ll need this information to report the activity accurately on Form 8606. The [IRS instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report nondeductible contributions and Roth conversions. If you also have money in traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRAs, the pro-rata rule may affect how much of the conversion is taxable.

### Estimate taxes on converted pre-tax funds alongside salary, bonuses, and practice income

The taxable portion of a Roth conversion is generally added to your income for that year. For physicians, that may mean accounting for salary, bonuses, call pay, partnership distributions, or practice profits alongside the conversion. A large conversion in a high-income year can increase your tax bill and may affect other income-based calculations.

Estimate the taxable share before converting rather than assuming the transaction will be tax-free. The pro-rata calculation considers your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA funds, including amounts outside the account you convert. Review the calculation on [Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) with a tax professional so you can weigh the potential tax cost against the long-term benefits of moving money into a Roth IRA.

### Review federal and state tax treatment

Federal rules determine how you report a Roth conversion on your return, but state tax treatment can vary. This is important if you moved during the year, live in one state and work in another, or receive income from a practice across state lines. Residency and state-specific rules may affect where a conversion is taxable and how you report it.

The [IRS publication on traditional and Roth IRAs](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a) explains federal contribution and conversion rules. For state requirements, check the tax agency for each state involved or consult a tax professional familiar with multistate physician income. Don’t assume that your state handles every IRA transaction exactly as the federal government does.

### Consider paying conversion taxes with non-IRA funds

If part of your conversion is taxable, plan how you’ll pay the resulting tax bill. When your cash flow allows, using money outside your IRA can leave more of the converted amount invested in the Roth account. Withholding taxes from the IRA means less money reaches the Roth, and it may have additional consequences, particularly if you’re under age 59½.

Estimate the tax before converting and consider setting aside funds or adjusting withholding if needed. The right choice depends on your income, available cash, and other financial priorities, such as student loan payments, practice expenses, or retirement plan contributions. A tax professional can help estimate the bill, while a financial planner can help you see how the payment fits into your broader plan.

### Separate Roth IRA qualified-distribution rules from the five-year conversion rule

Roth IRAs have two five-year rules, and they serve different purposes. To withdraw earnings tax-free and penalty-free, you generally need to meet the Roth IRA five-year holding requirement and a qualifying condition, such as reaching age 59½. The [IRS guidance on Roth IRA distributions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b) explains when withdrawals qualify.

A separate five-year period applies to each conversion. If you withdraw converted taxable amounts before that period ends and before age 59½, the 10% additional tax may apply, unless an exception covers you. The conversion period generally begins on January 1 of the year you convert. Check the rules before taking money out, and don’t confuse this conversion rule with the requirements for qualified earnings withdrawals.

### Coordinate conversion timing with income changes, practice ownership, and tax planning

The right time to convert depends on your financial picture for the year. A transition from residency or fellowship to attending work, a planned reduction in clinical hours, or a year with lower practice earnings may affect the tax cost. Compare your expected income with bonuses, distributions, and other sources before deciding whether to convert and how much.

If you have pre-tax IRA funds, ask whether your employer’s 401(k) or 403(b) accepts eligible rollovers before starting. Plan rules vary, and SIMPLE IRAs have a two-year restriction on certain rollovers. Practice owners should also consider business income, retirement plan contributions, and estimated tax payments. Coordinating these decisions with a [physician-focused tax strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help connect the conversion to your wider financial plan.

## What Backdoor Roth IRA Mistakes Should Physicians Avoid?

A Backdoor Roth IRA involves two separate steps: making a nondeductible contribution to a traditional IRA, then converting funds to a Roth IRA. Each step has its own reporting rules, and a conversion can be partly taxable even when your contribution was made with after-tax dollars. Existing IRA balances, investment gains, and the timing of your transactions can all affect the outcome.

Before you begin, take stock of your retirement accounts and gather records of past contributions and conversions. This is especially important if you have accounts from residency, a former employer, or a medical practice. Keep copies of prior Forms 8606, which track your after-tax IRA basis. The IRS [Form 8606 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report nondeductible contributions and Roth conversions. You can also coordinate the strategy with your broader [tax planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians), particularly if your income includes salary, bonuses, or practice earnings.

### Include rollover, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances when estimating pro-rata taxes

Before converting, review the combined balances in your traditional, rollover, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs. For the pro-rata calculation, these accounts are generally considered together, even if they are held at different financial institutions or you plan to convert only one account. As a result, a conversion may be partly taxable when you have pre-tax IRA funds alongside nondeductible contributions.

The calculation also considers IRA values at year-end, so check your projected December 31 balances before acting. Assets in a 401(k) or 403(b) are not included in the IRA pool, but an employer plan may accept eligible pre-tax IRA funds. Confirm the plan’s rules before arranging a rollover, and check whether any restrictions apply to a SIMPLE IRA. The [IRS guidance on IRA contributions and rollovers](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a) can help you identify the rules to review.

### Avoid deducting a contribution intended to be nondeductible

A Backdoor Roth IRA begins with a contribution to a traditional IRA. If you intend that contribution to be nondeductible, do not also claim a tax deduction for it. A deduction would treat the contribution as pre-tax money, affecting how much of a later conversion may be taxable.

Report the nondeductible contribution on Form 8606 to establish your after-tax basis. Keep the contribution confirmation and a copy of the filed form with your tax records. Deductibility depends on factors such as your income, filing status, and whether you or your spouse participate in a workplace retirement plan. Review the [IRS rules for traditional IRA deductions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a) before filing, and ask a tax professional if you are unsure how to report a contribution.

### Stay within the annual limit and assign the contribution to the right tax year

The annual IRA contribution limit applies to your combined traditional and Roth IRA contributions, not to each account separately. You also need eligible compensation, and an age-based catch-up contribution may apply. Check the limit for the tax year you are funding, and include any contributions you have already made to either type of IRA. The [IRS IRA contribution limit guidance](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-ira-contribution-limits) explains the current rules.

If you contribute near the filing deadline, tell your IRA custodian which tax year the contribution is for. A contribution made early in the year may be designated for the prior tax year, while a conversion is reported for the calendar year in which it occurs. If those dates fall in different years, you may report the contribution and conversion on separate tax returns. Save confirmations that show the amount, transaction date, and contribution year.

### Consider the consequences before withholding taxes from a conversion

You can generally elect to have taxes withheld from a Roth conversion, but the withheld amount does not enter the Roth IRA. It is treated as a distribution from the traditional IRA and may be subject to an additional tax if you are under age 59½ and no exception applies. Consider the effect before choosing withholding, especially if your goal is to move the full amount into the Roth account.

Any investment gains or interest earned before conversion may also be taxable when converted. Check the account balance before completing the transaction, even if the contribution has been in the account only briefly. Some physicians pay conversion-related taxes from non-IRA funds, but that choice depends on available cash and the projected tax bill. The [Form 8606 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report taxable and nontaxable portions of a conversion.

### File Form 8606 and track basis from prior years

Form 8606 records nondeductible traditional IRA contributions and helps calculate the taxable portion of a Roth conversion. File it when required, even if you believe the conversion is entirely tax-free. Without accurate records, your after-tax basis may not be accounted for properly, increasing the risk of confusion or being taxed twice on the same dollars.

Keep copies of filed Forms 8606, contribution confirmations, conversion records, and year-end account statements. If you made nondeductible contributions in earlier years, use your prior forms to verify the basis carried forward before reporting a new conversion. If a past return omitted or misstated Form 8606, ask a tax professional how to correct it. The [IRS instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) cover contributions, conversions, and basis carried forward.

### Estimate taxes and set aside payment funds before converting

Before converting, estimate how much may be taxable. The result can depend on your pre-tax IRA balances, after-tax basis, investment gains, and other IRA activity during the year. For physicians, conversion income is added to other taxable income, which may include salary, bonuses, partnership income, or practice earnings. Consider the full-year tax picture rather than looking at the IRA transaction alone.

If you expect to owe tax, set aside funds and check whether estimated payments may be needed. A conversion can affect your federal and state tax bills, and the amount you owe may change if your income or deductions shift. A tax professional can help project the impact and determine whether your payment plan is appropriate. The [IRS estimated tax guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes) outlines when estimated payments may apply.

## Is a Backdoor Roth IRA a Good Fit for Your Situation?

A backdoor Roth IRA may help physicians contribute to Roth savings when their income is above the limit for direct Roth IRA contributions. Whether it makes sense depends on more than income: your tax outlook, existing IRA balances, workplace retirement plans, and cash needs all matter. Consider how the strategy fits with your broader financial plan before you contribute or convert.

### Compare your current tax bracket with your expected future bracket

A Roth conversion can mean paying tax now on the taxable amount converted in exchange for the potential for tax-free qualified withdrawals later. The tradeoff may be appealing if you expect your tax rate to be similar or higher in retirement, but it is not automatically the right choice. Compare your current federal and state tax brackets with likely retirement income, planned work hours, and where you expect to live.

Physician income can shift substantially between residency, fellowship, an attending role, and practice ownership. A year with lower income may look different from one with a large bonus or practice distribution. Estimate the tax impact before converting, and consider asking a tax professional to model different scenarios. [Empower’s overview of backdoor Roth IRAs](https://www.empower.com/the-currency/money/backdoor-roth-ira-good-move) explains why weighing the current tax cost against potential future benefits is an important part of the decision.

### Weigh existing IRA balances, rollover options, and available tax funds

Review your traditional, rollover, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances before you start. Under the pro-rata rule, pre-tax money in these accounts can make part of a Roth conversion taxable, even when the contribution you just made was nondeductible. The calculation uses your IRA activity and year-end balances, so check your records and the [IRS instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) before converting.

If your employer’s 401(k) or 403(b) accepts eligible rollovers, moving pre-tax IRA funds into the plan may be an option to discuss with the plan administrator and a tax professional. Plan rules vary, and special restrictions apply to some SIMPLE IRA rollovers. Also decide how you would pay any conversion tax. Using money outside your IRA may preserve more for Roth savings, but make sure it does not strain your cash reserves.

### Coordinate Roth contributions with workplace and practice retirement plans

A backdoor Roth IRA is one part of a retirement strategy, not a substitute for reviewing your workplace or practice plan. Check whether your employer offers a match, what contribution options your plan allows, and how each account fits your savings goals. Physicians with access to a 401(k), 403(b), or practice-sponsored plan may have several ways to save, each with its own limits and tax treatment.

If you own a practice, review the plan design with your benefits administrator and tax or financial professionals. Employer contributions, employee deferrals, and Roth features can affect how your savings fit together. Coordinate IRA decisions with your broader [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians), so you can weigh tax diversification alongside plan benefits, contribution rules, and your long-term goals.

### Balance retirement savings with cash flow and other financial priorities

Even if the tax treatment seems favorable, a Roth contribution may not be the best next use of your money. Consider high-interest debt, emergency savings, student loan payments, insurance, and upcoming costs such as a home purchase or practice investment. Residents and fellows may have different priorities from established physicians, while practice owners may need cash available for payroll, equipment, or uneven revenue.

Set a contribution amount that fits your budget, and avoid using money you may need for near-term expenses or conversion taxes. If you are weighing retirement savings alongside debt, taxes, and practice cash flow, [wealth management for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) can help put the Roth decision in context with your other financial priorities.

### Revisit the strategy as your income, practice ownership, and retirement plans change

Your decision does not have to be permanent. Review the strategy each year, especially after moving from training to an attending role, changing your work hours, experiencing a significant income shift, or becoming a practice owner. Your tax bracket may change, and a new employer retirement plan could offer rollover options that were not previously available.

Revisit the plan as you approach retirement, too. Your expected withdrawals, tax situation, and estate goals may evolve. Keep records of contributions, conversions, and prior Forms 8606 to track your after-tax basis accurately. A regular review with your tax professional and financial planner can help you decide whether to repeat the strategy, adjust it, or focus on another retirement savings option.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a backdoor Roth IRA conversion always tax-free?**\
No. A conversion may be partly taxable if your traditional, rollover, SEP, or SIMPLE IRAs contain pre-tax money, or if your contribution earns interest before you convert it. The pro-rata calculation determines how much of the conversion is taxable.

**Do 401(k) and 403(b) balances count toward the pro-rata rule?**\
Workplace plan balances are generally not included in the calculation. However, your traditional, rollover, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances are generally considered together. Ask your plan administrator whether your 401(k) or 403(b) accepts eligible pre-tax IRA rollovers.

**When do I report a contribution and a conversion made in different years?**\
Report the nondeductible contribution for the tax year you designated it for. Report the conversion for the calendar year it took place. Keep the dates and account statements so you can report each transaction correctly.

**Do I need to file Form 8606 if I owe no tax on the conversion?**\
Generally, yes. Form 8606 reports nondeductible contributions and tracks your after-tax IRA basis, even if the conversion results in no taxable income. Keep a copy with your tax records.

**Can I use this strategy more than once?**\
You may be able to repeat it in later years, as long as each contribution meets the applicable IRA limit and compensation rules. Review your IRA balances and tax situation each year, since they can change how much of a conversion is taxable.

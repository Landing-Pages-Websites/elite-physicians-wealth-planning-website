From residency to an attending role, or from hospital employment to practice ownership, a physician’s career can involve several employers and retirement accounts. When you change jobs, deciding what to do with an old 401(k) is an opportunity to bring those savings into focus. How doctors should roll over a 401(k) when changing jobs depends on account rules, tax treatment, investment choices, and when you may need access to the funds. You might keep the account in the former plan, move eligible savings into a new employer plan, or consider an IRA. Before choosing, review the full picture, including other retirement accounts and any plans for backdoor Roth IRA contributions. The right next step is the one that supports your overall strategy.

## Key Takeaways

* **Compare your choices before moving funds**: Check whether you can keep the 401(k), transfer it to a new employer’s plan, or roll it into an IRA. Compare fees, investments, access rules, and plan requirements.
* **Review tax consequences before requesting a transfer**: A direct rollover can help avoid withholding. Cashing out, converting pre-tax funds to Roth, or adding a traditional IRA balance may affect your taxes and backdoor Roth strategy.
* **Coordinate the decision with your wider financial plan**: Check vesting, outstanding loans, other workplace benefits, and your retirement timeline. Confirm plan details with administrators and consult a tax or financial professional when needed.

## What Can Doctors Do With a 401(k) When Changing Jobs?

Changing jobs gives you a reason to review your retirement savings, but you do not have to move your 401(k) right away. Depending on your former employer’s plan rules and your new employer’s benefits, you may be able to leave the account where it is, transfer eligible funds to a new workplace plan, or roll them into an IRA. Each choice can affect fees, investment options, access to funds, and future tax planning.

Start by gathering recent statements and plan documents. Check your account balance, which employer contributions are vested, whether you have an outstanding loan, and what your new plan will accept. The [IRS rollover guidance](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc413) explains how rollovers are generally treated for tax purposes, though the details depend on the accounts and the way you transfer the money.

For physicians, a rollover may also intersect with a new job’s benefits, a move into private practice, or plans to make backdoor Roth IRA contributions. Consider the decision alongside your income, savings priorities, and retirement timeline. A coordinated [wealth management plan for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) can help you assess how your workplace accounts fit with your wider financial goals.

### Leave it in the former employer’s plan, if allowed

You may be able to leave your 401(k) with your former employer. The account remains yours, and its investments continue to change in value. You generally cannot make new contributions to the plan after leaving the job, but you can keep managing the balance under the plan’s rules.

Before deciding, check whether the plan allows former employees to keep their accounts. Some plans require people with smaller balances to move or withdraw their money. Compare the account’s fees, investment choices, and available support with the alternatives. Leaving the funds in place may suit you if the plan offers low-cost investments and useful features. Just update your contact information and keep a record of the account so it remains part of your retirement plan.### Roll eligible funds into the new plan, if accepted

Your new employer’s 401(k) may accept rollovers from a former plan, but acceptance is not automatic. Ask the plan administrator which account types it accepts and whether any minimums, restrictions, or special steps apply. Certain plans, including some 457(b) plans, may have rules that affect access to transferred funds.

Combining accounts can make your savings easier to track, but compare the new plan’s fees, investments, and withdrawal rules before moving money. If you proceed, ask both administrators how to complete the transfer and how the funds will be invested once they arrive. Confirm that the new plan has received the money and that it is allocated according to your choices. As [rollover guidance for former employer plans](https://www.thefinitygroup.com/blog/what-do-i-do-with-my-old-retirement-plan/) notes, consolidating accounts can simplify oversight, but you do not have to roll over your savings just because you changed jobs.### Roll pre-tax funds into a traditional IRA and Roth funds into a Roth IRA

You can roll eligible 401(k) funds into an IRA. Generally, pre-tax savings go into a traditional IRA, while Roth savings go into a Roth IRA. Keeping each type of money in the corresponding account usually preserves its tax treatment. An IRA may offer more investment choices than a workplace plan, though the fees, service, and investment support vary by provider.

Before choosing an IRA, consider how the move could affect other tax plans. A pre-tax balance in a traditional IRA may affect the tax calculation if you later use the backdoor Roth IRA strategy. This can be relevant for physicians whose income limits their ability to contribute directly to a Roth IRA. Review your existing IRA balances and discuss the potential consequences with a tax professional before arranging the rollover.### Cash out only after weighing taxes, penalties, and lost growth

Taking your 401(k) savings as cash can provide money for expenses between jobs, but it can also reduce what you have set aside for retirement. The taxable portion of a distribution is generally treated as ordinary income. If you are younger than 59½, an additional 10% early-withdrawal tax may apply unless you qualify for an exception.

Before requesting a payment, estimate the taxes and any penalty, and compare the amount you would receive with the long-term cost of removing the money from your retirement savings. Your plan may withhold taxes from the payment, but withholding may not cover your final tax bill. If you need cash, consider whether other funds could meet the expense without drawing from retirement. Because the consequences depend on your age, account, and circumstances, review the decision with a tax professional before taking a distribution.### Check balance rules, vested contributions, loans, and deadlines

Read your former plan’s distribution notice and account statement before making a choice. The plan may set rules for smaller balances, and some employer contributions may not be fully vested. Check the vested amount, which is the portion you are entitled to keep under the plan’s terms, before estimating how much you can transfer.

If you have an outstanding 401(k) loan, ask the plan administrator what happens to it when you leave. Repayment terms may change, and an unpaid balance could be treated as a loan offset or distribution, which may have tax consequences and deadlines. Request a written explanation of your options and note any dates that apply. Keep copies of the plan documents, notices, and completed forms, then confirm that the transfer or payment has been processed as requested.### Review 403(b)s, pensions, and deferred compensation separately

A job change can affect more than your 401(k). Physicians who have worked for hospitals, universities, public employers, or medical groups may also have 403(b) accounts, 457(b) plans, pensions, cash-balance plans, or deferred compensation. Each may have its own fees, vesting terms, investment choices, payout options, and access rules. Do not assume that a choice available for your 401(k) also applies to these benefits.

Make a list of every account, its balance, the plan administrator, and any deadlines or decisions you need to address. Review pensions and deferred compensation separately because their terms and tax treatment can differ from those of a 401(k). Gathering this information helps you coordinate old benefits with your new employer’s plan and your broader [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians).

## How Should Doctors Compare a New 401(k) With an IRA?

A 401(k) rollover is not just a transfer between accounts. It can affect your investment choices, access to savings, taxes, and future retirement planning. Before moving money, compare the new employer’s plan with your former plan and an IRA, then consider how each option fits your career plans and household finances.

The best choice depends on the details, including plan rules and your need for flexibility. A doctor changing employers may have different priorities than a practice owner or someone planning to retire early. Take time to review the options before requesting a distribution.

### Compare fees, investments, and services

Compare the total cost of each account, not just the expense ratios of its investment funds. A 401(k) may charge administrative or recordkeeping fees, while an IRA’s costs depend on its provider and investments. Ask for a clear breakdown so you can compare like with like. The Department of Labor’s guide to 401(k) fees can help you identify common charges.

Next, review the investment options and any planning support offered. An IRA often provides a broader range of investments, while a 401(k) may offer a smaller menu that is easier to manage. Consider which account gives you suitable choices and support for your goals. Physicians can also review how retirement accounts fit into a broader [wealth management plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

### Check rollover rules, investment options, and advice

Do not assume your new employer’s plan will accept an old 401(k). Ask the plan administrator whether rollovers are permitted, which account types are eligible, and whether any investments must be sold before the transfer. Confirm how the plan handles pre-tax and Roth balances, since these funds generally need to move into compatible account types to preserve their tax treatment.

Read the plan documents and compare their terms with your existing plan and potential IRA provider. If any part of the transfer is unclear, ask the administrators, a tax professional, or a fiduciary financial planner to review the details before you act. The [IRS rollover chart](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-tege/rollover_chart.pdf) outlines common rollover options, but the receiving plan’s rules determine what it will accept.

### Compare loan, withdrawal, and creditor-protection rules

Check how each account handles loans and withdrawals. Some 401(k) plans allow participants to borrow from their accounts, while IRAs do not offer loans. Withdrawal rules and exceptions can also differ, so review the plan documents and ask how a rollover could affect your access to funds. This can matter if you expect a career transition or need to plan around a period of reduced income.

Creditor protections are another factor, but they depend on the account type and your circumstances. Employer plans generally receive federal protection under ERISA, while IRA protections can vary based on the type of claim and state law. Do not make a decision based on a general rule alone. If you own a practice or have specific asset-protection concerns, discuss them with a qualified professional as part of your [physician-focused wealth plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

### Consider whether the age-55 401(k) exception applies

If you leave a job during or after the calendar year you turn 55, you may qualify to take distributions from that employer’s 401(k) without the usual 10% early-withdrawal penalty. The distributions are generally still subject to income tax. This exception may be relevant if you are considering early retirement, changing roles, or taking time away from clinical work.

The exception generally applies to the 401(k) of the employer you leave, not to an IRA. Rolling the account into an IRA can therefore remove access to this specific exception for those funds. Eligibility depends on the details, so confirm the rules before moving money. The [IRS guidance on early distributions](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc558) explains the penalty and exceptions. If timing matters, consider the rollover alongside your [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians).

### Weigh convenience and consolidation against flexibility and control

Consolidating eligible accounts can make it easier to see your retirement savings in one place and manage your investments. This may appeal to physicians who have changed employers several times, moved between academic and private practice, or accumulated accounts at different institutions. A new employer’s plan may provide a convenient workplace option, while an IRA can offer more choice over investments and providers.

But consolidation is not automatically the best move. An old plan may have competitive fees or features worth keeping, and a new plan may have limited investments or withdrawal rules that do not suit your needs. Compare each account’s costs, services, investment options, and access rules before deciding. If you have multiple income sources or practice-related retirement benefits, include the choice in your broader [retirement planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians).

### Remember: a rollover is optional

You do not need to move an old 401(k) as soon as you change jobs. If the former plan permits it, you may be able to leave your savings there. Other options may include transferring eligible funds to your new employer’s plan or rolling them into an IRA. Each route has different rules, costs, investment choices, and implications for accessing your money.

Before making a decision, consider how a pre-tax IRA balance could affect future backdoor Roth IRA contributions. The tax calculation may include the balance in traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs, so review your full account picture and your spouse’s accounts with a tax professional. Your household income, career plans, and other financial goals may also matter. A coordinated [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you assess the rollover as part of your overall plan.

## What Are the Tax Implications of a 401(k) Rollover?

A 401(k) rollover can keep retirement savings invested while preserving their tax-advantaged status, but the tax outcome depends on how the transfer is handled. The source and destination accounts matter, as do the types of contributions in your plan. Moving pre-tax funds into a Roth account, for example, can create taxable income, while taking the money as a check may trigger withholding and a short deadline to complete a rollover.

For physicians, a job change can also affect more than one account. You may have a 401(k), 403(b), pension, or deferred compensation plan, and your new role could change your income, benefits, and retirement savings options. Before moving money, ask both plan administrators what the plans allow and confirm the tax treatment with a qualified tax professional. A coordinated [tax planning strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you weigh the rollover against your income, household accounts, and longer-term goals.

The points below cover common tax considerations, but plan rules and individual circumstances vary. Treat a rollover as a decision to review, not an automatic step when you leave an employer.

### Use a direct rollover to avoid withholding and unintended taxable income

A direct rollover sends eligible retirement funds from your former employer’s plan to a new employer’s plan or an IRA without paying the money to you first. When the receiving account accepts the funds and the transfer is completed correctly, the rollover generally isn’t included in your taxable income for that year. Direct rollovers from an employer plan also avoid the mandatory federal withholding that generally applies when an eligible distribution is paid to you.

If the plan issues a check, ask whether it can be made payable to the receiving institution for your benefit. Confirm the payee instructions with both plan administrators before requesting the transfer. The IRS explains how [rollovers are treated for tax purposes](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc413), but each plan sets its own procedures and may limit which assets it accepts.

### Keep pre-tax and Roth funds in the right account types

Employer plans may hold pre-tax contributions, designated Roth contributions, and sometimes after-tax contributions. Each type can have different rollover options and tax consequences, so ask the plan administrator to identify the sources of money in your account before starting a transfer.

Pre-tax funds can generally move to a traditional IRA or an eligible employer plan. Designated Roth funds can generally move to a Roth IRA or to a designated Roth account in another employer plan that accepts them. After-tax contributions may require additional care to preserve the correct tax treatment. The IRS [rollover chart](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-tege/rollover_chart.pdf) outlines common transfer options. Review the receiving account’s rules, and keep records showing where each portion of your balance went.

### Understand the tax cost of converting pre-tax funds to a Roth account

Moving pre-tax 401(k) savings to a Roth IRA or designated Roth account is generally a Roth conversion, not a tax-free rollover. The untaxed amount converted is typically included in your taxable income for that year. A large conversion could raise your tax bill, so estimate the impact before authorizing the transaction.

Your income may change when you start a new position, receive a signing bonus, become a partner, or earn income from a practice. Those changes can affect whether a conversion makes sense and when to complete it. Review the expected tax cost with a tax professional, and coordinate the decision with your broader [physician tax strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians). If you proceed, consider how you’ll pay the resulting tax. Using retirement funds to cover it may reduce the amount that stays invested.

### Check how a traditional IRA rollover could affect backdoor Roth contributions

If you use the backdoor Roth IRA strategy, check the tax consequences before rolling pre-tax 401(k) money into a traditional IRA. The pro-rata rule generally considers your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances when calculating how much of a Roth conversion is taxable. As a result, adding pre-tax funds to an IRA can make a later backdoor Roth conversion partly or mostly taxable.

This doesn’t mean you should rule out an IRA rollover. Compare the investment choices, fees, and plan features first, and consider your household’s full account picture. The calculation may also depend on the value of your IRA accounts at year-end. The IRS explains the relevant conversion rules in [Publication 590-A](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a). A tax professional can help you assess the effect before you move funds.

### Know withholding rules and the 60-day deadline before taking a distribution

A direct rollover is usually simpler than having the money paid to you. When an eligible rollover distribution from an employer plan is paid to you, the plan generally must withhold 20% for federal income taxes. You may still be able to roll over the full amount within 60 days, but you would need to replace the withheld portion from other funds. If you roll over only the amount you received, the withheld balance may be treated as a taxable distribution.

The 60-day deadline generally applies when you receive an eligible distribution, rather than when funds move directly between accounts. Exceptions may apply in certain situations, but don’t assume you qualify. Before accepting a check, ask the plan administrator about the payment method and review the IRS guidance on [rollover timing and withholding](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc413).

### Review taxes and possible early-withdrawal penalties before cashing out

Cashing out your 401(k) can create a tax bill and reduce the amount you have invested for retirement. The taxable portion of a distribution is generally taxed as ordinary income. If you’re under age 59½, an additional 10% tax may apply unless an exception is available. Any tax withheld from the payment is only an advance payment, not a final calculation of what you owe.

Before taking a distribution, compare its immediate value with the taxes, potential penalty, and long-term effect on your savings. A job transition may bring expenses or a gap in income, but consider other cash-flow options before using retirement funds. The IRS outlines general [early-distribution tax and penalty rules](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc558). Ask a tax professional to review your circumstances, including whether an exception may apply.

### Keep transfer confirmations and tax forms

Save the paperwork from both the account sending the funds and the account receiving them. Keep rollover forms, statements, transfer confirmations, and tax documents such as Form 1099-R or Form 5498. A Form 1099-R may report a distribution even when you completed a valid rollover, so check the details and report the transaction correctly on your tax return.

Confirm that the amount received matches the amount sent and that pre-tax and Roth funds reached the intended accounts. Keep any records related to withholding, after-tax contributions, or a Roth conversion. The IRS provides instructions for reporting rollovers in its [Form 1040 guidance](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040gi). If a tax form appears inconsistent with the transfer, contact the plan administrator or a tax professional before filing.

## How Do You Complete a 401(k) Rollover?

A 401(k) rollover takes more than moving money between accounts. First, confirm what types of contributions your account holds, such as pre-tax savings and Roth 401(k) funds. Then compare your options: you may be able to leave the money in your former employer’s plan, move it to a new employer’s plan, or roll it into an IRA. Your next plan must accept the rollover, and different rules may apply to each type of money.

Before starting, gather recent account statements and plan documents. Ask both administrators about fees, forms, timing, investment restrictions, and any outstanding loan. A direct transfer from one trustee to another can help reduce the risk of withholding or a missed deadline. The [IRS rollover chart](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-tege/rollover_chart.pdf) is a helpful reference for which account types may generally roll into others, but your plan’s terms and your circumstances matter.

For physicians changing jobs, it can help to coordinate the rollover with benefits enrollment, household cash flow, and other retirement accounts. A decision about one old 401(k) could also affect your tax strategy or future savings options. If you have several workplace plans or want help weighing the tax and investment considerations, include the decision in your broader [physician-focused wealth plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

### Confirm the receiving plan or IRA accepts the account and assets

Before requesting a transfer, contact the receiving plan or IRA provider to confirm it accepts rollovers from your 401(k). A new employer’s plan may accept money from former employers, but it is not required to. Ask which types of funds it can receive. For example, the plan may have different procedures for pre-tax savings and Roth 401(k) funds.

Check whether every asset in your account can move to the destination. Some plan-specific investments may not be available in an IRA or another employer’s plan, so they may need to be sold before the transfer. Confirm the eligibility of each portion of your balance, including employer contributions and any Roth funds. The [IRS rollover chart](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-tege/rollover_chart.pdf) offers a starting point for checking which account types may generally transfer to one another. Get the receiving institution’s instructions before you submit a request.

### Review plan documents, fees, forms, and transfer rules

Read the summary plan description and rollover materials from both the old and new plans. Check for account maintenance fees, investment expenses, distribution or processing charges, and rules that affect access to your money. Review vesting, too. If you leave a job before employer contributions are fully vested, you may forfeit some of that balance.

Ask each administrator which forms you need, how to submit them, and whether any signatures or additional verification are required. Find out whether the old plan charges a transfer or account-closing fee, and whether the receiving plan has a minimum balance. Compare the investments and services available through each option rather than assuming an IRA or workplace plan is automatically less expensive. If the rollover could affect your taxes or retirement strategy, consider it as part of a broader [wealth management plan for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

### Ask both administrators about timing and investment restrictions

Contact the administrator of your former employer’s plan and the provider receiving the money. Ask how long each step typically takes, when the transfer will be complete, and whether there are processing windows or deadlines to know about. This can help you coordinate the rollover with benefits enrollment at a new hospital, group practice, or private practice.

Ask whether your investments can transfer as they are or must be sold first. A receiving account may not offer the same investments as your old 401(k), and some plan-specific funds cannot transfer at all. Find out whether the money could be held in cash during processing and whether you will need to choose new investments once it arrives. The transfer does not set your long-term investment mix. Review the available options as part of your [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians).

### Request a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer, if available

When possible, ask the plan to send the funds directly to the receiving plan or IRA. A direct rollover means the money does not pass through your personal bank account, which can help prevent withholding and reduce the risk of missing a deadline. If the plan issues a check, confirm that it is payable to the receiving institution for your benefit, rather than to you personally.

A distribution paid directly to you may be subject to mandatory federal income tax withholding if it is an eligible rollover distribution. The [IRS explains rollover and withholding rules](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc413), including how the payment method can affect the process. Before submitting paperwork, verify the instructions with both administrators. Keep copies of your forms, check details, and confirmation numbers so you can follow up if anything is delayed.

### Check whether investments transfer in kind or need to be sold and reinvested

Ask whether your investments can move to the new account without being sold. This is called an in-kind transfer, and whether it is possible depends on the investment and the receiving account. Many 401(k) plans offer funds that are available only within that plan. If the receiving institution cannot hold them, the plan may sell those investments and transfer the proceeds as cash.

If a sale is required, ask when it will occur and whether you can choose the timing. Find out whether there may be a period when the money is out of the market during processing. After the funds arrive, check whether they are invested or sitting in a cash or settlement option. You may need to select investments yourself. Before authorizing a transfer of a large or complex portfolio, consider how the change fits your overall investment allocation and retirement plans.

### Track the transfer and confirm the funds reach the right account

Save copies of every form, letter, and confirmation number after you submit the rollover request. Record when the old plan processes the distribution and when the receiving institution expects the money. If the transfer takes longer than expected, contact both administrators to find out where the funds are and what steps remain.

When the funds arrive, confirm that the amount and account type are correct. Check whether the money is invested or held in cash, and make sure pre-tax and Roth funds went to the appropriate destinations. Keep statements and tax forms with your financial records. You may receive Form 1099-R from the old plan and, if the funds go to an IRA, Form 5498 from the IRA provider. A tax professional can help you confirm how to report the transaction. Include the transfer records in your [retirement planning review](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians).

### Ask how the plan will handle an outstanding loan or loan offset

If you borrowed from your 401(k), contact the plan administrator before leaving your job or requesting a rollover. Ask whether you can continue making payments, when repayment is due, and what happens if you do not repay the balance on time. A 401(k) loan generally cannot be moved into an IRA as part of a standard rollover.

If the plan reduces your account balance to cover an unpaid loan, that amount may be treated as a plan loan offset and could have tax consequences. Certain qualified plan loan offsets may qualify for additional time to roll over the amount, depending on the circumstances. The [IRS rollover guidance](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc413) explains the general rules. Ask the plan administrator for written details about your loan and speak with a tax professional before deciding how to handle it.

## Which Physician-Specific Factors Should Guide the Decision?

A job change can bring several financial decisions at once: enrolling in benefits, adjusting your budget, and deciding what to do with retirement savings. For physicians, the details can vary widely depending on whether you are moving between a hospital, an academic institution, private practice, or an executive role. Your benefits may include a 401(k), 403(b), 457(b), pension, or deferred compensation plan, each with its own rules.

Before moving an old account, look at how the choice could affect taxes, investment options, access to funds, and future Roth IRA contributions. Consider your household’s income and savings goals, too. Student loan payments, a spouse’s benefits, or plans to buy into or start a practice can all influence the timing and practical value of a rollover.

There is no single right choice for every physician. Compare the old and new plans, confirm how each handles rollovers and withdrawals, and note any deadlines. A physician-focused planner can help connect the decision to your [tax strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians), retirement goals, and broader financial priorities. These factors can help you evaluate your options.

### Coordinate the rollover with new-job benefits and plan enrollment

Before moving your old 401(k), learn how your new employer’s benefits work. Confirm when enrollment opens, when contributions begin, whether the plan accepts rollovers, and what fees and investment options it offers. If you are joining a hospital or academic system, ask whether you will have a 403(b), 457(b), pension, or several plans to consider.

The timing matters. You may want to compare both plans before deciding where the money should go, rather than moving it before you understand your new benefits. Ask both plan administrators about their procedures, required forms, processing times, and any deadlines. This [job-change rollover checklist](https://carasshulman.com/changing-jobs-dont-forget-about-your-401k-2) can help you organize the steps, but verify the rules directly with each plan.

### Review household income, tax brackets, and spouse IRA balances before backdoor Roth contributions

If you use a backdoor Roth IRA strategy, check how rolling pre-tax 401(k) money into a traditional IRA could affect your taxes. When you convert IRA assets to a Roth IRA, the pro-rata rule generally considers the value of your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs. A rollover into an IRA could therefore make some of a conversion taxable. Review your income, expected tax bracket, and timing with a tax professional before moving the funds. These [backdoor Roth considerations](https://www.thefinitygroup.com/blog/what-do-i-do-with-my-old-retirement-plan/) are worth checking before you act.

Your spouse’s IRA balances are not combined with yours for this calculation. Still, consider household income when reviewing Roth contribution eligibility and each partner’s retirement strategy. Evaluate your accounts separately, then consider how your choices fit your shared goals.

### List old 401(k)s, 403(b)s, pensions, and deferred compensation

Physicians can accumulate retirement benefits across employers and career stages. Make an inventory of current and former 401(k) and 403(b) plans, 457(b) accounts, pensions, cash-balance plans, deferred compensation, and IRAs. For each account, record its balance, tax treatment, fees, investment choices, beneficiary designations, and any outstanding loans.

This list helps you see which accounts may be eligible for a rollover and which have separate distribution rules. A pension or deferred compensation plan, for example, may offer choices and deadlines that do not apply to a 401(k). A [retirement planning checklist for physicians](https://beaconpointe.com/planning-through-the-ages-physicians-in-their-forties/) can help you gather information across accounts. Keep recent statements and plan documents together so you can compare options accurately.

### Factor in student loans, cash flow, and competing savings priorities

A rollover decision should fit your current cash flow as well as your long-term plans. Changing jobs may bring a gap between paychecks, relocation costs, credentialing delays, or unpaid time off. Student loan payments, family expenses, and a potential home purchase can also affect how much you can contribute to a new plan.

Estimate near-term expenses and make sure you have a practical cash reserve before committing more money to retirement savings. Taking money out of a retirement account can trigger taxes or penalties, so consider other sources for short-term needs. If you are weighing increased contributions against debt repayment or cash savings, compare the trade-offs in your budget. Physicians with variable income or a recent career transition may want to review these priorities before consolidating accounts.

### Review retirement-plan options when joining or starting a medical practice

Moving into private practice or ownership can change your retirement-plan options. Depending on your business structure, employees, and goals, you may need to compare an employer-sponsored 401(k) with a solo 401(k), SEP IRA, SIMPLE IRA, or cash-balance plan. Each option has different contribution limits, administrative requirements, and tax considerations. Do not assume your former employee plan will still be the best fit.

Ask who can participate, how contributions are handled, and whether the plan accepts incoming rollovers. Read the plan documents carefully, and consider having a fiduciary advisor or tax preparer review them before you act. ### Match account access to career timing, early retirement, and cash needs

Think about when you may need access to your retirement savings. If you expect to retire early or leave clinical work before traditional retirement age, compare the withdrawal rules for your old plan, your new employer’s plan, and an IRA. In some cases, leaving money in a former employer’s 401(k) may preserve access under the age-55 separation-from-service exception. Eligibility depends on your age and when you leave that employer, so confirm the details before moving the funds. Savant’s explanation of the age-55 rule can help you identify questions to ask.

An IRA may offer different withdrawal options, while an employer plan may limit when or how you can take distributions. Compare access alongside your expected income, other assets, and retirement timeline, and verify the rules with the plan administrator.

### Coordinate the choice with tax, retirement, and wealth planning

Treat a rollover as one part of your financial plan, not an isolated account decision. Consider its impact on taxes, investment costs, retirement income, Roth strategies, beneficiary choices, and how you manage other accounts. If you have worked for several employers or hold multiple types of workplace plans, consolidation may simplify oversight, but it could also mean giving up useful plan features.

Bring recent statements and plan documents to a financial or tax professional if you are comparing several options. ## What 401(k) Rollover Mistakes Should Doctors Avoid?

A job change can be a good time to review your retirement accounts, but rolling over a 401(k) is not automatically the best move. Your choice can affect account fees, investment options, access to your savings, and future tax strategies. Those details may matter especially for physicians moving between residency, fellowship, hospital employment, and private practice, or planning an early retirement.

Before you move money, compare the former employer’s plan, your new employer’s plan, and an IRA. Check each option’s fees, investment choices, withdrawal rules, and loan provisions. Also identify which contributions are vested and whether you have pre-tax and Roth funds. A direct rollover can help avoid mandatory withholding, but the funds still need to go to the correct type of account.

Think about your broader financial plans, too. A rollover could affect access to funds before retirement or the tax treatment of future backdoor Roth IRA contributions. Reviewing the decision alongside your [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) and tax plan can help you avoid a choice that creates complications later. Before submitting paperwork, watch for these common mistakes.

### Moving the money before they need to

Consolidating accounts can make them easier to manage, but moving your 401(k) too soon may affect when you can access the money. For example, if you leave your job during or after the calendar year you turn 55, you may qualify for penalty-free withdrawals from that employer’s 401(k), subject to plan rules. This age-55 exception generally does not apply to money rolled into an IRA. If you are considering early retirement or expect to use the account to cover a gap between jobs, keeping the funds in the former plan may be worth evaluating.

That does not mean you should always leave the account where it is. Compare its fees, investment choices, and withdrawal terms with your other options. The [IRS outlines exceptions to the early-distribution tax](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc558), including the separation-from-service rule. Ask the plan administrator how the rule applies before starting a rollover.

### Taking a check payable to themselves without planning for withholding and deadlines

If an eligible 401(k) distribution is paid to you instead of transferred directly to another retirement account, the plan generally must withhold 20% for federal income tax. You may still be able to complete a rollover within 60 days, but you would need to deposit the full distribution amount into the receiving account, including the amount withheld. If you deposit only the amount you received, the withheld portion may be taxable, and an additional tax may apply if you are under age 59½ and no exception applies.

A direct rollover can help avoid mandatory withholding. Ask the plan administrator to send the funds to the receiving plan or IRA, and confirm the payee instructions with both institutions. The [IRS rollover chart](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-tege/rollover_chart.pdf) explains which distributions may be rolled over. Review the transfer details before accepting a check made payable to you.

### Rolling pre-tax funds into an IRA without checking the backdoor Roth impact

Rolling pre-tax 401(k) funds into a traditional IRA may affect the tax calculation for a backdoor Roth IRA contribution. When you convert traditional IRA funds to a Roth IRA, the calculation generally considers the value of your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs, along with distributions and conversions during the year. A pre-tax IRA balance can therefore make part of a Roth conversion taxable. Employer 401(k) balances are not included in this IRA calculation.

Before choosing an IRA rollover, review your own IRA balances and planned contributions with a tax professional. Your spouse’s IRA balances are considered separately, rather than combined with yours for this calculation. You can also ask whether your new employer’s plan accepts incoming rollovers. The [IRS instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report nondeductible IRA contributions and Roth conversions. Consider the tax effects before moving pre-tax funds.

### Overlooking fees, investments, plan rules, vesting, or loan balances

A convenient transfer process does not necessarily mean the receiving account is a better fit. Compare account and investment fees, available investments, administrative services, and any advice included. Review rules for withdrawals and loans, too. A new employer’s plan may offer low-cost investment options, while an IRA may provide a broader selection. The details depend on the specific accounts.

Check your vesting status before leaving. Your salary deferrals are generally yours, but some employer contributions may be subject to a vesting schedule. Ask what happens to any unvested contributions when you leave. If you have an outstanding plan loan, ask how a job change affects repayment and whether an unpaid balance could be treated as a distribution or loan offset. Request the relevant plan documents and a clear explanation of fees, vesting, and loan procedures before moving the account.

### Assuming the new employer’s plan accepts rollovers

Not every employer plan accepts incoming rollovers, and some accept only certain account types or assets. A plan might accept pre-tax funds from a former 401(k) but have different rules for Roth 401(k) money. Even when rollovers are allowed, you may need to complete specific forms or follow instructions about where to send the funds.

Before requesting a distribution, ask the new plan administrator whether your account is eligible, which funds it can accept, and how to complete the transfer. Get the payee name, mailing address, and required paperwork in writing. If the plan does not accept your rollover, compare leaving the money in the former employer’s plan with moving eligible funds to an IRA. Confirming acceptance first can help prevent delays or a check being sent to the wrong place.

### Cashing out without weighing taxes, penalties, and long-term effects

Taking your 401(k) balance in cash may seem like a practical way to cover expenses during a job transition. But a cash distribution from a traditional 401(k) is generally included in taxable income. If you are under age 59½, an additional 10% tax may apply unless you qualify for an exception. Cashing out also removes money from a tax-advantaged account and ends its potential for future tax-deferred growth.

Before deciding, estimate how much you would receive after withholding, income taxes, and any applicable penalty. Consider whether cash reserves, a payment plan, or another source could cover the expense instead. Rules differ for Roth and pre-tax funds, and eligibility for exceptions depends on your circumstances. Review the [IRS guidance on early distributions](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc558) and consult a tax professional before treating your retirement balance as spendable cash.

### Failing to confirm the transfer or keep records

Submitting a rollover request does not confirm that the money reached the right account or was invested as you intended. Track the transfer with both plan administrators. Ask when the former plan expects to send the funds, whether the receiving account has received them, and whether investments were transferred in kind or sold before the transfer. Afterward, check the account type, amount, and investment instructions.

Keep copies of the rollover request, distribution paperwork, account statements, and transfer confirmations. You may also receive tax documents, such as Form 1099-R and, for an IRA, Form 5498. Review the forms against your records and ask a tax professional about anything that looks incorrect or unclear. Good documentation can help you resolve processing problems and report the transaction accurately.

### Ask a plan administrator, tax professional, or fiduciary financial planner for help when needed

A rollover can involve plan rules, tax reporting, early-withdrawal provisions, and future retirement decisions. The plan administrator can explain the plan’s procedures and features, but may not assess how a rollover fits your full financial situation. A tax professional can help you evaluate withholding, Roth conversions, and how an IRA balance could affect a backdoor Roth strategy.

A fiduciary financial planner can compare the former plan, new plan, and IRA in the context of your income, career plans, household finances, and retirement timeline. This may be particularly useful when changing employers, joining a practice, or coordinating retirement accounts with other benefits. Bring recent statements, plan documents, fee details, and information about any loans or expected withdrawals. A coordinated [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you assess the rollover as part of your broader financial plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I have to move my 401(k) when I change jobs?**\
No. If your former employer’s plan allows it, you can leave your savings there. You can also ask whether your new employer’s plan accepts rollovers or compare an IRA. Review the fees, investments, and access rules before choosing.

**Is a direct rollover usually better than having the money sent to me?**\
A direct rollover generally makes it easier to keep eligible savings in a retirement account and avoid mandatory withholding that may apply when a distribution is paid to you. Confirm the receiving account’s instructions with both plan administrators before starting.

**Could rolling my 401(k) into an IRA affect backdoor Roth IRA contributions?**\
It could. Pre-tax money in a traditional IRA may affect the tax calculation for a future Roth conversion. Check your IRA balances and plans for the year with a tax professional before moving pre-tax funds into an IRA.

**What should I do if I have a 401(k) loan when I leave my job?**\
Ask your plan administrator about repayment terms and deadlines before leaving or requesting a rollover. An unpaid loan may have tax consequences, so get the plan’s explanation in writing and review your options with a tax professional.

**How do I choose between my old plan, a new 401(k), and an IRA?**\
Compare fees, investment options, withdrawal rules, and any features you may need, such as access to funds before retirement. Also consider how the choice fits your tax plans and other workplace benefits. A financial or tax professional can help you review the details together.

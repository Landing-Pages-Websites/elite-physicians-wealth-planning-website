Physicians spend years learning how to care for patients, but medical training rarely covers the financial decisions that follow. Student loans, contract terms, retirement benefits, insurance, taxes, investments, and practice ownership can all shape your long-term security. A physician financial planner helps connect these decisions into one practical strategy. The goal is not simply to manage an investment account. It is to help you make thoughtful choices as your income, career, family, and responsibilities change. Whether you are a resident reviewing loan options, an attending physician building wealth, or a practice owner preparing for retirement, the right planner can help bring structure and clarity to your financial life.

## Key Takeaways

* **Prioritize medical-industry expertise**: Select a planner who understands physician compensation, student debt, benefits, insurance, practice ownership, and your current career stage.
* **Verify the relationship details**: Review fiduciary obligations, credentials, fees, conflicts, service scope, regulatory records, and communication practices before signing an agreement.
* **Look for coordinated, ongoing guidance**: Choose a planner who connects tax, investment, retirement, risk management, business, and estate decisions, then revisits the strategy as your circumstances change.

## What Is a Physician Financial Planner?

A physician financial planner helps medical professionals make coordinated decisions about income, taxes, investments, insurance, retirement, debt, business interests, and estate goals. Instead of treating each area as a separate task, the planner connects them into one strategy that reflects your career, family, risk tolerance, and long-term priorities.

That coordination matters because a physician’s financial life can change quickly. Residency ends, compensation rises, student loan payments begin, partnership opportunities appear, and practice ownership may become a possibility. Each decision can affect the others. For example, choosing a retirement plan may influence your tax bill, while purchasing into a practice may affect cash flow, debt capacity, and estate planning.

A physician financial planner may work directly with you or coordinate with your CPA, attorney, benefits team, insurance professionals, and practice consultants. The right professional should help you understand your options and implement a plan, not simply recommend investments. ### Physician Financial Planner vs. Traditional Financial Advisor

“Financial advisor” is a broad term. It can describe someone who manages investments, sells insurance, prepares financial plans, or provides a combination of services. A physician financial planner focuses on how the major parts of a doctor’s financial life work together.

Traditional advisors may serve clients across many professions and income levels. That does not automatically make them unqualified, but they may not understand physician-specific issues such as delayed peak earnings, student loan repayment options, productivity-based compensation, call pay, partnership tracks, or practice buy-ins.

A physician-focused planner should understand the financial decisions that arise throughout a medical career. Ask how many physicians the firm serves, whether it works with your specialty and practice model, and whether planning continues beyond portfolio management. Experience with physicians does not replace strong credentials or a fiduciary commitment, but it can make advice more relevant and easier to apply.

### Financial Planner vs. Investment Manager, CPA, and Insurance Agent

These professionals may all play important roles, but their responsibilities differ. An investment manager typically focuses on portfolio construction, investment selection, risk management, and account oversight. A CPA generally handles tax preparation, accounting, and tax compliance, although some CPAs also provide tax planning.

An insurance agent helps evaluate and arrange coverage, such as disability, life, or liability insurance. The agent may be paid through commissions, depending on the products and business model. A financial planner looks more broadly at how insurance fits with your income, family obligations, assets, debt, and future goals.

Some professionals provide more than one service, so ask exactly what the relationship includes. A planner may coordinate with your CPA and attorney rather than replace them. ### Why Physician-Specific Planning Matters

Physicians often earn substantial incomes, but high earnings do not make financial decisions simple. Medical professionals may face significant student debt, high malpractice exposure, demanding schedules, complicated benefits, irregular compensation, and limited time to review every financial choice.

A physician-specific planner understands that your needs can differ from those of a salaried professional with a predictable paycheck. Your plan may need to account for a 401(k), 403(b), defined benefit plan, backdoor Roth IRA strategy, employer stock, moonlighting income, bonuses, and business ownership. It may also need to address disability coverage that reflects your specialty and the duties you perform.

The right advice should reflect both your career and personal goals. A single physician with large loans may need a different strategy from a dual-physician household, a surgeon considering a partnership, or a specialist preparing to sell a practice. Physician-focused planning helps ensure that recommendations fit the way you earn, spend, protect, and build wealth.

### Account for Medical School Debt, Delayed Earnings, and Income Growth

Many physicians spend years in school and training before reaching their highest earning potential. During that time, interest may accumulate on student loans while retirement savings and other goals remain on hold. A financial planner can help you prioritize loan repayment, emergency reserves, insurance, and early investing without assuming that every dollar should go toward debt.

Your first attending position may create a sharp income increase. That transition can bring new choices, including refinancing, loan forgiveness, retirement contributions, taxable investing, home purchases, and lifestyle changes. A plan can help you decide what to do with additional income before it disappears into permanent expenses.

### Manage Multiple Income Sources, Complex Benefits, and Liability Risks

A physician may receive income from a hospital, private practice, academic appointment, locum tenens work, consulting, teaching, speaking, research, or investment activities. Compensation may include a salary, productivity incentives, call pay, bonuses, equity, partnership distributions, or benefits that differ across employers.

A planner should help you organize these income sources, understand withholding, and decide how each dollar supports your priorities. That may include choosing between retirement plans, setting aside money for estimated taxes, reviewing employee benefits, or building a reserve for irregular payments.

Liability risk adds another layer. Disability insurance, life insurance, umbrella liability coverage, malpractice protection, and business structures should be reviewed in relation to your income and responsibilities. Coverage should not be chosen in isolation. Your specialty, contract terms, family situation, assets, and practice ownership can all affect the type and amount of protection you need.

### Address Common Misconceptions About Physician Financial Planning

Financial planning is often confused with investment management or reserved for people who already have substantial assets. For physicians, useful planning may begin before a large portfolio exists. Early decisions about debt, insurance, taxes, cash flow, and retirement savings can influence your options for years.

The quality of advice depends on the planner’s scope, experience, compensation model, and willingness to explain recommendations. A planner should make complex choices clearer, identify tradeoffs, and help you follow through. Before hiring anyone, review the firm’s services, fees, regulatory disclosures, and approach to potential conflicts of interest.

#### Financial Planning Is Only for Wealthy Physicians

You do not need a large investment portfolio to benefit from financial planning. Residents and fellows may need help with student loans, disability coverage, emergency savings, contract review, and initial retirement contributions. Early-career physicians may need to decide how to divide a higher income among debt repayment, investing, homeownership, and family goals.

Waiting until you feel wealthy can leave important decisions unaddressed. The cost of an insurance mistake, missed tax opportunity, or poorly structured employment agreement may be significant even before you have accumulated substantial assets.

Look for a planner whose fee structure fits your current situation. Some firms offer hourly, project-based, subscription, or flat-fee planning instead of requiring a large investment account. Ask what the first engagement includes and whether you can receive planning without transferring your investments.

#### Financial Planners Only Manage Investments

Investments are one part of a financial plan, not the entire plan. A physician financial planner may also review cash flow, taxes, student loans, insurance, employee benefits, retirement income, practice ownership, and estate goals.

That broader view can prevent disconnected decisions. An investment recommendation may look reasonable until you consider a pending practice buy-in, a large tax payment, or the need for additional disability coverage. A planner should explain how each recommendation affects your other priorities.

Also ask whether the firm coordinates with your existing CPA, attorney, and benefits professionals. If the conversation focuses almost entirely on investment performance, you may not be receiving comprehensive planning.

#### You Need a Financial Plan Only Once

A financial plan is not a document you create once and file away. Your income, tax situation, family, benefits, debt, practice, and goals can change throughout your career. A new job, marriage, child, partnership offer, business expansion, divorce, inheritance, or approaching retirement may require a fresh review.

Ongoing planning does not mean making constant changes to your investments. Often, it means checking whether your decisions still support your priorities and making adjustments when circumstances change. A planner may review your savings rate, insurance, tax strategy, retirement projections, estate documents, and beneficiary designations on a regular schedule.

Ask how often meetings occur, what is reviewed between meetings, and whether implementation support is included. #### Professional Advice Costs Too Much

Professional advice is an expense, but that does not mean it lacks value. The better question is whether the planner’s services address decisions that matter to you and whether the cost is clear before you begin. Compare the fee with the scope of work, access to the planner, coordination provided, and implementation support.

Ask whether the firm charges a flat annual fee, hourly rate, project fee, subscription, assets-under-management fee, commissions, or a combination. Also ask about minimums and extra charges for tax planning, insurance analysis, business planning, or estate coordination.

A lower fee is not always the best value, and a higher fee does not guarantee better advice. Focus on transparency, relevant experience, and the quality of the relationship. You should understand what you are paying for, which decisions the planner will help with, and how the firm is compensated when recommending financial products.

## Which Credentials and Fiduciary Standards Matter?

Credentials and fiduciary standards can help you compare financial planners, but they should not be your only criteria. A designation may demonstrate education, training, or experience. It does not automatically mean a planner understands physician compensation, medical practice ownership, student loan decisions, or the financial demands of your specialty.

Review the planner’s credentials, services, compensation model, and regulatory disclosures. Then ask how those qualifications apply to your situation. A planner who primarily serves residents may have different experience than one who advises surgeons nearing retirement or physicians preparing to buy into a practice.

Fiduciary responsibility also deserves careful attention. A fiduciary must act in the client’s best interest within the scope of the relationship. However, that responsibility may apply only to certain services or accounts. Ask when fiduciary duty applies, how the firm is paid, and whether product recommendations create potential conflicts.

You can also check whether an investment professional is registered and review the firm’s public disclosures. The SEC’s investment adviser resources explain how registered investment advisers operate and where investors can find information about their services, fees, and conflicts.

### Consider CFP®, CFA®, CPA/PFS, and Other Relevant Credentials

Several professional designations may be relevant when you evaluate a physician financial planner. A **CERTIFIED FINANCIAL PLANNER™ professional** completes education, examination, experience, and ethics requirements through the [CFP Board](https://www.cfp.net/). CFP® professionals often advise on multiple areas, including cash flow, taxes, investments, insurance, and retirement.

A **CFA® charterholder** generally has extensive training in investment analysis and portfolio management. This background may be useful when your primary need involves portfolio construction or investment strategy. A **CPA/PFS** combines accounting expertise with personal financial planning, which may help physicians with complex tax situations or business interests.

Other designations, such as CIMA®, CRPC®, CPFA®, and CRPS®, may indicate specialized knowledge in investment management, retirement planning, fiduciary oversight, or retirement plan design. Ask what each credential requires, whether it remains current, and how it relates to your needs. Credentials matter most when paired with physician-specific experience and sound judgment.

### Understand What Fiduciary Responsibility Means for Physicians

A fiduciary financial planner has a responsibility to put your interests ahead of the firm’s interests when providing services covered by that duty. For physicians, those services may involve investments, retirement accounts, insurance, tax-sensitive strategies, or business decisions. The exact obligation depends on the firm’s registration, agreement, and services.

Ask the planner to explain fiduciary responsibility in plain language. Find out whether the firm recommends the best available option, a suitable option, or products available through its own platform. The SEC’s explanation of investment adviser fiduciary duty provides additional background on the obligations of registered investment advisers.

Fiduciary status does not guarantee investment returns or eliminate risk. It means the planner should provide advice based on your objectives, disclose relevant conflicts, and avoid placing compensation ahead of your interests. A trustworthy planner should welcome detailed questions about these responsibilities.

### Confirm When Fiduciary Duty Applies

Some planners act as fiduciaries throughout their relationship with a client. Others act as fiduciaries only when providing investment advice, while following a different standard when selling insurance or other financial products. This distinction can be easy to miss unless you ask directly.

During your initial meeting, ask, “Are you a fiduciary at all times, or only for specific services?” Then ask whether the firm’s employees, affiliated professionals, and outside product providers follow the same standard. Request the answer in writing through the advisory agreement or disclosure documents.

Also confirm which legal entity provides each service. A firm may offer financial planning through one company, investment management through another, and insurance through an affiliated agency. Those arrangements may involve different duties and compensation. Clear answers help you understand when the planner is advising you and when the firm may be acting as a product provider.

### Compare Fee-Only, Fee-Based, and Commission-Based Relationships

A **fee-only** planner receives compensation directly from clients and does not earn commissions from selling financial products. The fee may be hourly, project-based, subscription-based, or tied to the assets the firm manages. This structure can make compensation easier to understand, but you should still review the complete fee schedule.

A **fee-based** planner may receive client fees and commissions, depending on the services provided. A **commission-based** professional is generally paid when a client purchases certain insurance or investment products. Commission compensation is not automatically improper, but it can create incentives that deserve a closer look.

Ask how much the planner earns from your relationship, who pays the firm, and whether compensation changes based on the product selected. The SEC’s Investor.gov guide to working with investment professionals can help you compare common compensation arrangements. Focus on transparency and the total cost, not just the label used to describe the fee model.

### Check Registered Investment Adviser Status and Regulatory Disclosures

If a firm provides investment advice for compensation, check whether it is registered with the SEC or a state securities regulator. Registration does not guarantee good advice or investment results, but it gives you access to information about the firm’s services, fees, ownership, conflicts, and disciplinary history.

Use the SEC’s [Investment Adviser Public Disclosure database](https://adviserinfo.sec.gov/) to search for the firm and individual investment professionals. Review the firm’s current Form ADV, including Part 2A, which describes its business practices, fees, conflicts, and disciplinary information. Check whether the person you plan to work with appears in the firm’s filings.

Pay attention to the firm’s legal name, registration status, and affiliated companies. A website may use a brand name that differs from the entity listed in regulatory records. Ask the planner to explain any differences, especially if separate entities provide planning, investment, insurance, or tax-related services.

### Review Proprietary Products, Insurance Commissions, and Other Conflicts of Interest

A conflict of interest exists when a planner’s financial incentives could influence a recommendation. Common examples include proprietary investments, insurance commissions, referral fees, revenue-sharing arrangements, and sales bonuses. These arrangements do not automatically make advice unsuitable, but you deserve a clear explanation before proceeding.

Ask whether the firm recommends investments or insurance products that it owns, manages, distributes, or receives compensation for selling. Physicians often need substantial disability and life insurance coverage, so ask whether the planner compares policies from multiple carriers or primarily uses one company.

You should also ask whether the firm receives payments from custodians, mutual fund companies, retirement plan providers, or other partners. Review how the planner addresses these conflicts and whether firm policies limit product recommendations. A strong response will be specific, written, and easy to understand, rather than a general statement that the firm always acts in your best interest.

### Read Form ADV, Form CRS, and the Advisory Agreement

Before hiring an investment adviser, review Form ADV and Form CRS. **Form ADV Part 2A** provides detailed information about the firm’s services, fees, conflicts, and disciplinary history. **Form CRS** offers a shorter summary of the relationship, including services, costs, conflicts, and questions to ask.

The SEC’s Form CRS investor guide explains what this document should contain and how to use it. Compare these disclosures with what the planner told you. If the documents and conversation do not match, ask for clarification before signing an agreement.

The advisory agreement should identify the services provided, fee calculation, billing schedule, termination terms, investment authority, and responsibilities of each party. Read the sections covering custody, discretionary authority, third-party fees, and conflicts. If a clause is unclear, ask for an explanation in writing. You may also want an attorney to review the agreement if it involves a practice, partnership, or substantial investment commitment.

### Review Fee Schedules, Privacy Policies, and Disciplinary Disclosures

A fee schedule should show how much you will pay and how the firm calculates the charge. Look for separate costs related to financial planning, investment management, account custody, trading, fund expenses, insurance, tax preparation, or outside professionals. Ask whether fees change as your portfolio grows, your services expand, or your assets move between account types.

Privacy policies explain how the firm collects, uses, stores, and shares your personal information. This matters when you provide tax returns, employment contracts, insurance records, medical practice information, and estate documents. Ask how the firm protects sensitive data and which technology or custodial providers can access it.

You should also review disciplinary disclosures and client complaints listed in regulatory filings. A disclosure does not necessarily mean the firm is unsuitable, but it deserves context. Ask what happened, how the matter was resolved, and what the firm changed afterward. Direct answers are more useful than a polished marketing message that avoids specifics.

### Evaluate Credentials Alongside Experience and Fit

A credential can confirm that a planner completed a defined course of study, but it cannot tell you how well that person communicates or applies advice to a physician’s circumstances. Experience may matter more when you have an unusual compensation model, a partnership opportunity, a complex practice, or several competing financial priorities.

Ask how many physicians the planner serves and whether the firm works with professionals at your career stage. A resident may need help with loan repayment, insurance, and cash flow. An established practice owner may need tax planning, retirement plan design, valuation, and succession advice. The planner should explain how the process changes for each situation.

Fit also includes communication style, meeting frequency, technology, and follow-through. You will likely share sensitive information and make significant decisions over several years. Choose someone who listens carefully, explains tradeoffs without pressure, coordinates with your other professionals, and gives you a clear process for turning recommendations into action.

## What Does a Physician Financial Planner Cost?

The cost of working with a physician financial planner depends on the type of advice you need, the complexity of your finances, and the firm’s pricing model. A resident managing student loans may need a focused plan for cash flow, insurance, and benefits. A practice owner may need ongoing guidance for business finances, tax planning, retirement plan design, and succession. Those services require different levels of time and expertise.

Your portfolio size also affects the cost. Some planners charge a percentage of the assets they manage, while others use a flat fee, annual retainer, hourly rate, or project fee. A planner may also combine these approaches, such as charging an ongoing planning fee plus separate investment management costs.

When comparing firms, look beyond the advertised price. Ask how often you will meet, whether the planner helps implement recommendations, and whether coordination with your CPA and attorney is included. Confirm whether you will pay separate costs for investment funds, insurance products, tax preparation, or practice-related projects. A lower stated fee may not offer better value if it covers only limited investment oversight.

The right arrangement should match your career stage, investable assets, income, business interests, and need for ongoing support. Understanding the common fee structures can help you compare proposals more clearly.

### Compare Flat-Fee and Annual-Retainer Planning

A flat-fee or annual-retainer arrangement charges a set amount for defined planning services. Based on fees listed in the [White Coat Investor’s advisor directory](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/financial-advisors/), many annual flat fees fall between approximately $5,000 and $15,000, although pricing varies by firm and scope.

This model offers predictable costs and does not automatically rise as your investment portfolio grows. It may work well for physicians who want advice across several areas, including tax planning, retirement, insurance, student loans, investments, and estate planning. It can also suit practice owners who need continuing guidance on compensation, buy-ins, business decisions, and succession.

Before signing, ask exactly what the retainer includes. Does the planner help implement recommendations? Will they coordinate with your CPA and attorney? Are additional meetings or complex projects billed separately? Clear answers help you understand whether the annual fee reflects the level of service you need.

### Understand Assets-Under-Management Fees

Assets-under-management, or AUM, fees are based on the value of the investment accounts a planner manages. The firm typically charges an annual percentage, collected monthly or quarterly. For example, a 1% annual fee on a $500,000 portfolio would equal $5,000 per year, before considering other investment expenses.

Ask whether the firm uses graduated rates, a flat percentage, or a minimum annual fee. Confirm which accounts count toward the calculation and whether financial planning is included. Some firms include broad planning services, while others charge separately for tax strategy, retirement analysis, insurance reviews, or practice planning.

AUM pricing may be reasonable when you have a smaller portfolio and want investment management combined with planning. As your assets grow, however, a flat-fee or hourly arrangement may cost less than a percentage-based fee. The SEC’s investor education resources can help you review questions about investment professionals, compensation, and advisory relationships.

### Consider Hourly, Project-Based, and Subscription Planning

Hourly planning charges for the time a planner spends on your questions or financial analysis. This approach may work when you need help with one decision, such as reviewing an employment agreement, comparing loan repayment strategies, or evaluating a practice buy-in. It can also provide an introduction to a planner’s process before you enter an ongoing relationship.

Project-based planning uses a defined fee for a specific deliverable. A physician might commission a retirement income analysis, insurance review, tax strategy, or financial plan after receiving a new compensation package. Ask what the final deliverable includes and what happens if the project requires additional work.

Subscription planning usually involves a recurring monthly or quarterly fee for defined services and limited access to the planner. Before choosing this model, ask how quickly the planner responds, how many meetings are included, and whether major decisions fall outside the subscription. You should also understand how the firm bills work that exceeds the original scope.

### Review Commission-Based Compensation and Embedded Product Costs

Some advisors receive commissions when clients purchase investments, insurance policies, annuities, or other financial products. The provider may pay the commission directly, so you might not see it as a separate invoice. However, the cost can still affect the product’s expenses, surrender terms, or long-term value.

Ask the planner to explain every source of compensation in plain language. Find out whether the firm can recommend products from multiple providers, whether it sells proprietary products, and whether it offers a direct payment option for advice. You should also ask whether the planner receives different compensation for recommending one product over another.

Insurance can be an important part of a physician’s plan, particularly for disability, life, and liability protection. Still, review the policy costs, commissions, exclusions, surrender charges, and available alternatives before purchasing. A recommendation should address your needs first, not the compensation attached to a product.

### Check Asset Minimums, Service Limits, and Additional Fees

Some firms require a minimum investment balance before accepting a client. Others charge a minimum annual fee regardless of your portfolio size. These requirements can affect residents, fellows, early-career physicians, and medical professionals with high incomes but limited investable assets.

Read the service agreement carefully and look for costs outside the primary planning fee. These may include account management, financial plan updates, tax preparation, hourly work, third-party investment expenses, insurance services, or support during a practice transaction. A firm may also charge separately for work involving a divorce, inheritance, business sale, or partnership change.

Ask what happens when your financial needs become more complex. Will the planner provide the additional service under your existing agreement, or will you receive a separate project proposal? The answer can affect your total cost over time. A detailed fee schedule gives you a clearer basis for comparing firms and planning for future expenses.

### Compare Total Costs With the Services Provided

The stated fee does not tell you the full cost or value of a financial planning relationship. For example, comparing a 1% AUM fee with a $10,000 annual retainer requires you to consider your portfolio size, additional charges, and the services included in each arrangement.

One firm may provide investment management and two annual meetings. Another may coordinate tax planning, retirement projections, insurance reviews, student loan decisions, and practice planning throughout the year. The second firm may charge more, but the broader service could be more useful if your financial life includes several connected decisions.

Start by listing the advice you need now and may need soon. Then ask each firm whether it provides recommendations, implementation support, progress monitoring, and coordination with your other professionals. Review the firm’s [Form ADV and advisory disclosures](https://adviserinfo.sec.gov/) to understand its services, fees, business practices, and potential conflicts.

### Match the Fee Structure to Your Portfolio, Income, and Planning Needs

Your preferred fee model should reflect more than your current investment balance. A resident may need targeted guidance on student loans, insurance, cash flow, and employer benefits. An attending physician may need help with tax planning, retirement contributions, compensation changes, and lifestyle decisions. A practice owner may need advice for both household and business finances.

Consider how often you expect to need advice. A project fee may suit one major decision, while an annual retainer may provide better continuity during a partnership transition, practice expansion, or ownership change. AUM fees may be convenient when you want one firm to manage investments and provide planning, but review how the cost changes as your assets grow.

Choose several firms that serve your career stage, specialty, practice model, and planning priorities. Ask each one to explain why its fee structure fits your situation. A thoughtful explanation can reveal whether the planner understands your needs or is simply applying a standard pricing model.

### Watch for Physician Financial Planner Red Flags

A planner does not need to share every opinion you have about investing or risk. However, certain behaviors should prompt more questions or a decision to look elsewhere. Pay attention to how the planner discusses fees, products, credentials, risks, and the limits of their expertise.

Be cautious if a planner promises guaranteed investment returns, suggests that losses are impossible, or presents a single product as the answer to every concern. Investments involve risk, and legitimate recommendations should explain both potential benefits and limitations.

Pressure to act immediately is another warning sign. You should have time to review fees, risks, surrender charges, tax consequences, and alternatives. Ask whether the planner receives compensation for recommending a particular insurance policy, investment, annuity, or other financial product.

A planner should be able to explain the fee structure in plain language. If the firm avoids giving you a written fee schedule, uses unclear terms, or cannot tell you what is included, do not assume the details will become clearer after you become a client.

Review the advisory agreement before signing. Look for billing frequency, termination terms, account minimums, additional planning fees, custody arrangements, and third-party compensation. Ask for clarification on every item you do not understand.

A generalist may offer sound advice, but limited experience with physicians can create blind spots. If the planner does not understand physician compensation, student debt, benefits, malpractice exposure, partnership tracks, or practice ownership, you may spend significant time explaining the context behind each decision.

Ask direct questions about the firm’s medical client base and experience with your career stage. A planner should be comfortable discussing the financial details that commonly affect physicians while acknowledging when a CPA, attorney, or other specialist should be involved.

A portfolio review is only one part of physician financial planning. A strong plan should also consider taxes, debt, insurance, retirement income, estate documents, cash flow, and professional decisions. For practice owners, it should include business finances and succession planning.

If the first meeting immediately focuses on moving your assets or selecting investments, ask how the firm addresses the rest of your financial life. Investment recommendations should support your broader goals, not replace the planning process.

A physician financial planner connects decisions involving taxes, investments, debt, insurance, retirement, practice ownership, and estate goals. The planner may also coordinate with your CPA, attorney, benefits team, and other professionals so recommendations work together.

**When should a physician hire a financial planner?**\
You do not need to wait until you have substantial assets. Residents may need help with student loans, disability insurance, cash flow, and retirement benefits. Established physicians and practice owners may need support with tax planning, investments, partnership decisions, business finances, or retirement income.

**How much does a physician financial planner cost?**\
Fees vary based on the firm, services, and complexity of your situation. Common arrangements include flat fees, annual retainers, hourly or project-based pricing, assets-under-management fees, and commissions. Ask for a complete written fee schedule, including charges for tax, insurance, practice, or estate-related work.

Ask how many physicians the firm serves, whether it understands your specialty and career stage, which services are included, how the planner is compensated, and when fiduciary responsibility applies. You should also ask how the firm works with your CPA and attorney and whether it provides help implementing recommendations.

**Can a financial planner help with practice ownership and retirement?**\
Yes, if those services are part of the firm’s expertise. A physician-focused planner may help evaluate a practice buy-in, review business cash flow, plan owner compensation, prepare for succession, and connect the value of your practice with your personal retirement strategy.

For physicians, retirement may involve more than leaving a job. You might sell a practice, change ownership, continue consulting, or reduce your clinical hours. Each decision can affect when your current insurance ends, what income you report, and which coverage choices are available. Health care options for early retirement should be part of the transition plan, not a last-minute task. Compare Marketplace coverage, COBRA, a spouse’s plan, and any benefits tied to part-time work. Then account for premiums and likely out-of-pocket costs in your retirement projections. Coordinating coverage with income and tax planning can give you a clearer picture of the financial impact.

## Key Takeaways

* **Compare the full cost of each plan**: Weigh premiums alongside deductibles, prescriptions, provider access, and out-of-pocket limits.
* **Match coverage to your retirement timeline**: Confirm when current benefits end, check enrollment deadlines, and compare options for your household.
* **Connect health care costs to your financial plan**: Include insurance expenses, changing income, and practice transitions in retirement projections.

## What Health Coverage Can Early Retirees Choose?

Retiring before age 65 can leave a gap between employer-sponsored health coverage and Medicare. You may be able to fill it with an ACA Marketplace plan, a spouse’s employer plan, COBRA, or Medicaid. Retiree benefits and part-time work with health coverage may also be options. Short-term plans and medical cost-sharing arrangements are sometimes considered, but they may not offer the same protections as comprehensive health insurance.

Start by checking when your current coverage ends and when a new plan could begin. Then compare monthly premiums alongside deductibles, copays, coinsurance, out-of-pocket limits, provider networks, and prescription coverage. A plan with a lower premium may cost more overall if you need regular specialist visits or ongoing medication.

Your household income and work plans can affect both your coverage choices and the help available to pay for them. For physicians, a retirement date, practice sale, consulting work, or change in a spouse’s employment can shift the numbers. Include coverage costs and income assumptions in your retirement projections. [Physician-focused retirement planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help coordinate these decisions with your broader financial plan.

### Compare ACA Marketplace and off-Marketplace plans

An ACA Marketplace plan is a common option for people retiring before Medicare eligibility. Marketplace plans cover essential health benefits and cannot deny coverage or charge more because of a preexisting condition. Depending on your household income and other factors, you may qualify for a premium tax credit that reduces your monthly premium. Use the HealthCare.gov Marketplace to compare plans and check whether financial assistance may be available.

You can also buy individual coverage directly from an insurer, known as off-Marketplace coverage. Some plans sold directly meet ACA requirements, but premium tax credits are available only for plans purchased through the Marketplace. Before choosing either route, compare the deductible, out-of-pocket maximum, provider network, and prescription formulary, not just the premium. Confirm that your doctors and medications are covered before enrolling.

### Consider a spouse’s employer plan, retiree benefits, or part-time work with health coverage

If your spouse is still working, ask whether you can join their employer plan and what the added premium would be. Compare the employee-plus-spouse cost, deductible, network, and coverage rules with your other options. A familiar provider network can be valuable, but the plan’s details matter more than the monthly premium alone. [Planning for health care in retirement](https://www.ffcommunity.com/planning-for-healthcare-in-retirement) includes reviewing a spouse’s workplace coverage as one possible source of insurance.

Ask your former employer whether it offers retiree health benefits, and confirm who qualifies, what the coverage costs, and how long it lasts. Part-time work may also provide access to an employer plan. Before taking a job for its benefits, check the required hours, waiting period, employee contribution, and whether the plan covers your spouse or dependents.

### Review COBRA and state continuation coverage

COBRA may allow you to keep your employer health plan after retirement or another qualifying event. Coverage often lasts up to 18 months, though certain circumstances can allow an extension. You generally pay the full premium, including the amount your employer previously contributed, plus an administrative fee of up to 2%. The [U.S. Department of Labor’s COBRA guidance](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/health-plans/cobra) explains key eligibility and election rules.

Some states offer continuation coverage for workers who are not covered by federal COBRA or whose plans qualify under state law. Rules, deadlines, and coverage periods vary. Treat continuation coverage as a possible bridge, and check the date it ends before relying on it. Compare its full cost with Marketplace plans, and pay close attention to election and payment deadlines so you do not lose the option.

### Check Medicaid and state programs if you qualify

Medicaid may offer coverage if your income and other circumstances meet your state’s eligibility rules. Requirements vary, and household size, income, age, disability status, and other factors may affect eligibility. If your income changes after retirement, check again rather than assuming you are ineligible based on your former salary. The Medicaid state directory can help you find your state agency’s information.

Your state may also offer programs for people who need health coverage or related services. Contact the state health or social services agency to ask what is available and how to apply. If you also apply for Marketplace coverage, report household income accurately and update it when circumstances change. This helps keep your coverage and any financial assistance aligned with your current situation.

### Understand the limits of short-term plans and medical cost-sharing arrangements

Short-term health plans may have lower premiums, but they are not a substitute for comprehensive ACA-compliant coverage. Depending on the plan and state rules, they may exclude preexisting conditions, limit benefits, or omit services such as prescription drugs and preventive care. Read the plan’s exclusions and benefit limits carefully, especially if you have an ongoing condition or expect treatment.

Medical cost-sharing arrangements are also different from insurance. Members contribute toward eligible medical bills, but payment may not be guaranteed, and these arrangements may not follow the same consumer protections as regulated health plans. Ask what expenses qualify, whether payment limits apply, and what happens if a bill is not shared. A lower monthly contribution does not necessarily mean less financial risk if you need specialist care or costly medication.

### Use HSAs for eligible expenses, but don’t treat them as health insurance

A Health Savings Account (HSA) can help you save for qualified medical expenses, but it does not provide insurance or guarantee payment for care. In general, you can contribute only if you have an HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan and meet other IRS requirements. Check the rules before contributing if you are changing plans or nearing Medicare eligibility.

You can use HSA funds for qualified expenses, and eligible withdrawals are generally tax-free. An existing balance may help cover health costs in retirement, but it will not limit what you owe in the way an insurance policy’s out-of-pocket maximum can. Keep records of expenses and review the [IRS rules for HSAs](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969). When comparing coverage, evaluate the insurance plan and HSA separately, then include both in your budget.

## How Does COBRA Work, and When Should You Consider It?

COBRA can temporarily continue your employer-sponsored health coverage after you retire, leave a job, or experience another qualifying event. For physicians retiring before Medicare eligibility, it may provide a bridge while you compare longer-term options. The tradeoffs are cost and duration: you may pay the full premium, and coverage generally ends after a limited period.

Consider COBRA alongside Marketplace plans, a spouse’s employer coverage, or other options. The right choice depends on your budget, timing, and care needs. Include health coverage in your [retirement plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians), particularly if leaving work or selling a practice will also change your household income.

### Check eligibility, election deadlines, and payment rules

Federal COBRA generally applies to group health plans offered by private-sector employers and state or local governments with at least 20 employees. Smaller employers may be subject to state continuation laws, so ask your plan administrator which rules apply.

If eligible, you generally have 60 days to elect COBRA. The election period starts on the later of the date your coverage would end or the date you receive the election notice. After electing, you usually have 45 days to make your first payment. Confirm your deadlines and payment instructions with the plan administrator, and keep copies of notices and receipts. The [Department of Labor’s COBRA guide](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/laws-and-regulations/laws/cobra) provides more detail on eligibility and election rules.

### Review coverage duration and possible extensions

COBRA coverage commonly lasts up to 18 months after a qualifying event, such as job loss or a reduction in work hours. In certain circumstances, coverage for a spouse or dependent may continue for up to 36 months, including after the covered employee’s death or a divorce.

A disability determination may extend some coverage periods, but notice deadlines and other requirements apply. Ask your plan administrator to confirm the end date for each person covered and whether an extension may be available. Since COBRA is temporary, mark the end date and start comparing replacement plans well ahead of time. This gives you room to review networks, costs, and enrollment dates without rushing near the end of coverage.

### Budget for the full premium and administrative fee, and plan for coverage to end

After leaving an employer, you may lose the employer contribution toward your health insurance. With COBRA, you can generally be charged the full plan premium plus an administrative fee, often totaling up to 102% of the plan’s cost. Request the monthly price for each coverage option, including family coverage if needed.

Add premiums to your budget alongside deductibles, copays, coinsurance, and prescription costs. If you’re retiring from or selling a medical practice, coordinate your coverage dates with the ownership transition and your cash flow. Review how insurance expenses fit with household income and withdrawals as part of your [tax planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians).

### Weigh COBRA’s cost and limited duration against other options

COBRA can be useful when you need temporary coverage, but paying the full premium may make it more expensive than other choices. Compare its monthly and likely annual cost with Marketplace plans, a spouse’s employer plan, or retiree coverage. If you have access to a new employer plan, check when coverage would begin and when you need to enroll.

Look beyond the premium. Compare deductibles, prescription costs, provider networks, and expected out-of-pocket spending for your household. A lower-cost plan may still cost more overall if it covers less of the care you expect to use. If you’re considering Marketplace coverage, check plan options and financial assistance through HealthCare.gov.

### Compare provider access and treatment continuity with other plans

COBRA usually lets you continue with the same employer health plan, which may help you keep your current doctors and specialists. That can be valuable if you or a family member is in active treatment, takes specific medications, or wants to avoid a provider change during retirement.

Confirm that your doctors, hospitals, and prescriptions are covered under the exact plan you would continue. Then compare those details with other options, including network limits and prior authorization rules. If you’re leaving a hospital or transitioning out of practice ownership, check coverage end dates early. Planning ahead can help prevent a gap in coverage and give you time to arrange ongoing care.

## How Do Marketplace Plans Work for Early Retirees?

If you retire before becoming eligible for Medicare and lose employer-sponsored coverage, an Affordable Care Act (ACA) Marketplace plan may help cover the gap. You can compare plans by monthly premium, expected out-of-pocket costs, provider access, and prescription coverage. Depending on your household income and other eligibility factors, you may also qualify for financial assistance.

Start comparing options before your current coverage ends. A low premium may come with a high deductible, while a plan with a higher premium may fit better if you expect regular care or use costly medications. Check the plan’s details against your own needs, including those of a spouse or dependents who will need coverage.

For physicians, timing can be especially important when retirement coincides with a practice sale, ownership change, or shift to consulting work. Include the coverage start date and estimated costs in your transition plan, and consider how withdrawals or work income could affect financial assistance. A coordinated [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help bring these decisions together.

### Get essential health benefits and preexisting-condition protections

Marketplace plans cover essential health benefits, including hospital care, emergency services, prescription drugs, and preventive care. They also cover preexisting conditions. Insurers can’t deny you coverage or charge you more because of your health history. However, plans can differ in which specific services they cover and how much you pay for care.

Review a plan’s Summary of Benefits and Coverage before enrolling. Check for limits, exclusions, referral requirements, and cost-sharing that may apply to treatments you expect to use. The Marketplace’s overview of covered services explains which benefit categories plans must include. If you have an ongoing condition or see specialists regularly, confirm that your care and providers are covered under the specific plan you’re considering.

### Compare premiums and cost-sharing across Bronze, Silver, Gold, and Platinum plans

Marketplace plans are grouped into four metal tiers: Bronze, Silver, Gold, and Platinum. The tiers describe how you and the insurer generally share costs for covered care, not the quality of care you’ll receive. Bronze plans typically have lower monthly premiums and higher costs when you use services. Platinum plans generally have higher premiums and lower costs at the point of care, while Silver and Gold plans fall between them.

Compare each plan’s deductible, copays, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket maximum, as well as its premium. The Marketplace’s guide to plan categories explains how these tiers work. Then estimate your total costs based on the care you expect, such as routine appointments, specialist visits, procedures, and prescriptions. A higher-premium plan may make sense if you anticipate using more care, but compare the numbers for your situation rather than relying on the tier alone.

### Check your eligibility for premium tax credits and cost-sharing reductions

Depending on your household income and other eligibility rules, you may qualify for a premium tax credit that reduces your monthly premium. You may also qualify for cost-sharing reductions, which lower costs such as deductibles and copays. Cost-sharing reductions are available only if you enroll in a Silver plan, so compare Silver options if you appear eligible.

Use the Marketplace application to check whether your household qualifies for assistance. The amount available depends on your circumstances and the plans offered where you live. If you receive advance premium tax credits, report changes to your income or household during the year. The credit is reconciled when you file your federal tax return, so changes in your final income may affect the amount you owe or receive.

### Plan household income and taxes to understand how they affect financial assistance

The Marketplace uses projected annual household income to assess eligibility for financial assistance. For early retirees, income may come from several sources, including consulting work, investment income, retirement account withdrawals, or a medical practice. The Marketplace’s income calculation follows specific rules, so don’t assume it is the same as the taxable income figure on your return.

Estimate your income for the full coverage year and update your application if circumstances change. An estimate that differs significantly from your actual income can affect the premium tax credit you receive and the amount reconciled at tax time. Review the Marketplace’s income guidance when preparing your estimate. If you’re deciding when to take withdrawals or continue working part time, consider how those choices may affect both taxes and health coverage assistance.

### Meet enrollment deadlines and identify qualifying life events

You can generally sign up for a Marketplace plan during the annual Open Enrollment Period. If you lose job-based coverage outside that period, you may qualify for a Special Enrollment Period. Certain other life changes, such as marriage or a move, may also qualify, depending on the circumstances.

Check your enrollment window before your current coverage ends. Special Enrollment Periods have specific eligibility rules and deadlines, and your new coverage start date can depend on when you enroll and why you qualify. Review the Marketplace’s Special Enrollment Period rules and confirm your dates before leaving work or ending employer coverage. If you’re planning a practice sale or retirement, include the application timeline in your transition checklist so you can reduce the risk of a gap in coverage.

### Check provider networks, prescription formularies, and local plan availability

Marketplace options vary by location, so plans available in one county may not be offered in another. Before enrolling, check whether your primary care clinician, specialists, preferred hospitals, and pharmacies are in the plan’s network. Review the prescription formulary, too. It lists covered medications and may include restrictions such as prior authorization or step therapy.

Use the insurer’s provider directory to research your options, then confirm important details with both the insurer and the provider’s office. Networks can change, and a clinician may accept one plan from an insurer but not another. The Marketplace plan comparison tool lets you review plans available in your area and check prescription coverage. If you need ongoing treatment, ask the insurer how your care will be covered after the switch from your current plan.

## What Do Health Insurance Plans Cost Before Medicare?

Health insurance can be a significant expense if you retire before age 65. Your costs depend on factors such as your age, location, household size, plan choice, income, and health needs. The monthly premium is only one part of the budget: deductibles, copays, coinsurance, prescriptions, and services your plan doesn’t cover can add substantially to your annual spending.

For physicians, it helps to include these expenses in your retirement projections before leaving a job, selling a practice, or changing ownership. Estimate costs for each year until Medicare eligibility, and account for coverage for your spouse and dependents. Your income during this period may also affect eligibility for Marketplace financial assistance, so consider how retirement income and withdrawals fit into your health coverage estimate.

The sections below outline the main costs to review. Use them to compare plans based on your household’s needs, rather than relying on a single premium estimate.

### Compare premiums based on age, location, and plan choice

Marketplace premiums vary by age, location, household size, and plan. Prices and available options can differ between states and counties, so check plans where you live. Your household income may also affect eligibility for financial assistance and the amount you pay.

For example, one [pre-Medicare health care cost analysis](https://www.heydayretirement.com/heyday-blogs/retirement-risk-11-health-care-costs-up-to-age-65) estimates that a 62-year-old couple could pay about $1,501 to $1,554 per month for sample Bronze plans, with a $16,300 deductible. At those premium levels, three years of coverage would cost roughly $54,000 before deductibles, treatment, or prescriptions. These are illustrative figures, not quotes. Compare current plans and costs for each member of your household.

### Budget for deductibles, copays, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket maximums

Your premium doesn’t tell you what you’ll spend when you receive care. A deductible is the amount you generally pay for covered services before your plan begins paying its share. Copays are fixed fees for certain services, while coinsurance is a percentage of the cost. The out-of-pocket maximum limits what you pay for covered, in-network care during the plan year. It generally doesn’t include premiums or services the plan excludes.

Estimate costs for both a typical year and a year with more care than expected. A lower-premium plan may have a higher deductible or more cost-sharing. Review these details in the plan documents and set aside funds for expenses you may owe before reaching your deductible. [Health care planning guidance](https://www.ffcommunity.com/planning-for-healthcare-in-retirement) also recommends budgeting for these costs alongside premiums.

### Account for prescription drugs, dental and vision care, and uncovered services

Check whether your prescriptions are covered and how much you’ll pay. Plans use formularies, or lists of covered medications, and costs may depend on a drug’s tier, your pharmacy, or whether the plan requires prior approval. If you take regular medication, include its expected cost in your annual estimate.

Medical plans may not include dental and vision coverage, and some treatments or services may not be covered. You may need separate policies or have to pay for those expenses yourself. List your regular appointments, prescriptions, and anticipated care, then confirm coverage and costs with the insurer. [Retirement health care planning information](https://www.ffcommunity.com/planning-for-healthcare-in-retirement) identifies prescriptions, dental and vision care, and uncovered services as costs to factor into your budget.

### Include spouse and dependent costs, and avoid coverage gaps

Estimate coverage costs for everyone on your current plan before making a retirement decision. Your spouse may not qualify for Medicare yet, and children or other dependents may also need coverage. Compare the total household cost of a Marketplace plan with options such as a spouse’s employer plan or temporary continuation coverage.

Confirm the dates when your current coverage ends and your next plan begins. This is especially important when leaving a job, selling a practice, or changing ownership. A gap could leave you responsible for the full cost of care. Include your family’s coverage needs in your timeline and budget. A [review of health care costs before age 65](https://www.heydayretirement.com/heyday-blogs/retirement-risk-11-health-care-costs-up-to-age-65) also stresses planning for spouses and dependents before Medicare eligibility.

### Compare total annual costs, not just premiums

For a useful comparison, estimate a full year of costs for each plan. Include premiums, expected deductibles, copays, coinsurance, prescriptions, and care the plan doesn’t cover. Then review the out-of-pocket maximum and confirm which expenses count toward it. Premiums and excluded services typically don’t count toward that limit.

Compare at least two scenarios: a year when you use about the amount of care you expect, and a higher-cost year. A plan with a lower monthly premium may cost more overall if you regularly see specialists, fill prescriptions, or pay substantial cost-sharing. Comparing annual estimates can help you see how each option fits your budget. [Retirement health care guidance](https://www.ffcommunity.com/planning-for-healthcare-in-retirement) recommends considering premiums together with deductibles and other out-of-pocket expenses.

### Plan for changing premiums and health needs until Medicare eligibility

Your coverage needs and costs can change between retirement and Medicare eligibility. Premiums and plan options may shift, while a new health concern, specialist visit, or prescription can increase your spending. Build room for these changes into your retirement budget instead of assuming your first year’s costs will stay the same.

Review your coverage and estimates each year, and whenever your health, income, or household circumstances change. Physicians should also consider how coverage choices relate to retirement income, practice proceeds, and tax planning. Start by listing your household’s expected health needs, current providers, prescriptions, and any planned procedures. Then compare those needs against each plan’s costs and rules.

For physicians leaving a hospital, health system, or practice, timing and income deserve extra attention. Your coverage options may depend on when employer benefits end, whether you continue working, and how retirement income affects eligibility for financial assistance. ### Review referrals, prior authorization, and out-of-network rules

Check how each plan manages access to care. Some plans require a referral before you see a specialist; others may require prior authorization for certain tests, procedures, or medications. Ask who submits the paperwork, how long a decision may take, and what steps you can take if a request is denied. These rules can affect when you receive care and what you pay.

Also find out whether the plan covers out-of-network care and how much you would owe. The out-of-pocket maximum may not include every expense, such as out-of-network charges or excluded services. If you travel often, see specialists outside your area, or want to keep a particular hospital, check those rules before choosing. Request plan-specific answers from the insurer, and save the responses with your comparison notes.

### Check coverage dates and enrollment deadlines before leaving work

Map out when employer coverage ends, when a new plan begins, and when each family member needs coverage. A gap, even a short one, could leave you responsible for the full cost of care. If you are considering COBRA or a Marketplace plan, confirm the election or enrollment deadline, the coverage start date, and whether you need to act before your final day at work.

Plan ahead for Medicare, too. The initial enrollment period generally lasts seven months, beginning three months before the month you turn 65 and ending three months after it. Missing a deadline may have consequences, depending on your circumstances and other coverage. Review the timeline in [FFCCU’s guide to health care planning in retirement](https://www.ffcommunity.com/planning-for-healthcare-in-retirement), then confirm how the rules apply to your situation before making a decision.

### Read plan documents and confirm details with the insurer

Before enrolling, review the Summary of Benefits and Coverage, plan brochure, provider directory, and prescription drug formulary. These documents explain deductibles, copays, coinsurance, exclusions, and coverage limits. Focus on services you expect to use, such as imaging, physical therapy, mental health care, or ongoing specialist treatment.

If something is unclear, call the insurer with specific questions. Ask, for example, whether a particular physician is in network for the exact plan you are considering, or what you will pay for a medication. Your coverage needs can shift when your health, household, income, or work situation changes. Review your options during each enrollment period and after events such as a move, marriage, new diagnosis, prescription change, or a spouse’s retirement. ## How Can Early Retirees Manage and Budget for Health Care Costs?

Health care can become a significant retirement expense when you leave work before becoming eligible for Medicare. Employer coverage may have made costs less visible, so estimate premiums and out-of-pocket expenses before retiring or selling a practice. Then revisit the plan as your coverage, income, and health needs change.

### Include premiums and expected out-of-pocket costs in retirement projections

Start with the full cost of coverage, not just the monthly premium. Estimate premiums for each household member, then add likely deductibles, copays, coinsurance, prescription costs, and services the plan may not cover. This is especially important if you have been covered through an employer, which may have paid part of your premium.

Build more than one estimate: one for a year with routine care and another for a year with higher medical expenses. Update your numbers when you compare plans or your household circumstances change. Including these costs in your retirement projections can help you assess whether your savings and retirement timeline are realistic. A physician-focused [retirement planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can connect health care assumptions with income and savings decisions.

### Set aside funds for deductibles and unexpected care

Insurance does not eliminate the risk of a large medical bill. A procedure or specialist visit early in the year, before you meet your deductible, can require a substantial payment. Review the plan’s out-of-pocket maximum and check which services and providers count toward it. Set aside accessible savings for expected cost-sharing and unexpected care, rather than relying on investments you may not want to sell at a particular time.

Choose a reserve amount based on your plan, household, and health needs. Prescription changes, an unplanned procedure, or more frequent specialist visits can shift your costs from year to year. Revisit your estimate when you renew coverage. Remember that premiums and some services not covered by your plan generally do not count toward its out-of-pocket maximum.

### Coordinate coverage choices with retirement income and withdrawals

Your income and coverage choices can affect each other. For example, household income is one factor used to determine eligibility for Marketplace financial assistance. The amount and timing of income from investments, retirement accounts, consulting, or a practice transition may affect your coverage costs.

Compare plans alongside your retirement income strategy, rather than treating insurance as a separate expense. Consider how taxable withdrawals could affect your estimated household income and potential assistance. If you expect income to vary, model different scenarios and update them when your plans change. Coordinating these decisions with a [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you account for health care costs while making broader retirement and withdrawal decisions.

### Check HSA eligibility and rules for using existing funds

A Health Savings Account (HSA) can help pay for eligible medical expenses, but it is not health insurance. You can generally contribute only when you meet the requirements, including having an HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan and no disqualifying coverage. Before retiring, check whether your new plan qualifies and confirm the current rules with a tax professional.

Funds already in your HSA can generally be used tax-free for qualified medical expenses, even if you are no longer eligible to contribute. Keep receipts and records, and check whether an expense qualifies before taking a withdrawal. Your eligibility and HSA rules may change as your coverage changes, including when you enroll in Medicare. The [IRS guidance on HSAs](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969) explains contribution and distribution rules.

### Consider part-time work or a spouse’s employer plan

Part-time work may provide a bridge to Medicare if the employer offers health coverage to eligible employees. Before accepting a position for its benefits, ask how many hours you must work, when coverage starts, what it costs, and whether your doctors and prescriptions are covered. Compare the plan’s total annual cost with other options available to you.

If your spouse is still working, ask whether you can join their employer plan and when you can enroll. Check the added premium, deductible, provider network, and coverage start date. Do not assume the plan will be available for as long as you need it. Request plan details in writing, then coordinate the new coverage date with the end of your current plan to help prevent a gap.

### Plan for the transition to Medicare at age 65

Plan for Medicare well before you turn 65. The initial enrollment period generally lasts seven months, beginning three months before the month you turn 65 and ending three months after it. Different rules may apply if you have coverage through current employment, so confirm how your situation affects your enrollment deadline.

Review your Medicare coverage options, prescription drug coverage, and any supplemental coverage before your existing plan ends. Delaying enrollment when you do not qualify for a special enrollment period may lead to gaps or late-enrollment penalties. Check [Medicare’s enrollment guidance](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/sign-up/when-can-i-sign-up-for-medicare) and coordinate coverage dates with your retirement and income plans.

## What Health Care Costs and Coverage Limits Should You Watch For?

A monthly premium is only one part of the cost of health coverage. Before choosing a plan, check what you’ll pay when you receive care, which services are excluded, and how long coverage lasts. These details can have a substantial effect on your retirement budget, especially if you leave work before becoming eligible for Medicare.

Compare options based on your household’s needs, including any ongoing treatment, prescriptions, and coverage for a spouse or dependents. As part of a broader [retirement plan for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians), account for both expected health expenses and the possibility of a year with higher-than-usual medical costs.

### Budget for the employer’s former share of COBRA premiums

COBRA may let you keep your employer-sponsored health plan for a limited time after leaving a job. However, you may have to pay the full premium yourself, including the portion your employer previously paid. Plans may also charge an administrative fee, which can make COBRA considerably more expensive than it was while you were working.

Ask your plan administrator for the full monthly cost, payment deadlines, and the date your coverage would end. Then compare COBRA with Marketplace plans and other options. Keeping your current doctors and treatment arrangements may be valuable, but weigh that convenience against the cost. The [Federal Financial Community Credit Union’s overview of health care in retirement](https://www.ffcommunity.com/planning-for-healthcare-in-retirement) explains why COBRA premiums can increase when an employer’s contribution ends.

### Check whether short-term plans exclude preexisting conditions or important services

Short-term plans may have lower premiums than comprehensive coverage, but they can also provide fewer benefits. Depending on the policy and state rules, a plan may exclude preexisting conditions or limit coverage for services such as prescription drugs, mental health care, or maternity care. If you need care that isn’t covered, you could face significant costs.

Read the policy’s exclusions, benefit limits, and renewal rules before enrolling. Check specifically whether it covers the services and medications you expect to use, and don’t assume it offers the same protections as an ACA Marketplace plan. The [health care cost guide for people under 65](https://www.heydayretirement.com/heyday-blogs/retirement-risk-11-health-care-costs-up-to-age-65) notes that short-term plans may exclude preexisting conditions and offer limited benefits.

### Remember that medical cost-sharing arrangements aren’t comprehensive health insurance

Medical cost-sharing arrangements typically involve members contributing money toward eligible medical bills. They are not the same as health insurance, and payment for a particular bill may not be guaranteed. Rules for eligibility, exclusions, and member responsibilities vary by arrangement.

Before relying on one, ask which expenses qualify, whether there are payment limits, and how the arrangement handles ongoing or preexisting conditions. Find out what you would owe if a bill is not eligible for sharing, and review the written guidelines rather than relying on a summary. The [FFCCU retirement health care guide](https://www.ffcommunity.com/planning-for-healthcare-in-retirement) explains how medical cost-sharing differs from traditional insurance.

### Distinguish long-term care insurance from pre-Medicare coverage

Long-term care insurance may help pay for assistance with daily activities, such as bathing or dressing, or care at home, in an assisted-living facility, or in a nursing home. It serves a different purpose from health insurance that covers doctor visits, hospital care, and prescriptions before Medicare eligibility.

When reviewing a long-term care policy, check what triggers benefits, which care settings qualify, and how much the policy may pay. Keep this decision separate from choosing pre-Medicare health coverage. You may need to plan for both, but one does not replace the other. The [FFCCU resource on retirement health care](https://www.ffcommunity.com/planning-for-healthcare-in-retirement) describes the types of support long-term care coverage may help fund.

### Budget for uncovered care as well as premiums

Estimate the full cost of care, not just the monthly premium. Include deductibles, copays, coinsurance, prescriptions, and services your plan doesn’t cover. Dental and vision care may require separate coverage or out-of-pocket payments. Review the plan’s out-of-pocket maximum, too, and check which expenses count toward it.

Build estimates for both a typical year and a year when you need more care. Include your spouse and dependents, and leave room for unexpected treatment. Comparing total annual costs can help you see how a lower premium might come with higher costs when you use care. As your health needs or household circumstances change, revisit your assumptions and update your retirement budget.

## How Should Physicians Plan for Coverage Before Medicare?

Physicians planning to retire before 65 may need to arrange several years of coverage before Medicare eligibility. A practice sale, ownership change, or shift to part-time work can also affect when current benefits end. Start early, confirm dates and plan rules, and compare options based on the needs of your entire household.

### Coordinate coverage dates with retirement, a practice sale, or an ownership transition

Create a timeline showing when your current health coverage ends and when a replacement plan can begin. Employer coverage may end on your last day of work, at the end of the month, or on another date specified by the plan. If you own a practice, confirm how a sale or ownership transition affects benefits for you and your family. Ask the benefits administrator or insurer for the exact end date, then arrange new coverage to prevent a gap.

Include more than your planned retirement date. Account for notice periods, transition work, and any delay before new coverage starts. If you expect to retire before 65, estimate how many years you’ll need coverage. The [Federal Financial Community Credit Union’s retirement health care guide](https://www.ffcommunity.com/planning-for-healthcare-in-retirement) outlines why early retirees should plan for this period. Practice owners can also address benefits as part of [practice and business planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning).

### Compare employer coverage, a spouse’s plan, COBRA, and Marketplace options

Review your choices before leaving a job or completing a practice transition. If your spouse has employer coverage, ask whether you and your dependents can join, when enrollment is allowed, and what the plan costs. COBRA may let you temporarily keep your current employer plan, but you generally pay the full premium plus an administrative fee. Confirm the deadline to elect coverage, how long it can continue, and when payments are due.

An ACA Marketplace plan may offer another option for coverage before Medicare. Depending on your household and income, you may qualify for financial assistance. Compare premiums, deductibles, copays, and out-of-pocket limits, and check that your doctors, hospitals, and prescriptions are covered. HealthCare.gov’s information for early retirees can help you review Marketplace coverage and understand how it compares with COBRA or a spouse’s plan.

### Factor in consulting, part-time clinical work, and practice income

Consulting, locum tenens work, or part-time clinical practice may help cover expenses after you leave full-time work. Some part-time roles offer health insurance, but eligibility can depend on hours worked, waiting periods, and the employer’s rules. Ask about employee premiums, household eligibility, provider networks, and the coverage start date. Get the details directly from the employer before counting on the plan.

Income from consulting, clinical work, or a practice sale can affect your tax picture and may influence eligibility for Marketplace financial assistance. Estimate your household income for the full calendar year, including transition payments and investment income. If earnings change, update your estimate and review how that may affect your coverage costs. A coordinated [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you assess income timing alongside your health coverage choices.

### Include household coverage and dependent needs in the plan

Plan for every family member who needs coverage, not only the physician retiring or leaving a practice. List your spouse and dependents, note when each person’s current coverage ends, and identify available replacement options. Your spouse may be eligible for an employer plan, while children may have separate enrollment rules. Check whether each option covers the doctors, specialists, hospitals, and prescriptions your household relies on.

Compare the cost of one family plan with the cost of separate plans. A plan that works well for one person may have a limited network or higher costs for another. Include expected appointments, ongoing treatment, prescriptions, and planned procedures in your comparison. Before enrolling, confirm coverage details with the insurer and, when needed, the provider. Reviewing the household’s needs together can help you avoid gaps and choose coverage that fits the family’s care requirements.

### Coordinate income, tax, and health care assumptions in retirement projections

Include health insurance premiums and expected out-of-pocket costs in your retirement projections for each year before Medicare. Budget for deductibles, copays, coinsurance, prescriptions, and unexpected care. Identify which savings or income sources will cover these expenses, such as cash, taxable investments, or retirement accounts. The timing and amount of withdrawals can affect your taxes and may also influence eligibility for Marketplace financial assistance.

Use estimates that reflect your expected retirement date, location, household income, and coverage choice. Replace estimates with actual plan quotes when they become available, and revise your projections if work or practice income changes. A physician-focused [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help coordinate coverage costs with income and tax assumptions. Reviewing these factors together gives you a clearer basis for comparing retirement dates and plan options.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the most common health insurance option for someone retiring before 65?**\
An ACA Marketplace plan is a common choice, but it may not be the best fit for every household. You could also compare a spouse’s employer plan, COBRA, retiree benefits, or coverage through eligible part-time work. Consider the cost, coverage dates, provider network, and your expected care needs before choosing.

**Can early retirees qualify for financial help with Marketplace premiums?**\
Possibly. Eligibility depends on household income and other factors. Estimate income for the full coverage year, including consulting or practice income, investment income, and retirement account withdrawals. Update your application if your circumstances change, since the final amount of assistance is reconciled when you file taxes.

**Is COBRA a good choice after leaving a medical practice or employer?**\
It can be useful if you want to keep the same plan temporarily, especially during ongoing treatment. You’ll generally pay the full premium, plus an administrative fee, and coverage usually lasts for a limited time. Compare its cost and end date with other plans before relying on it.

**How much should I budget for health care before Medicare?**\
Include premiums, deductibles, copays, coinsurance, prescriptions, and services your plan may not cover. Estimate costs for both a typical year and a year with more care than expected. Remember to include coverage for your spouse and dependents, if needed.

**When should I start planning for Medicare?**\
Start reviewing your options before you turn 65. The initial enrollment period generally spans seven months, beginning three months before your 65th birthday month and ending three months after it. Confirm how your current coverage affects your enrollment timing with Medicare or a qualified benefits professional.

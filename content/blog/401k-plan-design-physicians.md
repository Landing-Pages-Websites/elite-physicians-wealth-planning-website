A retirement plan should reflect how a medical practice actually operates. Physician owners may receive different forms of compensation, employees may work part time or across related entities, and practice revenue can change from year to year. A formula that looks affordable at first may create unexpected costs as the team grows. Careful **401k plan design for physician practices** helps owners compare matching contributions, nonelective contributions, profit sharing, safe harbor options, and cash balance plans before making a decision. It also brings tax professionals, payroll teams, third-party administrators, and advisors into the same conversation. Here’s what to review before adopting or changing a plan.

## Key Takeaways

* **Match the plan to the practice**: Consider ownership structure, employee demographics, compensation, cash flow, growth plans, and retirement goals before choosing plan features.
* **Balance physician savings with employee value**: Compare pre-tax and Roth contributions, matching, safe harbor funding, profit sharing, and cash balance plans while accounting for testing and long-term costs.
* **Build compliance into the process**: Coordinate the TPA, payroll provider, recordkeeper, tax professional, and advisor to manage deposits, notices, testing, filings, investments, and regular plan reviews.

## 401(k) Plan Design for Physician Practices: What Matters?

A 401(k) plan can support far more than retirement savings. For a physician practice, the right design may help attract physicians and staff, manage taxable income, retain experienced employees, and prepare owners for a future transition. The right approach depends on the practice’s size, ownership structure, compensation patterns, workforce, and cash flow.

A solo physician with no eligible employees may need a very different plan from a growing multispecialty group or a dental practice with several related entities. Before comparing plan features, gather accurate ownership, payroll, and employee data. Then connect the plan with broader [wealth management for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) so retirement contributions support the rest of the financial strategy.

### Define the plan’s goals for physicians, owners, and staff

Start by deciding what the plan should accomplish for each group. Physician owners may want to maximize permitted contributions, reduce current taxable income, and create a consistent retirement savings process. Employees may place greater value on an employer match, simple enrollment, low fees, and clear education.

The plan should address both sets of needs. A design focused only on owners may create testing concerns or provide little hiring value. A basic, low-cost plan may be easy to operate but fail to support the physicians’ long-term savings goals.

List the priorities before reviewing formulas. Common goals include:

* Maximizing physician contributions
* Offering a competitive employee benefit
* Managing annual employer costs
* Keeping administration practical
* Supporting a future merger, sale, or ownership transition

This list gives the practice a framework for weighing plan features and tradeoffs.

### Explain deferrals, employer contributions, and tax-deferred growth

Employee elective deferrals are amounts physicians and staff choose to contribute from their pay. If the plan permits both options, employees may make pretax contributions, Roth contributions, or a combination. Pretax contributions generally reduce current taxable income. Roth contributions are made after taxes and may provide tax-free qualified withdrawals in retirement.

Employer contributions work differently. A practice may provide a match based on employee deferrals, a nonelective contribution for eligible employees whether or not they contribute, or a discretionary profit-sharing contribution. Eligible employer contributions may be deductible, subject to applicable rules and limits.

Invested plan assets generally grow tax deferred while they remain in the account. Contribution limits and other retirement plan rules can change, so owners should review the [IRS guidance on 401(k) contributions](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-contributions) when setting payroll elections and annual projections.

### Map practice entities, ownership, and affiliated relationships

Review every entity connected to the practice before selecting a contribution formula. The list may include a professional corporation, management company, real estate entity, surgery center, billing company, or other businesses owned by the physicians. Ownership percentages and business relationships can determine whether multiple entities must be treated as one employer.

Controlled-group and affiliated-service-group rules may require related businesses to share coverage and nondiscrimination responsibilities. For example, a plan that appears to cover only the physicians in one entity may also need to consider employees of a related company. The IRS rules for controlled groups provide useful background, but each structure requires a fact-specific review.

Gather entity documents, ownership records, payroll data, and existing plan documents early. A third-party administrator and tax professional can evaluate the structure, while [practice and business planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning) can connect retirement decisions with expansion, succession, or a future sale.

### Classify physicians, highly compensated employees, staff, and seasonal workers

An accurate employee census is essential. Include physician owners, employed physicians, dentists, advanced practice providers, nurses, administrative staff, part-time employees, seasonal workers, and anyone who works across related entities.

The plan document should explain eligibility requirements, service rules, entry dates, compensation, and vesting. It should also clarify how the practice will identify highly compensated employees and apply coverage and nondiscrimination testing.

A qualified plan generally cannot provide benefits only to owners when eligible employees work for the practice. Applicable rules are designed to provide meaningful opportunities for rank-and-file employees. Seasonal and part-time workers require particular attention because service-based eligibility rules may allow some of them to enter the plan sooner than the practice expects.

Review the census at least annually and whenever the practice hires, terminates, or changes an employee’s status.

### Set retirement, tax, cash-flow, and retention goals

Plan design requires tradeoffs. Physicians may want substantial retirement contributions, but the practice must fund employer contributions and administrative expenses consistently. A generous benefit may support recruitment and retention, yet become difficult to sustain if staffing expands or revenue falls.

Set a target for physician savings first, then estimate the related employee cost under several formulas. Compare projected tax deductions with matching, nonelective, profit-sharing, recordkeeping, testing, and administration expenses. Include payroll timing and expected changes in physician compensation in the cash-flow analysis.

Retention deserves attention, too. Vesting schedules, automatic enrollment, employer contributions, and employee education can affect how staff value the plan. Physicians approaching retirement may need a different design from physicians early in their careers. A coordinated [retirement strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can place plan contributions within the broader context of practice income, personal savings, and future distributions.

### Correct misconceptions about owner-only plans, limits, testing, and costs

A common misconception is that self-employed physicians cannot establish a 401(k) plan. An owner with no eligible employees may qualify for an individual, or solo, 401(k), depending on the business structure and circumstances. Hiring an eligible employee can change the plan’s requirements and administration.

Another misconception is that every physician can contribute the same amount. Employee deferrals, employer contributions, catch-up contributions, compensation limits, and annual additions all interact. A high salary does not permit unlimited contributions, and a discretionary employer contribution must follow the plan document and applicable testing rules.

Safe harbor provisions may simplify certain nondiscrimination tests, but they do not remove every compliance duty. Practices still need accurate payroll data, timely deposits, required notices, plan filings, and proper records. Total costs may include recordkeeping, third-party administration, legal services, audits, employee education, and corrections. Evaluate the plan based on its value to physicians, staff, and the practice, not solely on the lowest quoted fee.

## Which 401(k) Features Fit a Physician Practice?

A 401(k) plan can support several goals at once: help physicians and staff save for retirement, strengthen the practice’s benefits package, and give owners a tax-advantaged way to build long-term wealth. The right design depends on the practice’s ownership structure, payroll, workforce, cash flow, and plans for growth.

A medical practice may include physician owners, employed physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, nurses, administrative employees, and part-time or seasonal workers. Each group can affect the plan’s eligibility rules, contribution costs, and compliance testing. That makes plan design more involved than choosing a contribution percentage.

The plan should also fit the physician’s broader [retirement planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians). A design that works well for an owner may create higher employee costs, while a low-cost plan may not provide enough retirement-saving capacity for a high-income physician. Reviewing the features together can help the practice compare tradeoffs before finalizing plan documents.

### Set eligibility, service rules, entry dates, compensation, and vesting

The plan document should clearly explain who can participate and when participation begins. Eligibility rules may address minimum age, service requirements, and entry dates, such as the first day of a quarter. These rules must follow federal requirements, including provisions affecting long-term, part-time employees. The IRS guide to 401(k) plans provides an overview of the rules employers need to consider.

The compensation definition is also important. The plan may include regular wages, bonuses, commissions, or other eligible pay when calculating contributions, but the definition must be applied consistently and within applicable limits. Vesting determines when employees own employer contributions. Immediate vesting is straightforward, while graded or cliff vesting may support retention. The practice should also account for employees who leave, return, or transfer between related entities.

### Compare pre-tax and Roth employee contributions

Pre-tax employee contributions reduce taxable income in the year they are made. The money can grow without current income tax, although withdrawals are generally taxable later. This option may appeal to employees who expect their tax rate to be lower in retirement, but each participant’s income, career stage, and future plans are different.

Roth 401(k) contributions are made with after-tax dollars. They do not reduce current taxable income, but qualified withdrawals can generally be tax-free when applicable requirements are met. Offering both choices allows participants to decide how much of their retirement savings should receive tax treatment now versus later.

Residents and early-career clinicians may prefer Roth contributions while their taxable income is lower. Established physicians may favor pre-tax contributions because of their current tax bracket, or use both options for tax diversification. The plan should explain that a Roth 401(k) is different from a Roth IRA, which has separate eligibility rules and contribution limits. Employees should also understand that their election must be processed correctly through payroll.

### Choose matching, nonelective, or discretionary employer contributions

A matching contribution is based on how much an employee contributes. For example, a plan may match 100% of the first 3% an employee defers and 50% of the next 2%. Matching can encourage participation because employees generally need to save in order to receive the full employer contribution. It can also help the practice estimate costs based on employee participation.

A nonelective contribution does not depend on whether an employee makes elective deferrals. If the plan provides a 3% nonelective contribution, eligible employees may receive that contribution even if they contribute nothing themselves. This structure can be easier to explain and may help satisfy safe harbor requirements.

Discretionary contributions give the practice more flexibility. The employer can decide each year whether to contribute, subject to the plan’s terms and applicable rules. This may work well when collections and profits vary. However, discretionary does not mean unrestricted. Allocation formulas, compensation limits, coverage requirements, and nondiscrimination rules still apply. The practice should compare projected owner and employee costs before selecting a formula.

### Use safe harbor provisions to simplify ADP and ACP testing

Safe harbor provisions can help a practice avoid annual ADP and ACP testing when the plan meets specific contribution, notice, and operational requirements. ADP testing compares elective deferrals made by highly compensated employees with those made by other employees. ACP testing generally applies to employer matching contributions and certain after-tax contributions.

A safe harbor design typically uses either a required matching contribution or a required nonelective contribution. One common match provides 100% of the first 3% an employee defers, plus 50% of the next 2%. A nonelective design may provide at least 3% of eligible compensation to qualifying participants, even when they do not contribute.

The tradeoff is that safe harbor funding is generally required and must follow strict timing and notice rules. Safe harbor status does not eliminate every compliance obligation. The practice may still need to satisfy coverage, top-heavy, reporting, and plan administration requirements. The [IRS safe harbor plan guidance](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-401k-and-profit-sharing-plan-contribution-limits) can provide general background, but the plan’s third-party administrator should review the specific design.

### Add profit sharing with integrated or cross-tested allocations

Profit sharing can provide retirement contributions beyond employee deferrals and required safe harbor funding. The contribution is often discretionary, allowing the practice to adjust or skip it when profits are lower. That flexibility can help practices manage variable collections, changing overhead, or uneven financial results.

An integrated allocation considers Social Security wages when assigning contributions. A cross-tested design may provide different contribution rates to employee groups, often using age and projected retirement benefits in the analysis. Since older participants have fewer years for contributions to grow, a cross-tested plan may support a larger percentage of pay for older physician owners while still meeting nondiscrimination requirements.

These designs require accurate census and compensation data. Age, compensation, ownership, service, and employee classification can all affect the outcome. Profit sharing may provide additional retirement benefits for senior physicians or valued staff, but the practice should model the required contributions for every eligible employee before adopting the formula. The plan should also fit the owner’s broader [wealth management strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians), including investment, tax, and cash-flow goals.

### Add auto-enrollment, auto-escalation, and participation features

Auto-enrollment places eligible employees into the plan at a stated contribution rate unless they opt out. Auto-escalation increases that rate over time, often by one percentage point each year until it reaches a specified limit. These features can help employees begin saving without requiring them to make every decision themselves.

The practice must provide required notices, process opt-out requests, and apply elections accurately through payroll. It should also select a default investment option for participants who do not choose their own investments. That option should be reviewed by the plan’s fiduciaries and investment professionals.

SECURE 2.0 includes automatic enrollment and escalation requirements for many new 401(k) plans, subject to effective dates, exceptions, and other conditions. Practices should confirm how the law applies to their particular plan rather than relying on a general template. Auto-enrollment is most effective when employees receive clear information about contribution rates, tax treatment, investments, and how to change their elections.

### Set investment, education, loan, withdrawal, and beneficiary provisions

The investment menu should provide enough variety for participants to build suitable portfolios without making the plan difficult to use. Many plans offer diversified mutual funds, target-date funds, and a qualified default investment alternative for participants who do not make an investment election. A 3(38) investment fiduciary may also select and monitor investments under a defined advisory arrangement. The Department of Labor’s fiduciary guidance outlines important oversight considerations for retirement plan sponsors.

Participant education should cover contribution elections, tax treatment, investment risk, fees, and retirement income planning. The plan may allow participant loans, hardship withdrawals, or other in-service distributions, but these features create additional administrative requirements and can reduce long-term savings. Practices should weigh employee access against the goal of preserving retirement assets.

Participants should also be encouraged to name and review beneficiaries regularly. Beneficiary designations may need to change after marriage, divorce, the birth of a child, or an update to an estate plan. The practice should maintain a process for communicating these provisions without providing individualized legal or tax advice. For owners, these decisions should be coordinated with [legacy and estate planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/legacy-estate-planning).

## How Can Physicians Maximize Contributions and Tax Savings?

A well-designed retirement plan can help physicians save more for retirement while making the practice’s tax strategy more effective. The right design depends on several factors, including physician age, compensation, ownership structure, staffing levels, practice profitability, and the amount the practice can contribute each year.

A 401(k) plan should not be designed separately from the rest of the practice’s financial plan. Employer contributions, physician compensation, cash flow, tax deductions, and employee benefits all affect one another. Before choosing a contribution formula, review the practice’s ownership and workforce structure, then model how different designs would affect physicians and staff.

The IRS retirement plan contribution limits change periodically, so use current figures when projecting contributions. It is also important to coordinate the plan with tax planning, practice operations, and each physician’s personal retirement goals.

### Maximize elective deferrals and age-based catch-up contributions

Physicians can begin by contributing as much as practical through salary deferrals. Traditional 401(k) contributions generally reduce current taxable income, while Roth contributions are made with after-tax dollars and may provide tax-free qualified withdrawals later. A plan that offers both options gives participants more flexibility based on their current income, expected future tax rate, and long-term goals.

Participants who meet the applicable age requirements may make catch-up contributions above the standard annual limit. These additional contributions can be valuable for established physicians who earn substantial income but have fewer years remaining before retirement.

Encourage physicians to review their deferral elections before each plan year and after compensation changes. Payroll should monitor contributions throughout the year to prevent excess deferrals and address errors promptly. Owners should also confirm that their elections work with employer contributions and the plan’s testing requirements.

### Coordinate physician pay with employer contribution limits

Employer contributions are often based on eligible compensation, so physician pay can affect the amount a practice contributes to the plan. Depending on the plan document, eligible compensation may include salary, bonuses, productivity payments, or other forms of pay. Review the compensation structure alongside the desired retirement contribution instead of making these decisions separately.

Practices with employees generally cannot create a plan that benefits only owners or physicians. The plan must satisfy coverage and nondiscrimination requirements, and eligible employees may need to receive meaningful benefits. The [Department of Labor’s retirement plan guidance](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/retirement) outlines key responsibilities for employer-sponsored plans.

Practice owners should coordinate payroll decisions with the CPA, third-party administrator, and retirement plan advisor. This team can estimate owner and employee contributions before the practice finalizes salaries, bonuses, or other compensation decisions.

### Combine safe harbor contributions with discretionary profit sharing

A safe harbor 401(k) design can make it easier for highly compensated physicians to contribute the full amount allowed under the plan. Depending on the formula, the employer may provide matching contributions or a nonelective contribution to eligible employees. In return, the plan may avoid certain annual nondiscrimination tests that can restrict highly compensated employees’ deferrals.

A practice may also add discretionary profit sharing when cash flow permits. This approach gives the employer flexibility to make additional contributions without promising the same amount every year. However, allocations must follow the plan document and applicable testing rules.

A safe harbor contribution can create a predictable employee benefit, while profit sharing may allow additional retirement funding for physician owners when the plan design supports it. Before adopting this structure, model changes in staffing, payroll, and practice revenue. A formula that works for a small staff may become costly after hiring associates or opening another location.

### Evaluate cash balance plans for high-income or late-career physicians

A cash balance plan may help high-income or late-career physicians contribute more than a 401(k) alone permits. It is a type of defined benefit plan that provides a stated account value based on a contribution formula and an assumed interest credit. Annual contribution targets depend largely on age, compensation, retirement goals, and actuarial calculations.

Older participants may have higher allowable contributions because they have fewer years to build the targeted retirement benefit. For some physicians, annual funding can reach well into six figures, although the actual amount varies by plan design and individual circumstances. Contributions are generally deductible to the practice when the plan is properly structured and funded.

Cash balance plans also require actuarial work, formal administration, and consistent funding. They may suit an established practice with stable profits, but they may be less appropriate for a new practice or one with unpredictable cash flow. Review [retirement planning for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) before committing to this type of plan.

### Compare 401(k)-only and combined plans using age and pay data

A 401(k)-only design may be appropriate for a younger physician group, a practice with modest retirement goals, or a business that needs greater flexibility. A combined 401(k) and cash balance plan may be worth considering when owners are older, highly compensated, and interested in making larger deductible contributions.

Age and compensation data are central to this comparison. A cash balance plan may support larger contributions for older owners, while required employee contributions depend on the allocation method and workforce demographics. The practice should also compare projected tax deductions, administrative expenses, investment assumptions, and long-term funding obligations.

Ask the plan professionals to prepare side-by-side illustrations using current census data. Each illustration should show physician contributions, employee contributions, expected deductions, testing results, administrative fees, and total plan costs. This approach helps the practice compare the full financial effect of each design instead of focusing only on the largest possible owner contribution.

### Weigh actuarial funding, permanent contributions, and cash-flow commitments

Cash balance and other defined benefit plans require more planning than a traditional 401(k). An actuary determines the funding range, and the practice generally must make contributions according to the plan’s requirements. Funding may be adjusted within certain limits, but the employer cannot treat the plan like a discretionary bonus that can be skipped whenever profits decline.

These plans may help late-career physicians build retirement assets faster, but the practice should have a reliable funding source. Consider collections, payer mix, staffing costs, equipment purchases, debt payments, partner compensation, and expected changes in physician productivity. A plan that creates a large tax deduction but strains operating cash flow may not be sustainable.

Review funding requirements before adopting the plan and at least annually afterward. If the practice expects a partner’s retirement, a new associate, a merger, or a significant equipment purchase, discuss the change with the actuary and advisor early. Advance planning may provide more options than a last-minute adjustment.

### Coordinate retirement contributions with tax, wealth, and practice strategy

Retirement plan contributions should support the physician’s broader financial plan. A deductible employer contribution may reduce current taxable income, but physicians should also consider liquidity, Roth assets, taxable investments, debt repayment, insurance, and future income needs. Using different account types can create more flexibility when physicians begin drawing income in retirement.

For practice owners, retirement funding should align with entity structure, compensation strategy, succession plans, and the possible timing of a sale. The plan may affect partner buy-ins, employee retention, and the overall value of the benefits package. It can also influence how much capital remains available for hiring, technology, debt repayment, or expansion.

Coordinate the retirement design with the practice’s [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) and broader [wealth management plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians). When the CPA, advisor, actuary, and plan administrator work from the same information, the practice can make decisions based on its complete financial picture rather than a single year’s tax result.

## How Can Practices Balance Employee Costs and Talent?

A retirement plan can support physician wealth goals while helping a practice attract and retain skilled employees. The challenge is creating a design that fits the practice’s budget, workforce, ownership structure, and growth plans. A formula that works for a small, stable office may become expensive after hiring additional staff, adding a physician, or opening another location.

Start by defining what the practice can fund consistently. Then compare contribution formulas, administrative expenses, employee needs, and potential tax benefits. The goal is not to choose the most generous plan in every category. It is to create a sustainable benefit that supports physician retirement goals and gives employees a meaningful reason to join and stay with the practice.

Plan design should also account for the practice’s broader financial strategy. Employer contributions affect cash flow, payroll, tax deductions, and the amount physicians may be able to save through the plan. A coordinated approach can help owners evaluate these decisions alongside [retirement planning for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians), wealth management, and practice planning.

### Set contribution and administration budgets before choosing a formula

Before choosing a 401(k) formula, establish separate budgets for employer contributions and plan administration. Include matching or nonelective contributions, profit sharing, recordkeeping, third-party administrator fees, investment expenses, employee education, compliance testing, and required notices.

This process keeps the plan aligned with practice cash flow. It also lets owners compare options on an equal basis. A formula with lower required contributions may involve more testing or administrative work. A more generous design may offer greater value for recruitment, but it may also create a larger recurring expense.

Model the budget under several conditions, including lower practice revenue, a larger staff, additional eligible physicians, and future workforce growth. Strategic retirement planning for small doctor’s offices can help owners compare plan costs, benefits, and administrative demands before selecting a design.

### Compare match, nonelective, safe harbor, profit-sharing, and recordkeeping costs

Employer contribution formulas create different cost patterns. A matching contribution generally depends on employee deferrals, while a nonelective contribution may require funding for eligible employees whether they contribute or not. Safe harbor contributions can simplify certain nondiscrimination tests, but they typically involve required contributions and notice obligations.

Discretionary profit sharing offers more flexibility because the practice may decide whether to contribute in a given year, subject to the plan document and applicable rules. The allocation method can affect how contributions are divided between physicians and staff. As [Oncology Practice Management explains](https://oncpracticemanagement.com/issues/2012/july-2012-vol-2-no-4/retirement-plans-and-your-medical-practice), profit sharing can give medical practices flexibility when setting annual contributions.

Include recordkeeping, administration, payroll integration, investment, and communication expenses in the comparison. Review the estimated annual cost of the entire plan, not just the amount allocated to employee accounts.

### Account for turnover, eligibility classes, part-time staff, and workforce growth

Employee demographics can change a plan’s cost significantly. A practice with frequent staff turnover may have different contribution and vesting outcomes than one with a long-tenured team. Part-time employees, seasonal workers, and employees who move between related entities may also affect eligibility and coverage requirements.

Ask the plan administrator to model current and expected workforce data, including age, compensation, hours, service, turnover, and ownership relationships. Consider whether the practice expects to hire more nurses, technicians, administrators, or physicians. A plan that fits today’s census may need adjustments as the practice grows.

Eligibility classifications must follow the plan document and applicable rules. They cannot be created simply to exclude rank-and-file employees from benefits. The plan must satisfy coverage and nondiscrimination requirements. [Retirement plan guidance for medical practices](https://oncpracticemanagement.com/issues/2012/july-2012-vol-2-no-4/retirement-plans-and-your-medical-practice) emphasizes the importance of designing benefits that serve employees as well as owners.

### Balance staff benefits with physician retirement goals

Many physicians want to save as much as possible during their peak earning years. At the same time, the practice may need to provide meaningful benefits to employees. A design focused only on owner contributions can create testing concerns, unexpected costs, or a benefit package that feels uncompetitive to staff.

Profit sharing, cross-tested allocations, and cash balance plans may create additional savings opportunities for older or highly compensated physicians. However, the design must account for employee coverage, required funding, and long-term affordability. Employee ages, compensation, tenure, and participation all influence the outcome.

The plan should fit the physician’s larger financial picture. Contributions, tax deductions, practice cash flow, personal investments, and future income needs should be reviewed together. A [wealth management strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) can help connect retirement plan decisions with the rest of the physician’s financial life.

### Use vesting, participation, and auto-enrollment to support retention

Vesting determines how quickly employees gain ownership of employer contributions. A graded or cliff vesting schedule may encourage employees to remain with the practice, but overly restrictive terms can make the plan less appealing to candidates. Review the vesting schedule alongside turnover rates, hiring goals, and the competitive benefits offered by other employers.

Participation features can make the plan easier for employees to use. Auto-enrollment places eligible employees into the plan at a stated contribution rate, while auto-escalation gradually increases that rate. Employees should receive clear notices and have the ability to change or stop contributions when permitted under the plan.

According to [Human Interest’s overview of 401(k) plans for physicians](https://humaninterest.com/industries/retirement-plans-for-doctors), auto-enrollment can increase participation. It works best when paired with simple investment choices, accessible education, and payroll procedures that process employee contributions accurately.

### Present the plan as part of the practice’s recruitment and benefits package

A retirement plan has more value when employees understand it before accepting a position and after joining the practice. Include the plan in job postings, offer materials, onboarding meetings, and annual benefits communications. Explain eligibility, contribution options, employer funding, vesting, and investment choices in plain language.

Be precise about what the practice offers. State whether the plan includes a match, nonelective contribution, profit sharing, Roth contributions, or automatic enrollment. Do not describe a contribution as guaranteed if it is discretionary or depends on plan terms.

A 401(k) can support recruitment when candidates compare offers from hospitals, larger groups, and competing practices. [Human Interest notes that retirement benefits](https://humaninterest.com/industries/retirement-plans-for-doctors) can form an important part of a medical practice’s recruitment and retention strategy. Present the plan alongside health coverage, paid leave, professional development, and opportunities for advancement.

### Tailor education for residents, staff, physicians, owners, and late-career employees

Employees need different types of retirement information at different career stages. Residents and early-career clinicians may need help understanding contribution rates, Roth versus pre-tax savings, and basic investment choices. Staff members may benefit from examples showing how employer contributions and consistent saving can affect long-term account growth.

Established physicians and owners may need more detailed discussions about contribution limits, profit sharing, cash balance plans, tax planning, and practice cash flow. Employees approaching retirement may focus on distribution options, beneficiary designations, rollover decisions, and coordinating plan assets with other income sources.

Use several communication formats, including short meetings, written guides, enrollment checklists, and opportunities to ask questions. [Benefits communication guidance from Selerix](https://selerix.com/blog/create-an-engaging-employee-benefits-communication-plan) recommends adapting content, tone, and delivery methods for different employee groups. Clear education can increase participation and help employees recognize the retirement plan as a meaningful part of their compensation.

## How Do Practices Manage 401(k) Compliance and Administration?

A 401(k) plan can support physician recruitment, retention, and long-term tax planning, but it also creates ongoing administrative responsibilities. A practice must follow its plan document, apply eligibility and contribution rules consistently, complete required testing, and maintain accurate records.

These responsibilities become more involved when a physician owns multiple entities, employs physicians and staff, or operates a practice alongside a management company, surgery center, or real estate business. Related entities may need to be considered together, and an error in one area can affect the entire plan.

The practice owner remains responsible for overseeing the plan, even when a third-party administrator, recordkeeper, payroll provider, or financial advisor handles specific tasks. The [Department of Labor’s fiduciary guidance](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/retirement/fiduciaryresp) explains that fiduciaries must act prudently and follow the plan’s governing documents.

A dependable process assigns each responsibility to a specific person or provider. It also includes calendar reminders for testing, notices, deposits, filings, and plan reviews. A physician-focused wealth advisor can help coordinate these tasks with compensation, tax planning, cash flow, and ownership goals, while specialized retirement professionals handle technical administration.

### Complete ADP, ACP, coverage, top-heavy, and other required tests

Traditional 401(k) plans generally must pass annual nondiscrimination tests. The Actual Deferral Percentage, or ADP, test compares elective deferrals made by highly compensated employees with those made by other eligible employees. The Actual Contribution Percentage, or ACP, test compares matching and certain other contributions between the same groups.

A practice may also need coverage testing to confirm that the plan benefits a sufficient portion of non-highly compensated employees. A top-heavy test determines whether key employees hold more than the permitted percentage of plan assets. Test results can affect owner contributions and may require corrective distributions or additional employer contributions.

Testing depends on the plan document, employee census, compensation definitions, ownership, and related entities. The IRS overview of retirement plan nondiscrimination tests explains the basic rules. A third-party administrator should complete the calculations and explain any required corrections.

### Understand what safe harbor provisions do and do not exempt

A safe harbor 401(k) can help physician owners make planned contributions without relying on annual ADP and ACP test results. To qualify, the plan generally must provide a required employer contribution, follow specific notice rules, and meet applicable timing and vesting requirements.

Safe harbor status does not remove every compliance obligation. The plan may still need coverage, top-heavy, and other tests. The practice must also make promised contributions, deliver required notices, apply eligibility rules correctly, and maintain reliable payroll records.

A safe harbor design may use a matching contribution or a nonelective contribution for eligible employees. The better option depends on staff compensation, participation, turnover, and the owners’ savings goals. The IRS safe harbor guidance offers helpful background, but plan-specific advice is important before selecting a contribution formula.

### Review controlled-group, affiliated-service-group, and leased-employee rules

A physician may own several related businesses, such as a medical practice, ambulatory surgery center, management company, or real estate company. Those entities may be treated as a controlled group or affiliated service group for retirement plan purposes. If they are, employees across the businesses may need to be considered together for eligibility, coverage, testing, and contributions.

Leased employees can create another layer of complexity. A worker supplied through another organization may still count as an employee for certain retirement plan rules if the arrangement meets applicable requirements. Excluding someone solely because another entity issues the paycheck can create a compliance problem.

Before adopting or changing a plan, gather ownership percentages, operating agreements, management relationships, and employee information for each related entity. The IRS retirement plan qualification guidance explains why these relationships require careful review. A TPA or ERISA attorney can evaluate the structure before the plan is implemented.

### Define eligible compensation, payroll integration, and deposit timing

The plan document should clearly define compensation for elective deferrals, matching contributions, profit sharing, and testing. Compensation may include salary, bonuses, commissions, or certain taxable benefits, while excluding other payments. Practices should not assume that their payroll definition matches the retirement plan’s definition.

Payroll integration helps ensure that elections and employer contributions are calculated correctly each pay period. Establish who updates eligibility, processes deferral changes, reconciles payroll, and reviews contribution reports. These steps are especially important when physicians receive bonuses, productivity compensation, or different types of owner pay.

Employee contributions generally must be deposited as soon as reasonably possible after being withheld. Small plans have a specific seven-business-day safe harbor for depositing participant contributions. The Department of Labor’s deposit timing guidance explains the requirement and the risks of late deposits.

### Maintain plan documents, amendments, notices, records, and Form 5500 filings

A practice should maintain its signed plan document, adoption agreement, amendments, participant notices, payroll reports, contribution records, testing results, and service-provider agreements. These records help show that the plan has been administered according to its terms.

Some plans must provide notices about safe harbor status, automatic enrollment, qualified default investment alternatives, or other features. Notices may have specific delivery deadlines, so include them on the practice’s annual compliance calendar.

Many retirement plans must file Form 5500 each year, generally by the last day of the seventh month after the plan year ends. A plan may qualify to file Form 5500-SF if it meets the applicable small-plan requirements. The [DOL Form 5500 resources](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/employers-and-advisers/plan-administration-and-compliance/reporting-and-filing) provide filing information and deadline details. Keep copies of filings and confirmation records with the plan’s permanent files.

### Prepare for plan audits and required reports

A plan with 100 or more participants at the beginning of the plan year may generally need an annual audit by an independent qualified public accountant. The participant count can include more than employees who actively contribute, so practices should confirm how the rule applies to their plan with a TPA or auditor.

An audit may examine participant records, contributions, distributions, investments, payroll information, and internal controls. The auditor may request plan documents, bank statements, contribution reports, testing results, and evidence that required notices were delivered.

Preparation should begin before an audit request arrives. Reconcile contributions throughout the year, organize records by plan year, and document corrections as they occur. The [DOL’s annual reporting guidance](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/employers-and-advisers/plan-administration-and-compliance/reporting-and-filing) explains when an audit may be required and how it relates to Form 5500 reporting.

### Assign duties to fiduciaries, advisors, TPAs, recordkeepers, payroll teams, and tax professionals

Create a written responsibility chart for each major plan task. The TPA may handle eligibility, testing, amendments, and Form 5500 preparation. The recordkeeper may manage participant accounts, investment elections, statements, and distributions. The payroll provider may process deferrals and employer contributions.

A financial advisor may help review investments, fees, participant education, and fiduciary procedures. The CPA or tax professional can coordinate deductions, entity structure, compensation, and tax reporting. A plan fiduciary, often the practice owner or a committee, remains responsible for overseeing the arrangement and selecting providers prudently.

Delegating work does not eliminate the need for oversight. Review service agreements, confirm deliverables, monitor deadlines, and retain evidence of those reviews. The DOL’s guidance on selecting and monitoring service providers provides a useful framework for assigning and supervising these responsibilities.

### Monitor investments, fees, participant outcomes, and fiduciary processes

Compliance involves more than completing annual tests. Fiduciaries should review whether the investment menu is appropriate, expenses are reasonable, and participants receive understandable information. Reviews may consider investment performance, risk, fees, diversification, and whether available choices suit employees with different levels of experience.

The practice should also monitor participation rates, deferral levels, employer contributions, loans, withdrawals, and beneficiary designations. Low participation may indicate that employees need clearer education, simpler investment options, or additional enrollment support.

Document the date of each review, the information considered, decisions made, and follow-up responsibilities. The DOL’s fiduciary resources can help clarify oversight obligations. A physician-focused advisor can connect these reviews with employee retention, recruiting, compensation, and the owners’ broader wealth plan.

### Correct late deposits, excess contributions, missed notices, and other errors

Identify and correct errors promptly. Common problems include late employee contribution deposits, incorrect compensation calculations, excess deferrals, missed eligibility, improper exclusions, missed notices, and failed nondiscrimination testing. The appropriate correction depends on the error, the plan document, the timing, and the participants affected.

A late deposit may require restoring lost earnings to affected accounts. An excess elective deferral may need to be distributed by the applicable deadline. A missed contribution or eligibility error may require a corrective employer contribution. Practices should not assume that the same correction applies to every situation.

Document what happened, when it was discovered, who was affected, and how the correction was calculated. The IRS Retirement Plan Correction Programs and the DOL’s Voluntary Fiduciary Correction Program provide starting points for different errors. A TPA, ERISA attorney, or qualified advisor should review significant issues before corrective action is completed.

## Which Tax Benefits and Credits Should Practices Evaluate?

A 401(k) plan can serve as both a retirement benefit and a tax-planning tool for a physician practice. The right design may help physicians save more, reduce taxable income, and offer a competitive benefit to employees. Tax credits may also offset some plan startup, administration, or employer contribution costs for qualifying small businesses.

These benefits do not apply automatically. The outcome depends on the practice’s legal structure, payroll, ownership, employee population, compensation definitions, and plan provisions. A design that works well for a solo practice owner may create higher employee contributions or testing requirements after the practice hires physicians, nurses, administrators, or other staff.

Practices should evaluate these opportunities with their CPA, third-party administrator, and financial advisor. A coordinated review can connect retirement contributions with [tax planning for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians), practice cash flow, and long-term wealth goals. Before adopting a plan, ask the advisory team to model both the tax benefits and the full cost of maintaining the design.

### Deduct eligible matching, profit-sharing, and cash balance contributions

Employer contributions to a qualified retirement plan may be deductible when they satisfy applicable tax and plan requirements. Depending on the design, a practice may contribute through a matching formula, a nonelective contribution, or discretionary profit sharing. These contributions can support employee retirement savings while creating a business deduction for the practice.

Physician owners who want to save substantially more may also evaluate a cash balance plan alongside a 401(k). A cash balance plan is a defined benefit plan that may allow larger contributions than a 401(k), particularly for older or higher-income physicians. The allowable amount depends on age, compensation, service history, plan design, and actuarial calculations.

Cash balance contributions are not automatically deductible simply because the practice intends to make them. The actuary must determine the required contribution, and the tax professional should confirm deduction timing and applicable limits. Practices can review the IRS rules for retirement plan deductions before finalizing a contribution strategy.

### Distinguish pre-tax deductions from Roth contributions

Physicians and employees may be able to choose between traditional pre-tax deferrals and Roth 401(k) contributions. Pre-tax contributions generally reduce current taxable income, although withdrawals are usually taxed as income later. Roth contributions do not provide an upfront deduction, but qualified withdrawals can be tax-free when the account and distribution requirements are met.

This choice can be useful when physicians have different expectations about current and future tax rates. A high-income practice owner may prefer pre-tax deferrals during peak earning years, while a resident or early-career physician may consider Roth contributions when current income is lower.

Employee elections do not change whether the practice can deduct matching or profit-sharing contributions. However, the plan document and payroll system must distinguish the contribution types correctly. The IRS explanation of designated Roth accounts provides additional information on how Roth 401(k) contributions work.

### Account for tax-deferred growth and future taxable distributions

Traditional 401(k) contributions and investment earnings generally grow without current taxation while they remain in the account. This allows the full balance to remain invested instead of requiring annual tax payments on interest, dividends, or capital gains. For physicians saving throughout a long career, that deferral can be valuable.

The tax benefit does not eliminate taxation permanently. Distributions from traditional accounts are generally included in taxable income, subject to applicable exceptions and special rules. Physicians should consider required minimum distributions, retirement timing, charitable giving, Roth conversions, Social Security, and other income sources.

A large pre-tax balance may create higher taxable income later, especially when combined with practice sale proceeds or other retirement income. Coordinating the 401(k) with a broader [retirement planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help owners evaluate both the current deduction and future tax exposure.

### Check eligibility for the startup plan credit of up to $5,000 annually for three years

An eligible small employer may qualify for a tax credit for certain costs involved in starting a new retirement plan. Qualifying expenses may include plan setup, administration, and employee education costs, subject to statutory limits and eligibility requirements. For many employers, the maximum credit is up to $5,000 per year for three years.

The actual credit depends on factors such as the number of eligible employees, the number of employees participating, the type of plan, and whether the employer maintained another retirement plan during the applicable period. A practice should not assume it qualifies simply because it is privately owned or has a small payroll.

The practice should document eligible expenses and confirm the calculation with its tax professional. The IRS guidance on the small employer pension plan startup cost credit explains the general requirements and limitations.

### Check eligibility for the automatic-enrollment credit of up to $500 annually for three years

A practice that adds automatic enrollment to a new retirement plan may qualify for a separate tax credit of up to $500 per year for three years. The credit is intended to encourage employers to automatically enroll eligible employees, subject to the plan’s terms and applicable requirements.

Automatic enrollment may also increase participation among staff members who might otherwise delay signing up. The feature requires clear communication about the default contribution rate, investment selection, escalation schedule, and how employees can change or stop contributions.

Payroll must apply the default election accurately, and the plan must satisfy required notice rules. The credit may be available alongside other retirement plan credits, but the same expense cannot be counted twice. Practices can review the IRS information on retirement plan tax credits and ask their TPA whether the proposed design qualifies.

### Review small-employer credits for qualifying employer contributions

Some eligible small employers may receive a credit for a portion of employer contributions made for employees during the first years of a new plan. This credit is separate from the startup cost credit and the automatic-enrollment credit. Depending on the applicable rules, the credit may reach $1,000 per eligible employee in the first year and then phase down over later years.

Additional limits apply. Eligibility may depend on employer size, employee compensation, the type of contribution, and whether the employees meet the applicable requirements. The practice should also determine whether the credit applies to its proposed match, nonelective contribution, or another funding formula.

This opportunity may affect the choice between a match and a nonelective contribution. However, the practice should model costs after the credit period ends. The IRS overview of employer contribution credits can help practices identify the questions to review with their tax advisor.

### Coordinate deductions with entity structure, physician pay, and payroll strategy

The tax result of a retirement plan depends partly on how the practice is organized and how physicians are paid. An S corporation, partnership, professional corporation, and sole proprietorship may calculate retirement plan compensation differently. Owners should also review guaranteed payments, W-2 wages, K-1 income, bonuses, and compensation paid through related entities.

Payroll data must match the plan document. If compensation is understated, classified incorrectly, or processed after the applicable deadline, the practice may miscalculate contributions or miss a deduction. Affiliated practices may also need to be treated as one employer for certain retirement plan rules.

A coordinated review involving the CPA, payroll provider, TPA, and financial advisor can connect plan funding with the practice’s broader [business planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning). Do not finalize contribution estimates until the team confirms compensation definitions, ownership relationships, and the treatment of related entities.

### Weigh tax savings against employee funding and administration costs

A tax deduction or credit should be evaluated as part of the plan’s total cost, not as the sole reason to choose a particular design. A plan may create meaningful tax savings for physician owners while also requiring matching contributions, profit sharing, testing support, employee education, recordkeeping, annual filings, and investment oversight.

Those costs can change as the practice hires more staff, adds another location, or combines operations with a related medical entity. A cash balance plan may also require actuarial services and ongoing contributions that affect cash flow.

Ask the plan team to compare several scenarios using current census and compensation data. The analysis should show owner contributions, employee contributions, employer funding, tax deductions, expected credits, recordkeeping fees, TPA fees, audit costs, and actuarial expenses. It should also account for the practice’s ability to maintain the contribution formula during weaker revenue years.

A design that supports long-term retirement, tax, and retention goals may be more valuable than one that produces the largest first-year tax result.

## How Do Practices Implement and Review the Plan?

Implementing a 401(k) plan involves more than selecting a provider and signing plan documents. A physician practice needs a repeatable process for gathering data, comparing design options, assigning responsibilities, educating employees, and reviewing results. This process helps connect retirement benefits with the practice’s tax strategy, cash flow, staffing needs, and long-term business plans.

Because physicians may work across multiple entities, the first step is to bring the practice’s financial and organizational information into one review. A coordinated approach can uncover issues that might be missed when payroll, retirement planning, and tax decisions are handled separately. It can also show how the plan fits within the practice’s broader [wealth management strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

### Gather ownership, entity, payroll, census, and compensation data

Start by collecting the practice’s ownership chart, legal entity details, payroll reports, employee census, compensation records, hire dates, work status, and existing benefits information. Include physicians, advanced practice providers, administrative staff, part-time employees, seasonal workers, and employees who may be shared with another entity.

Review ownership carefully. A physician may own an interest in a professional corporation, management company, real estate entity, or another practice. Controlled-group and affiliated-service-group rules may require related businesses to be treated as one employer for certain retirement plan purposes. The IRS guidance on controlled groups can help identify relationships that need further review.

Compensation data should include wages, bonuses, ownership income, and any other pay included in the plan’s definition of compensation. Accurate data gives the advisor and TPA a reliable basis for modeling contributions and testing results.

### Prioritize owner, employee, tax, cash-flow, and retention goals

Before comparing plan formulas, decide what the practice needs the plan to accomplish. Physician-owners may want to make larger retirement contributions or create additional tax deductions. Employees may value a meaningful match, immediate access to contributions, simple enrollment, or useful financial education. The design should address both groups without placing unnecessary pressure on practice cash flow.

List the goals in order of importance. A practice might prioritize maximizing physician contributions, keeping annual costs predictable, increasing employee participation, or strengthening its benefits package for recruitment and retention. It may also need flexibility if revenue fluctuates throughout the year.

Tax planning should be part of this discussion from the start. The timing and amount of employer contributions can affect taxable income, owner compensation, and cash reserves. Practices can coordinate retirement decisions with a broader [tax planning strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) before choosing a final design.

### Compare projected contributions, fees, testing results, and tax effects

Ask for side-by-side projections of each plan design under consideration. A useful comparison should show estimated physician contributions, employee contributions, employer funding, administrative expenses, investment fees, and potential tax deductions. It should also show how results may change when compensation, staffing, or participation changes.

Testing results matter when owners and highly compensated employees want to receive larger contributions than other employees. A design that looks attractive in one scenario may produce different results if more staff enroll, an employee’s pay changes, or a physician joins or leaves the practice. Review projected ADP, ACP, coverage, and top-heavy testing outcomes before implementation.

Look beyond the headline contribution amount. A plan may allow substantial physician contributions but require higher staff funding or more administrative work. A retirement plan professional can explain the tradeoffs, while the tax professional can evaluate the expected deduction and broader tax effect.

### Coordinate the advisor, TPA, recordkeeper, payroll provider, and tax professional

A 401(k) plan usually involves several service providers, each with distinct responsibilities. The advisor may help with plan design and investments. The third-party administrator, or TPA, often prepares plan documents, performs compliance testing, and handles annual administration. The recordkeeper manages participant accounts, statements, and many enrollment functions.

The payroll provider must apply the correct compensation definition, calculate deferrals, transmit contributions, and maintain accurate payroll records. The tax professional can coordinate employer contributions with entity structure, owner compensation, estimated taxes, and deductions. Assigning each task in writing helps prevent important responsibilities from falling between providers.

The practice should also identify a primary plan contact. This person can maintain the service calendar, collect required information, approve payroll changes, and schedule regular meetings. Clear coordination supports the practice’s broader [retirement planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians).

### Complete plan documents, service agreements, investment selection, and fiduciary reviews

Before the plan accepts contributions, complete the formal implementation work. This may include the adoption agreement, plan document, trust arrangements, service agreements, investment lineup, payroll setup, and participant communication materials. Review each document to confirm that eligibility, compensation, vesting, contributions, distributions, and administrative duties match the intended design.

Investment selection also requires a documented process. Plan fiduciaries should evaluate the investment menu, fees, risk levels, diversification, performance information, and participant needs. A large menu is not automatically better. Employees often benefit from a clear lineup that includes diversified options and understandable education.

Schedule fiduciary reviews at regular intervals and document the decisions. The [Department of Labor’s fiduciary guidance](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/retirement/fiduciaryresp) explains the importance of acting prudently and in the interest of plan participants. Practices should confirm whether providers are meeting their responsibilities and whether fees remain reasonable for the services provided.

### Explain eligibility, enrollment, contribution choices, and employer funding

Employees need practical information before the plan begins. Explain who can participate, when eligibility starts, how to enroll, how to change a contribution rate, and what happens after an employee leaves. Use clear examples to show the difference between pre-tax and Roth contributions when both options are available.

Explain employer contributions just as clearly. Employees should understand how a matching formula works, whether nonelective contributions are provided, and when employer contributions become vested. If the plan uses automatic enrollment or automatic escalation, explain the default contribution rate and how employees can change or stop contributions.

Education should reflect the workforce’s different needs. A resident may need help starting contributions on a limited income, while an established physician may focus on maximizing contributions and coordinating the plan with other accounts. Enrollment meetings and concise written materials can improve understanding without overwhelming employees.

### Establish payroll, deposit, notice, testing, filing, and correction workflows

Create a written workflow before the first payroll runs under the new plan. Identify who confirms eligibility, updates payroll, calculates deferrals, approves employer contributions, transmits deposits, and reviews contribution files. Include deadlines for required notices, annual testing, plan amendments, and Form 5500 filing.

Deposit timing requires close attention. Employee contributions generally must be deposited as soon as reasonably possible after being withheld from pay. The Department of Labor’s contribution deposit guidance provides information about timely deposits and small-plan rules.

Document a correction process for missed deferrals, incorrect compensation, late deposits, failed notices, and excess contributions. Prompt review can limit the cost and administrative work involved in correcting an error. Keep payroll reports, deposit confirmations, testing results, notices, and correction records in an organized plan file.

### Review participation, fees, contributions, and testing results regularly

A plan review should assess more than investment performance. Track employee participation, average contribution rates, automatic enrollment outcomes, employer contributions, forfeiture balances, and the number of eligible employees who do not participate. These figures can show whether the plan is understandable and useful to the workforce.

Review total fees and services at least annually. Consider recordkeeping, third-party administration, advisory, investment, transaction, and consulting costs. Compare fees with the services the plan receives instead of focusing only on the lowest quoted price. Review whether the investment lineup remains appropriate and whether participants receive useful education.

The annual compliance review should include ADP, ACP, coverage, and top-heavy testing when applicable. Ask the TPA to explain any failed test, corrective action, and deadline in plain language. Regular reviews help the practice address small problems before they become more expensive administrative issues.

### Revisit plan design after ownership, staffing, pay, or cash-flow changes

A retirement plan should be reviewed when the practice changes. Revisit the design after a merger, acquisition, physician retirement, ownership transition, new entity formation, or change in the relationship between related businesses. Staffing changes can also affect testing results, contribution costs, eligibility, and the value of a particular allocation formula.

Compensation changes matter as well. A shift from salary to bonus compensation, a new productivity model, or a significant change in physician income may affect employer contributions and tax planning. Review the plan before implementing a major payroll change so the practice can identify unintended consequences.

Cash flow should remain part of the discussion. A plan that worked during a period of strong collections may require adjustments when the practice adds staff, purchases equipment, opens a new location, or experiences uneven revenue. Practices considering a transition or ownership change can coordinate the review with [practice owner planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning), keeping retirement, tax, and business decisions aligned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What should physician practices consider before choosing a 401(k) plan design?**\
Start with the practice’s ownership structure, related entities, employee census, compensation, cash flow, and retirement goals. The plan should balance physician savings opportunities with employee benefits, administrative responsibilities, and the practice’s plans for growth or ownership transition.

**Can a physician practice combine a 401(k) with a cash balance plan?**\
Yes, an established practice may combine these plans to create additional retirement savings opportunities for older or highly compensated physicians. A cash balance plan requires actuarial analysis and generally involves more consistent funding, so the practice should confirm that its cash flow can support the required contributions.

**How can a 401(k) help with physician tax planning?**\
Employee deferrals may reduce current taxable income when made on a pretax basis. Employer contributions, including matching, nonelective, profit-sharing, and qualifying cash balance contributions, may also be deductible to the practice. Tax results depend on the entity structure, compensation, plan terms, and applicable limits.

**What costs should a practice include when evaluating a 401(k) plan?**\
Look beyond employer contributions. Include recordkeeping, third-party administration, investment expenses, payroll integration, employee education, compliance testing, annual filings, audit costs, and possible actuarial fees. Compare the total cost under different staffing and revenue scenarios.

**Who should oversee the practice’s 401(k) plan?**\
The practice owner or appointed fiduciary remains responsible for oversight, even when tasks are delegated. A TPA may handle testing and plan administration, while the recordkeeper, payroll provider, CPA, and financial advisor manage other responsibilities. Written assignments, regular reviews, and organized records help keep the plan on track.

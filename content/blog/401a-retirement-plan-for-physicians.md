A 401a retirement plan for physicians may be an important part of an employer benefits package, but the name alone does not tell you what you must contribute or what you may keep when you leave. Your employer’s written plan terms determine contributions, vesting, investments, and distribution choices. Start with those terms, then consider the account alongside your other savings and career plans.

## What does a 401(a) plan mean for a physician?

A 401(a) is an employer-sponsored qualified retirement plan. The employer establishes the arrangement and specifies its features within applicable rules. Depending on the plan, it may require employee contributions, provide employer contributions, or combine both. The plan may also set limits on elections, investments, and distributions.

That design flexibility matters more than the label. Two physicians can each have a 401(a) yet face different contribution formulas, vesting schedules, investment menus, and distribution options. One employer might require a percentage of eligible compensation; another may provide some or all contributions. These are examples, not assumptions about any specific employer.

Do not infer that a 401(a) operates like a 401(k) because the names look alike. The summary plan description (SPD), governing plan document, account statement, and disclosures offer a more reliable picture. If a hospital, university, health system, or other organization offers several arrangements, confirm your eligibility and terms for each one.

## Why do plan details matter across a medical career?

A plan can affect payroll cash flow, ownership of employer contributions, investment costs, and the choices available after employment. These questions arise at onboarding, when comparing job offers, during a move between institutions, and as retirement approaches.

A candidate comparing two offers should ask what compensation is eligible, whether an employee contribution is required, when employer contributions vest, and what costs and distribution choices apply. A headline contribution percentage is not an apples-to-apples comparison without those terms.

A transition can raise a different set of questions. The account may contain employee and employer contributions with different vesting treatment. The old plan may allow the balance to remain, or it may offer distribution choices. Moving money immediately is not automatically the best step. Establish the current account value and terms before comparing available choices.

Reviewing the 401(a) with other accounts also helps avoid a narrow decision. A change to one investment allocation affects the overall mix; a transfer can affect other retirement assets. The goal is to understand what each account does and how the pieces fit together.

## Which documents should you collect first?

Gather current, plan-specific information: the SPD, formal plan provisions when available, latest statement, investment menu, fee disclosures, and employer materials. Save dated copies. If a document is missing or appears out of date, request the current version from the plan administrator or benefits office.

The SPD is designed to explain provisions in accessible terms, but it may summarize details rather than reproduce every governing provision. If a portal summary and SPD appear inconsistent, ask the administrator in writing which provision applies and request the relevant document or explanation.

* **SPD and plan provisions:** Find eligibility, contributions, vesting, distributions, and job-separation rules.
* **Statement:** Record total balance, contribution sources, transactions, and any vested balance shown.
* **Investment menu:** Identify available options, default arrangement, and election-change procedures.
* **Fee disclosures:** Separate investment expenses from account or plan charges.
* **Employer communications:** Collect contribution schedules and notices, then verify important terms against plan documents.
* **Personal records:** Keep payroll records, prior statements, forms, and administrator replies.

Make a document log with the name, date, account, and unanswered question. For instance, note “employee contribution required: confirm against current SPD,” then record the answer and source once verified. This avoids mixing a prior employer’s rules with the current plan.

## Which contribution rules should you examine?

Separate three questions: who contributes, how the contribution is calculated, and when it reaches the account. A plan may require employee contributions, provide employer contributions, or use both. The formula may apply to a plan-defined type of eligible compensation, and timing may follow payroll or other terms.

### Is an employee contribution mandatory or optional?

Find the exact section describing employee contributions. Does the plan require a rate, let you choose, or automatically enroll eligible employees? Can elections be changed? Clarify before adjusting cash-flow assumptions. If payroll deductions are required, ask how a leave, partial year, appointment change, or compensation adjustment is handled.

### What counts as eligible compensation?

Locate the plan’s compensation definition. It may differ from an offer letter or pay statement. Ask whether base salary, bonus, incentive compensation, call pay, or other earnings count. Do not assume that every kind of taxable pay is included.

If you start midyear, move from training to an attending role, or receive a bonus, ask how the definition and formula apply. Request a written explanation using your actual situation rather than relying on a general description.

### How do you calculate an estimate?

Translate the formula into dollars, labeling assumptions. If a plan required 5% of eligible compensation and the confirmed eligible amount were $200,000, the arithmetic would be $10,000 for a full year. That does not establish whether all $200,000 qualifies, whether limits apply, or how payroll timing works. Verify those points.

For employer amounts, determine whether the formula is a fixed sum, percentage, match, or another allocation. Check eligibility conditions and timing. If multiple plans are available, ask how contributions coordinate and what limits apply; do not add headline amounts and assume the total is permitted.

## How should you assess vesting?

Vesting describes the portion of an account a participant has a nonforfeitable right to retain under plan terms. Employee and employer contributions may receive different treatment. Do not assume that the full balance shown is vested.

Find the vesting schedule and how service is measured. Ask what counts as a year of service and how breaks or changes in employment status are handled. Compare the total balance, vested balance, and unvested employer-funded amount. If the statement does not separate sources, request a source-by-source calculation from the administrator.

For a hypothetical illustration, imagine an account showing $42,000: $24,000 associated with employee contributions and investment changes, and $18,000 associated with employer contributions and investment changes. If some employer contributions remain unvested, the amount retained after departure may be below the displayed total. The actual result depends on the plan’s schedule and account records.

Vesting can matter when comparing offers or considering a departure date. A larger stated employer contribution subject to a schedule is not directly comparable to a smaller amount with different terms. Consider eligibility date, service, and expected employment horizon alongside contribution rates; they do not decide whether to stay or leave on their own.

## What should you compare in the investment menu and fees?

Review the current investment menu, any default arrangement, election procedures, and expense disclosures. Then compare current holdings with investments across your other accounts. Looking at each account in isolation may hide repeated exposures or an overall allocation that differs from your intended approach.

Use consistent criteria rather than choosing solely by recent performance. Consider each option’s role, risks, expenses, and fit with your time horizon, risk tolerance, and other assets. Past performance does not establish future results. A qualified investment professional can assess the full picture.

Fees may include investment expenses and account or plan charges. Identify what applies and how it is assessed. Ask whether an expense is reflected in published investment information or charged separately. Compare similar services and investment types; a lower number alone does not establish suitability.

Save dated copies of menus and disclosures. Revisit them after a plan amendment, employer change, major time-horizon change, or significant household change. Avoid reacting to a short-term market move without considering the complete picture.

## How do 401(a), 401(k), 403(b), 457(b), and IRAs compare?

This table is a question guide, not a description of every plan with these names. Actual availability and tax treatment depend on eligibility, documents, current rules, and individual circumstances.

| Arrangement | General role                                                                                           | Questions to answer                                                                   | Records to compare                                      |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| 401(a)      | Employer-designed qualified plan; employee contributions, employer contributions, or both may feature. | Are employee contributions required? What compensation counts? How does vesting work? | SPD, formula, vesting schedule, statement, disclosures. |
| 401(k)      | Workplace plan commonly structured for salary deferrals and potentially employer contributions.        | What elections and employer amounts apply? What costs and options are available?      | Plan terms, payroll elections, menu, disclosures.       |
| 403(b)      | Workplace arrangement often associated with eligible public education or nonprofit employers.          | What investments, fees, employer contributions, and provisions apply?                 | Plan documents, employer notices, account statement.    |

Fill in a separate row for every actual account, not just every account type. A current 401(a) and a former employer’s 401(k) are distinct arrangements with distinct administrators. Mark unanswered items “confirm.” When multiple workplace plans are active, ask how contributions and applicable limits coordinate in your circumstances.

## How can you coordinate a 401(a) with other accounts?

Build an inventory of current and former employer plans, IRAs, and retirement arrangements connected with other work. For each, record type, administrator, balance date, contribution sources, vesting, investments, fees, beneficiary information, and distribution or rollover options. Keep confirmed plan facts distinct from personal preferences, such as wanting fewer accounts.

### Step 1: Establish the account’s role

Note whether the 401(a) is a current employer account, a legacy account, or a plan with mandatory contributions. Identify restrictions that actually apply, such as limited elections or distribution timing. Ask rather than assume that a minimum-balance condition or other restriction exists.

### Step 2: Map contributions

List employee and employer amounts separately, with calculation basis, frequency, and conditions. Do the same for other workplace plans. This clarifies payroll deductions and employer allocations without treating them as interchangeable.

### Step 3: Review the combined investment picture

Record broad investment categories and proportions from statements dated as close together as practical. Combine the information to understand household exposure. The purpose is not to make all accounts identical, but to see whether the total fits your time horizon and risk preferences.

### Step 4: Compare costs and practical features

Record investment expenses, plan charges, options, administrative access, beneficiary procedures, and distribution choices. Cost matters, but it is not the only consideration. Compare actual features and restrictions.

### Step 5: Assign unanswered questions

Send plan-interpretation questions to the administrator, tax questions to a qualified tax professional, and investment questions to an appropriately qualified professional. A rollover can involve all three areas, so make sure each person has the same account facts and timing.

### Step 6: Set a review trigger

Review after a job change, plan amendment, compensation change, new account, move into practice ownership, family change, or as retirement approaches. A scheduled review can prevent years of neglect without encouraging needless reactions to market fluctuations.

## Worked example: how might contribution figures change?

Suppose a hypothetical physician has $300,000 of annual compensation, confirmed by the plan as eligible. Assume a required employee contribution of 5% and employer contribution of 3%, both calculated on that compensation. The employee amount would be $15,000 (0.05 × $300,000); the employer amount would be $9,000 (0.03 × $300,000). Together that is $24,000 before investment changes, limits, timing, or other plan terms.

This is arithmetic under stated assumptions, not a description of a particular plan. Confirm that the employee rate is mandatory, that the full compensation amount qualifies, when the employer contribution enters the account, and how it vests. Also ask how the plan coordinates with any other workplace plan.

If the physician is considering a job change, the relevant comparison is not simply $24,000 saved versus $24,000 saved. Employee and employer contributions may have different vesting treatment. Ask the administrator to confirm the actual vested balance, whether the account may remain after separation, and what distribution choices are available.

If a bonus is excluded from eligible compensation or the formula applies only to part of pay, the estimate changes. A useful worksheet records the formula, compensation definition, dates, and assumptions. If the terms are difficult to apply, request a written example based on actual compensation.

## What should residents and fellows check?

Onboarding can be compressed, so request the SPD and contribution schedule early. Confirm when eligibility begins, whether contributions are automatic or elected, what payroll deductions will occur, and how elections can be updated. Ask how partial years and transitions from training into attending roles are treated.

When comparing offers, use the same checklist for each employer: employee contribution requirement, employer formula, vesting, compensation definition, investment menu, fees, distribution terms, and effective date. Save statements and plan materials when leaving a position, and record the administrator’s contact details.

## What should you check before changing employers?

Before the final week, ask the current administrator to confirm the balance, vested amount, contribution sources, and options after separation. Request distribution instructions, but do not initiate a transaction simply because a form is available. Ask whether the account can remain and whether conditions apply.

Obtain the prospective employer’s current plan materials. Compare eligibility dates, contribution rules, vesting, compensation definition, investments, costs, and distributions. If both plans are active in the same year, get guidance on coordination. A worksheet with columns for current plan, new plan, confirmed facts, and unknowns makes gaps visible.

## What can you do with a former employer’s 401(a)?

Leaving a job does not necessarily mean the account must move immediately. Depending on plan terms and applicable rules, possible choices may include leaving assets in the plan if permitted, transferring eligible assets to another arrangement, or taking a distribution. Each choice can have different costs, investment options, administrative effects, tax treatment, and protections.

Ask the same questions of each option: Can the account remain? What investments and charges apply? What future distribution choices exist? What receiving-account requirements or paperwork apply? Would a proposed transfer change tax treatment or how contribution sources are handled? Verify all answers before acting.

If considering a transfer, ask whether a direct rollover is available and what sending and receiving institutions require. Clarify reporting and whether any amount would be paid to you. Do not assume the steps, deadline, or tax result. The administrator can explain its procedures; a qualified tax professional can assess your situation. Keep forms and confirmations.

Consolidation may simplify administration, while keeping the old plan may preserve features that suit your needs. Neither preference alone settles the decision. Compare actual costs, menu, service, future access, and your ability to monitor each account.

## How should retirement timing affect the review?

As retirement approaches, focus not only on contributions but also on access and distribution choices. Ask what events permit distributions, whether partial or installment options exist, and what procedures apply. These are plan-specific terms; another employer’s options do not establish what yours allows.

List retirement accounts with balance dates, governing terms, and the administrator for each. Consider how a potential distribution fits with household spending, other resources, tax planning, and timing. Ask about processing well ahead of a desired start date, including required forms, acknowledgements, and expected turnaround.

Investment risk remains relevant. Money needed sooner may have a different role from money intended for a longer horizon, but the right allocation depends on the household and plan options. Avoid a sudden change solely because a distribution date is near; discuss timing and other accounts with a qualified professional.

## What mistakes can a careful review prevent?

* **Assuming every 401(a) is alike.** Use the current plan’s actual documents.
* **Treating the total balance as fully vested.** Verify contribution sources and vested amount.
* **Using the wrong pay figure.** Confirm eligible compensation before estimating contributions.
* **Comparing employer amounts without conditions.** Check eligibility, vesting, and timing.
* **Focusing only on performance or fees.** Review risk, diversification, costs, and plan features together.
* **Assuming plans coordinate automatically.** Ask how contributions and limits apply.
* **Moving assets before comparing alternatives.** Review staying, transferring, and distribution options.
* **Waiting until the last day.** Request documents before access to staff and records becomes harder.
* **Filling unknowns with assumptions.** Mark them clearly and ask the right source.

These mistakes often begin when a label or one number is treated as if it answers several questions. Verify the relevant terms and keep a short record of confirmed facts and open items.

## How can you turn the review into a decision?

Make a one-page summary with the plan name, administrator, document dates, employee and employer contribution rules, eligible compensation, vesting, balance, investment options, fees, distribution terms, and next review date. Label each fact with its source and identify unknowns.

Then write a specific next action: confirm whether bonus pay counts; compare the new plan after eligibility is established; or wait for written transfer instructions and tax guidance before acting. Match each question to the person who can answer it. The administrator explains plan provisions and procedures; qualified tax and investment professionals address their respective areas.

## What questions do physicians ask about 401(a) plans?

### Is a 401(a) the same as a 401(k)?

No. They are different plan types, and contribution rules may differ. Review each employer’s documents and ask how contributions, vesting, investments, and distributions work.

### Can I choose whether to contribute?

It depends on the plan. Some arrangements may require employee contributions. Check the SPD and confirm whether elections can be changed before adjusting payroll assumptions.

### Are employer contributions immediately vested?

Not necessarily. Find the schedule, confirm how service is measured, and ask for your current vested balance. The displayed account total may differ from the amount you retain after departure.

### Can I move a 401(a) to an IRA or another plan?

Possibly, but eligibility and tax treatment depend on plan terms, assets, receiving account, and applicable rules. Ask both administrators about procedures and consult a qualified tax professional before initiating a transaction.

### Should I leave an old 401(a) in place?

There is no universal answer. Confirm that the plan allows it, then compare costs, investment choices, service, distribution options, and alternatives with qualified guidance.

A 401(a) is easier to evaluate when you know the written rules, understand the difference between total and vested balances, and see how the account fits beside your other plans. Verify details with the administrator and use qualified professional guidance for decisions involving your broader circumstances.

Multi-state tax planning for physicians begins with a simple discipline: separate where you live, where you work, what income you receive, and what each state’s rules may require. A home move, locums assignment, rotating clinical schedule, remote administrative work, or ownership interest can put more than one jurisdiction in the picture. The useful first step is not to guess at the answer. It is to build a dated, documented account that a qualified tax professional can assess state by state.

## Why can a physician have tax questions in more than one state?

A physician’s work and personal life do not always fit within one state boundary. You may reside in one state, provide services in another, travel to a third for a temporary assignment, and have an ownership interest in an entity with activity elsewhere. Each fact can raise a separate question. A state may have rules for residence, for income connected to work performed there, or for business activity. The answer depends on the state’s law, the type of income, dates, and the complete set of facts.

Consider a physician who maintains a home in Maryland, covers a series of locums shifts in a neighboring state, and receives salary from a hospital system headquartered somewhere else. That description identifies questions to ask; it does not establish which states can tax which amounts. The employer’s address, the place a contract was signed, or the state listed on one pay statement may not resolve every issue. The dates and physical work locations, the employment arrangement, and each relevant state’s rules need review.

Planning early can make the work more orderly. It can help you identify records to retain, spot inconsistencies, coordinate with payroll, and ask better questions before a filing deadline. It cannot guarantee a particular tax result, remove tax, or replace professional review. The goal is a reliable process: observe the facts, preserve evidence, identify the jurisdictions, get appropriate advice, and then document the decisions made.

## What does a practical multi-state review cover?

A state-by-state review is more than checking the address on a tax form. It ordinarily starts by sorting information into several tracks, then asking a qualified preparer how each state applies its own rules. Keeping these tracks distinct helps prevent one convenient fact from standing in for the whole analysis.

* **Residence and home:** where you lived, which homes were available, when circumstances changed, and what evidence supports the timeline.
* **Physical work:** where clinical services and other work were actually performed, and on which dates.
* **Income and source:** what you were paid, by whom, under what arrangement, and what records describe the work or activity connected with the payment.
* **Business and ownership:** which entities or practices operated where, and how income, payroll, distributions, and responsibilities were recorded.
* **Payments and filings:** what was withheld or paid, which returns were filed, and whether notices or amended documents arrived.

These tracks overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A residence fact can matter even if most work is elsewhere. A work location can matter even if you commute from a different state. A business interest may prompt entity-level questions separate from the owner’s personal return. A withholding entry shows a payment was made or reported; it does not by itself settle the final filing analysis.

Use the review to produce clear questions rather than a premature conclusion. For example: “I lived at address A through June 12, worked at facilities in states B and C on the attached dates, and changed employers on July 1. How should each income category and period be evaluated?” That is more useful than “I think I became a resident in June,” unless a professional has already established that conclusion.

## How should you create a reliable residence and work timeline?

Begin with a calendar for the full tax year. Mark every day with the state where you slept or otherwise spent time, the state where you worked, and the reason for travel when relevant. If your workday crosses a boundary or you worked remotely, note that explicitly instead of forcing it into a single field. Your professional can decide which categories matter. The aim is to capture facts in a way that can later be checked.

Use contemporaneous records whenever possible. A clinical schedule, call roster, calendar entry, travel confirmation, lease, closing statement, utility record, or payroll document may help establish dates or locations. No single item proves every part of an analysis. A collection that agrees across sources is usually easier to review than a calendar reconstructed from memory months later. Keep a note when records conflict. Do not silently change a date just to make the documents align.

A useful log can include date, overnight location, work location, work type, employer or contracting entity, and source document. For days with no work, record travel or personal reasons if that is relevant to your own tracking. If the state is uncertain, mark it for follow-up. Avoid recording sensitive patient details; the purpose is to document the physician’s location and work arrangement, not clinical information.

At the end of each month, spend a short, fixed time comparing the log with work schedules, mileage or travel records, and pay statements. Resolve obvious errors while the details are fresh. If a shift was moved, a flight was cancelled, or a remote day was added, preserve the updated record and note why the original plan changed. At year end, the log should tell a coherent story, but it should not make legal conclusions on its own.

### A simple example of a timeline entry

Suppose you live in one state, drive to a hospital across a border for a single shift, then return home. Your log could identify the date, overnight state, hospital state, shift hours or workday, and the schedule or payroll record that corroborates the shift. A separate note can record that travel was for work. If a snow closure changed the location or cancelled the shift, update the entry and retain the message or schedule revision. The example does not tell you how a state will count that day; it gives an adviser traceable facts to evaluate.

## What residence and domicile questions should you take to an adviser?

Many physicians use “home,” “residence,” and “domicile” as if they always mean the same thing. States may define and apply these concepts differently. A driver’s license or voter registration can be one fact among many, but it should not be treated as a universal, automatic test. Likewise, owning a home, renting an apartment, or spending a certain number of days does not have one universal consequence across all states.

Prepare a residence file that records the history of each home: when it became available, who used it, whether it was rented or occupied by someone else, when it was sold or left, and where important household activities took place. Add the dates when you moved, changed employment, relocated a spouse or dependents, or changed state records. Include facts that seem to point in different directions. A balanced record is more useful than a selective one.

Ask the tax professional to identify the applicable tests and explain how the evidence fits them. Useful questions include:

* Which states may consider me a resident, part-year resident, or nonresident for the year?
* What dates or events matter for determining a change in status?
* How does each state treat homes available to me in more than one place?
* Which documents are most useful to support the timeline?
* Are there state-specific forms or statements that should accompany a return?
* Should I keep a day count in a particular format, and what counts as a day for the issue being considered?

Do not change a license, address, registration, or account solely to create an appearance without understanding the real legal and practical consequences. Records should reflect accurate facts. If you are considering a move, discuss the plan before acting when feasible; if it already happened, provide the actual chronology and ask how to handle it.

## What should residents and fellows review when training crosses state lines?

Residency and fellowship can involve a move to a new state, rotations at multiple hospitals, temporary housing, and a first professional paycheck. The workload makes it easy to delay records until filing season, when recollections have faded. A small setup routine at the beginning of training can preserve the facts without creating a second full-time job.

First, write down the date you left your prior home, the date you became able to use the new home, the date work began, and any period spent in temporary housing. Keep a copy of the lease or closing documents and employment agreement. Next, ask the program or employer where your work sites are expected to be and how payroll records state withholding. If rotations change during the year, keep each schedule version and record where you actually worked rather than relying only on the planned rotation.

Before a tax meeting, assemble prior returns if relevant, current pay statements, year-end wage forms when available, relocation records, a travel and work calendar, and notices from tax agencies. For a training year with a move, note which income was earned before and after the move and any work done outside the primary location. Ask whether part-year returns, income allocation, or withholding corrections need review. Do not assume payroll withholding determines the final liability. Do not alter withholding based on an informal estimate either; ask an appropriate professional about the consequences and process.

For example, a fellow may spend the first part of a year in one state, move for a new program in July, and complete a short rotation elsewhere in October. The right preparation is a three-part timeline with actual dates, addresses, work locations, and pay documents. It is not a self-assigned answer about which state is entitled to tax each payment. A preparer can use that chronology to identify the rules and return questions that apply.

Residents and fellows can also use the firm’s [resident and fellow planning resource](https://elitephysicianswealthplanning.com/physicians/residents-fellows) to organize broader career-stage questions alongside tax preparation. Keep that broader planning conversation separate from the tax preparer’s jurisdiction-specific filing advice, while coordinating the timing and records where appropriate.

## How can locums physicians organize each assignment?

Locums work often means that contracts, facilities, schedules, and payment arrangements change more than once during a year. Create one assignment record for every engagement, even if it lasts only a few days. The record should identify the contracting party, the facility where services were provided, the work dates, the state, the compensation arrangement, and expenses or reimbursements shown in the documentation. Keep the signed contract and any amendments together.

Separate planned dates from actual dates. An assignment can be extended, shortened, shifted to another facility, or cancelled. Maintain the original schedule and the revision, then note what occurred. Save invoices, payment statements, payroll documents, corrected tax forms, travel receipts, and messages that explain a change. If a staffing agency, hospital, and payroll company all appear in the paperwork, list each role as stated rather than guessing which entity controls a tax question.

For each engagement, ask the preparer to review whether services are treated as performed in the state where the facility is located, how the payment is characterized, and what filing or payment obligations may follow under that state’s rules. Do not assume the contract-signing location or the agency’s headquarters resolves the sourcing question. The actual services and applicable law matter. Also ask whether an expense reimbursement is reported or treated differently from compensation; retain the supporting agreement and receipts so the preparer can assess it.

At the close of the assignment, reconcile the contract dates to shifts actually worked and payments received. Note unpaid or delayed amounts, corrections, and any amount reported under a different tax document than expected. A simple reconciliation does not determine tax treatment; it helps identify missing documents before the return is prepared.

### Locums assignment checklist

1. Save the signed contract, amendments, and facility assignment details.
2. Record the facility address, service dates, and actual shifts completed.
3. Keep travel and lodging documents if they support the dates or reimbursement records.
4. Save payroll, invoices, payment statements, expense reports, and year-end forms.
5. Compare the state and amounts shown on forms with your own assignment log.
6. Ask a qualified preparer how each state and income category should be reviewed.

If a form appears inconsistent, contact the employer or payer promptly and keep the response. Do not ignore a document simply because it seems wrong, and do not change a return figure without professional guidance. The document may require correction, explanation, or another response based on the facts.

## What changes when physicians provide telehealth or remote services?

Remote services create facts that a familiar office-based schedule may not capture. The clinician’s physical location, the patient’s location, the employer or contracting entity, and the service arrangement may all be relevant to different legal or administrative questions. Tax sourcing is not the only issue that can arise; licensing and business rules may also need review. Do not assume that remote work is automatically sourced only to the state where you sit, or only to where the patient is located.

For each remote work arrangement, document the dates, the place where you performed the work, the entity paying you, and the role described in your agreement. Keep schedules and payroll records that distinguish clinical telehealth from administrative work if the records do so. Do not record unnecessary patient information in a tax log. Instead, retain appropriate employer documentation and ask which records can be used to establish work location without exposing protected information.

Bring the arrangement to a tax professional familiar with the involved jurisdictions. Ask whether the employer’s payroll setup reflects your work locations, whether the state treats different categories of remote duties differently, and whether a change in home location or work pattern should prompt a new review. Where licensing or practice rules are implicated, consult a qualified professional for those questions rather than treating a tax conclusion as a licensing answer.

Remote work can change in the middle of a year. A physician may move, take call from a different location, or shift from on-site to remote administrative duties. Record the effective date and save the related schedule or employer communication. The adviser then has a basis to evaluate whether the change affects state reporting, rather than relying on a general description such as “I worked remotely most of the year.”

## How should employed physicians plan a move or second work location?

When you move during a tax year, open a transition file before details scatter across email and payroll systems. Put the old and new addresses, dates the homes were available, physical move date, work schedule before and after the move, and employer changes in one chronology. Save lease, closing, utility, and relocation records that accurately support those dates. Keep pay statements around the transition because an employer’s withholding update may happen before or after the actual move.

If you continue working in a state different from your new home, ask whether each state requires a return, how the relevant income is sourced, and whether a credit for tax paid to another state may be available under the rules. A credit is not necessarily automatic and may not eliminate differences between state systems. The question is not merely “Did I pay tax somewhere?” but which state’s law applies, to what income, for which period, and what evidence is required.

Before the move, if possible, ask HR or payroll what information is needed to update withholding and when the change will take effect. Keep the response and confirm the first pay statement after the change. The employer’s process handles payroll administration; it does not replace independent advice on residence, allocation, or filing. If you work at multiple sites after moving, maintain a separate schedule of actual workdays and locations.

A second work location can be a regular commute, an occasional clinic day, a hospital assignment, or a new remote site. Make a list of recurring and one-time locations and identify the dates each arrangement began or ended. Ask your preparer whether the pattern changes any return or withholding questions. Do not rely on a colleague’s answer from a different state combination or employment arrangement; a similar schedule can still produce a different analysis.

## What additional questions arise for practice owners and partners?

Practice owners and partners should prepare both a household file and a separate business file. A practice may operate at sites in more than one state, employ or contract with people there, or distribute income to owners who live elsewhere. Entity type, ownership agreements, payroll, services, and business records can each affect the issues that a professional needs to evaluate. Personal residence and business activity should be coordinated, but they are not one question.

The business folder can include formation and ownership documents, agreements, payroll reports, distribution records, worksite addresses, a list of states where the practice has operations or people working, and prior business filings or notices. Add dates when a new site opened, a service arrangement changed, or ownership shifted. Keep a record of who approved each change and which professional was asked to review it. Do not assume that registration in a state, the entity’s mailing address, or an owner’s residence decides the complete tax treatment.

For each state connected with the practice, ask the business tax professional whether the entity has filing or payment questions, what activity is relevant, and whether owner-level reporting needs coordination. Then ask how the business answer connects to each owner’s personal return. A business-level filing does not automatically answer an individual’s residence question, and an individual’s return does not replace the entity’s responsibilities. Clear responsibility matters: identify who obtains data from payroll, who prepares entity returns, and who communicates changes to the personal preparer.

Before opening a location, hiring across a state line, changing where work is performed, or admitting a partner who lives elsewhere, seek advice early enough to understand the administrative steps. The point is not to treat every proposed change as a tax problem; it is to identify whether state registration, payroll setup, recordkeeping, or reporting questions should be addressed before the change becomes routine.

## How can you coordinate compensation records across states?

Physician compensation may include salary, bonuses, call pay, productivity-related amounts, partnership distributions, and payments for separate assignments. The relevant records can come from several systems and arrive on different schedules. Start by creating an inventory of income sources rather than assuming one annual wage form tells the whole story. For each payer, identify the type of work or relationship as described in the documents, the dates involved, the state information reported, and the contact point for questions.

Compare pay statements and year-end forms against the work calendar. Check whether an employer changed payroll systems, corrected a form, or reported state wages for a location that differs from your own record. Keep both the original and corrected document, and note when the correction was issued. If a compensation amount covers a period that spans a move or a change in work location, flag it rather than assigning it yourself to one state without review.

For a physician with both employed and contract work, maintain separate subfolders for each arrangement. Do not blend expenses, reimbursements, and income into one net number before the preparer evaluates the records. Preserve the gross amounts and supporting statements. If a payer provides a year-end summary that does not match your ledger, ask the payer about the discrepancy and document the answer.

Ask your professional which categories require additional detail and how they want the information delivered. Some records may need a state-by-state workday summary; others may call for a contract or entity document. A consistent package lets the preparer reconcile rather than reverse-engineer the compensation stream from scattered deposits. It also makes it easier to identify whether a question concerns payroll, classification, sourcing, or an incomplete form.

## What does withholding tell you, and what does it not tell you?

Withholding is a payment made during the year based on payroll or payer information. It is not a final determination of tax liability, residence, source, or whether a return is required. A payer may have incomplete information about where you lived or performed services. A move may not have been reflected immediately, or a payment may have been reported under a state setup that needs review. The reverse can also happen: withholding in one state does not by itself show that no other filing question exists.

Review each pay statement for state wages and withholding, and compare it with your timeline. When you find an unexpected state entry or missing withholding, collect the relevant records and ask payroll and your tax professional what to do. A payroll correction may be appropriate, but it may not resolve the return treatment for an earlier period. Avoid changing an employee form or filing position based only on an online explanation that does not address your facts.

If a professional recommends estimated payments or a withholding adjustment, ask how the recommendation relates to each state, the expected timing, and the assumptions used. Keep a record of the advice and the payments made. If income or work locations change later, tell the adviser; a calculation based on an earlier schedule can become outdated. The practical goal is to compare anticipated payments with a reviewed estimate, not to treat a withholding line as proof that the full obligation has been met.

Do not send money to an unfamiliar source in response to an unexpected email or text claiming a state tax issue. Verify notices and payment instructions through a known official channel or with your tax professional. Preserve the notice and the envelope or electronic record. This is basic document handling, not a conclusion about whether the notice is valid.

## How can you prepare for tax credits and overlapping state claims?

Physicians sometimes ask whether tax paid to one state can be credited against tax claimed by another. The availability, calculation, and limitations of any credit depend on the states, the kind of income, the returns, and the applicable rules. Do not assume a credit exists for every tax paid elsewhere, that both states calculate income the same way, or that a credit will remove the full difference.

To support a professional review, gather copies of the relevant state returns, payment confirmations, notices, wage forms, and the work and residence timeline. Identify the income item connected to the tax payment and the period it covers. If a state return is amended or a payment is refunded, tell the preparer handling other states as well; the overall picture may need updating. Keep a record of the order in which returns were prepared and any assumptions about another state’s calculation.

Ask the professional to explain the sequence and dependencies in plain language. For example: Which state’s return is being used to establish a figure on another return? Does a later correction require a change elsewhere? What documentation supports the credit claimed? Who will monitor a notice or refund that could alter the calculation? Answers should be tied to your jurisdictions and actual records, not a general rule of thumb.

When the state returns are prepared by different professionals, name one person to coordinate the data exchange. Share final, not draft, figures when possible, and identify any estimate that remains open. A short status note can prevent one preparer from relying on a number that another later changes. Coordination is especially valuable when an assignment, move, or business allocation affects multiple returns.

## How do you distinguish a personal return issue from a business issue?

State questions may apply at different levels. An individual return concerns the person’s residence, income, payments, and other facts relevant under the applicable law. A practice or entity may have separate filing, payroll, or reporting questions based on its activities and structure. A physician can have both sets of questions at once. Treating them as a single task can leave gaps; treating them as entirely unrelated can create inconsistent records.

Build an issue list with a column for the responsible person or adviser, a column for documents needed, and a due date or next check-in. For example, the practice administrator may provide payroll and worksite data, the business preparer may assess entity reporting, and the individual preparer may address the physician’s personal returns. The physician should confirm that each professional knows about the other relevant work and that the information shared is appropriate and secure.

At a review meeting, clarify scope. Ask whether a recommendation addresses the individual, the business, or both. If it addresses only one, ask who is responsible for the other side. Confirm whether the answer relies on an assumption about ownership, compensation, a facility, or a move. These questions are practical safeguards; they do not imply that one adviser should make decisions outside their professional role.

Keep the final records together by year and by entity or person, but avoid mixing personal and business originals in a way that obscures ownership. A clear file structure helps the next preparer understand which return, entity, or transaction a document belongs to. Use secure transfer methods for sensitive information and follow each professional’s retention guidance.

## What should you bring to a state-by-state tax meeting?

A focused meeting starts with a concise overview, not a stack of unsorted documents. Prepare a one-page chronology with residence changes, work locations, employers or contracts, and major business events. Attach an index of the supporting records. Label uncertain details as uncertain. This lets the professional identify what is known, what needs verification, and which legal questions depend on missing facts.

Useful questions include:

* Which states may view me as resident, part-year resident, or nonresident based on these facts?
* Which income categories need state-by-state sourcing or allocation?
* What does each state consider relevant about my home, travel, and workdays?
* Could I have a filing obligation even though a payer withheld tax elsewhere?
* How should we evaluate a credit or other interaction between state returns?
* Should withholding or estimated payments be reviewed, and what assumptions are being used?
* Does my practice or ownership interest create separate business filings or reporting?
* Which records should I retain, in what format, and for how long?
* Who will tell me when a new assignment, move, remote schedule, or practice site warrants a new review?

Ask the professional to explain the reasoning and identify any assumptions. If the answer depends on a date, work location, or contract you have not supplied, agree on how to resolve that gap. Ask whether the recommendation is limited to a particular state or period. Make a short written record after the meeting: question, conclusion or open issue, next action, owner, and target date. That record helps keep the decision distinct from an informal conversation or a future change in circumstances.

## What is a manageable year-round recordkeeping routine?

A useful system can be simple. Create a secure folder for each tax year, with subfolders for residence, work calendar, employers and locums, income forms, business or ownership, state filings, payments, and notices. Use consistent file names that include the date and source, such as “2026-08-14-hospital-schedule” rather than “document-final-new.” Keep an index that explains what each folder contains and flags documents still missing.

Each month, update your location log and compare it with schedules and payroll. Each quarter, review whether work locations, remote arrangements, contracts, or homes changed. When a material change occurs, send the relevant facts to your adviser rather than waiting for the annual meeting. The professional can tell you whether it requires immediate action or can be addressed later. At year end, reconcile the log with final work schedules and income records.

Use a secure channel recommended by your preparer. Limit access to people who need the records, and avoid sending tax documents through unsecured messaging. Retain originals or reliable electronic copies according to professional guidance and applicable requirements. If documents are corrected, preserve both versions and a note explaining which one is current. A tidy archive reduces avoidable back-and-forth, but it cannot settle a legal interpretation by itself.

Choose a routine that fits clinical work. A 15-minute calendar review at month end may be more sustainable than a complicated daily form no one completes. If an assistant or practice administrator helps gather business records, define exactly what they may access and who checks the final package. The physician remains responsible for telling advisers about personal changes that the office may not know, such as a move or a second home.

## What might a worked example look like without assuming a tax outcome?

Suppose an employed physician receives $240,000 in salary for the year, moves from State A to State B in July, and completes three locums shifts in State C. These numbers and labels are hypothetical and illustrate organization only. They are not a tax calculation, and the salary amount does not establish the tax due in any state. The physician should not divide the salary evenly by month and assume that is the correct state allocation.

The physician could create three workstreams. First, the residence timeline would show the move date, when each home was available, and supporting records. Second, the employment file would include pay statements before and after the move, the employer’s work location information, schedules, and the date payroll changed its records. Third, the locums file would include the State C facility, dates actually worked, contract, payment details, and any year-end form. The physician would also list state withholding shown on each pay statement and any questions about the forms.

At the meeting, the preparer might need to determine what rules apply to the residence change, how salary and locums amounts should be evaluated, whether any returns are needed, and whether payments or credits should be reviewed. The physician’s role is to supply accurate facts and follow up on missing documents. The preparer’s role is to apply the relevant state law within the agreed engagement. If the employer reports an amount differently from the physician’s records, the discrepancy should be resolved or documented rather than hidden by a rough estimate.

One way to make the example actionable is to write down three unresolved questions: “What dates determine the residence periods?” “What records describe work locations for salary and locums income?” and “Do the reported state wages match those facts?” The adviser may add questions about other income or entity activity. This method narrows a complex situation into verifiable pieces without suggesting a predetermined result.

## What are common planning mistakes to avoid?

* **Using one address as the whole analysis:** Residence questions can involve several facts and state-specific definitions. Give the professional the complete timeline.
* **Relying only on payroll withholding:** Withholding is a payment record, not a final decision about returns or liability.
* **Reconstructing travel from memory:** Keep a contemporaneous log and supporting schedules so dates can be checked.
* **Assuming a short assignment has no consequences:** Duration alone does not establish the state treatment. Ask about the applicable requirements.
* **Mixing practice and personal questions:** Coordinate the two, but clarify whether advice covers the entity, the owner, or both.
* **Waiting until filing season to report a change:** A move, contract, or new worksite is easier to examine while the facts are current.
* **Assuming every state works alike:** Definitions, forms, filing rules, and deadlines may differ. Verify each jurisdiction.
* **Discarding inconsistent documents:** Preserve the record and resolve the discrepancy with the payer or preparer.
* **Taking a colleague’s answer as your own:** Different dates, work structures, and states can change the analysis.
* **Making an unsupported change to payroll or a return:** Get qualified guidance on the facts and process before taking action.

These are process risks, not predictions that a particular physician owes tax or has made an error. A careful record and a timely professional review can help surface questions before they become a last-minute scramble. If you discover a possible mistake in a prior filing, gather the relevant documents and ask a qualified preparer what steps, if any, are appropriate.

## How should changes during the year trigger a fresh review?

Build a short “change trigger” list and keep it somewhere easy to find. Events worth reporting to your tax professional may include a move, a new home, a change in the number or location of work sites, a new locums contract, a switch from employee to contractor status, a remote work arrangement, a new state for practice activity, or a change in ownership. These events do not automatically create an obligation; they are prompts to check whether existing advice still fits.

When a trigger occurs, send a brief factual note: what changed, the effective date, which states are involved, what documents are available, and what remains uncertain. Ask whether the matter needs immediate review or can wait for the scheduled meeting. Keep the response with the year’s records. If the professional’s advice depends on a projected schedule, update them if the plan changes materially.

A quarterly check can prevent small changes from disappearing. Review your home and work locations, contracts, practice activity, payroll entries, and upcoming transitions. ## What is a practical sequence for the next 30 days?

If you have not yet organized a multi-state year, use a short sequence rather than trying to solve everything at once. The dates below are a planning cadence, not a tax deadline.

1. **Days 1–3: list the jurisdictions.** Write down states connected to your homes, clinical work, contracts, remote duties, practice activity, or payments. Mark anything uncertain.
2. **Days 4–7: build the timeline.** Put moves, home availability, work locations, assignments, and employer changes in date order.
3. **Week 2: collect records.** Gather schedules, agreements, payroll statements, year-end forms, payment records, business documents, and relevant correspondence.
4. **Week 3: reconcile differences.** Compare the calendar with pay statements and forms. Ask the payer about apparent discrepancies and retain the response.
5. **Week 4: request professional review.** Send a concise fact summary, identify open questions, and agree on who will handle each follow-up.

## How should you coordinate several state returns from start to finish?

When more than one state is involved, the order and handoff of information can matter. Start by identifying every return or question that might be in scope, then ask the tax professional to set a practical sequence. One return may use figures calculated on another return, or a later correction may change numbers already sent elsewhere. The professional should explain those dependencies for your case rather than relying on a generic sequence. Keep an up-to-date list of which returns are being prepared, by whom, and whether each one is draft, under review, filed, or awaiting information.

Agree on one source of truth for basic facts. This might be a shared timeline or an indexed file that includes the final residence dates, workday records, income documents, and business information. If two advisers keep separate versions, send them the same corrected document and identify which version replaces an earlier one. Do not assume that emailing a correction to one preparer automatically updates the others. A concise handoff note can state what changed, which periods and states may be affected, and what further details are still unknown.

Before filing, ask what review steps are included. Check that the preparer has the final wage and payment documents, the work location summary, and the relevant prior-year information. Ask how open questions are being resolved and whether any estimates are still being used. Review names, addresses, account details, and the state information shown on draft returns. The physician does not need to perform the professional’s technical review, but should flag obvious factual mismatches and ask for an explanation before authorizing a filing.

After filing, save the complete copies, proof of submission, payment confirmations, and any instructions about follow-up. If one return is delayed because another is not ready, ask the preparer to explain the options and deadlines in the states involved. Do not make an independent decision to skip a filing or payment because another return is pending. Once all returns are complete, create a short year-end memo stating what changed, what was filed, what remains open, and what records or dates to track in the next year.

## What should you do if a state sends a notice?

A notice should be treated as a document that needs review, not as proof that the state’s position is correct or that the physician has done something wrong. Note the date it arrived, the response deadline printed on it, the tax year and issue described, and any payment or information requested. Keep the envelope or the original electronic notice because delivery dates and identifiers may matter. Do not discard it after sending a photograph to an adviser.

Send the notice promptly to the professional who prepared the relevant return, and include the filed return and supporting records if requested. If a different preparer handled another state return that may connect to the issue, tell that preparer too. Ask who will respond, what information is needed, and who is responsible for meeting the deadline. If the notice involves a business, make sure the responsible business contact receives it without confusing it with the individual return.

Be cautious about paying or sharing personal information based only on an unexpected call, text, or email. Verify the notice through an official channel you identify independently or ask your tax professional how to confirm it. Use secure delivery for documents. Keep a copy of every response, submission confirmation, and subsequent communication, organized by state and year.

A notice may request a simple document, question a reported amount, or raise a more complex issue. The response should address the request accurately and within the relevant process. Do not speculate in a reply or send extra sensitive material that was not requested without professional guidance. A careful record of the timeline and a clear assignment of responsibility help keep a notice from becoming lost among routine clinical and practice tasks.

## What should you expect from a useful professional review?

A clear review should tell you what facts were considered, what issues were within scope, what assumptions remain, and what action is expected next. The adviser may need further documents or a separate specialist. Ask for the applicable jurisdictions and tax years to be stated, and clarify whether the work covers personal returns, a business entity, payroll, or only a specific question. This prevents an answer about one part of the situation from being mistaken for a conclusion about everything.

It is reasonable to ask the preparer to describe uncertainties. State rules can be fact-dependent, records can be incomplete, and an employer’s reporting may need correction. An explanation that names those limits is more useful than an overly broad assurance. Ask how a move, work schedule change, or later correction would affect the advice, and whether you should contact the preparer before the next filing cycle.

After the review, maintain a decision note with date, adviser, states addressed, key facts, recommendation, open questions, and next action. Store the note with the documents supporting it. If another professional handles a related return, share the relevant conclusion through a secure channel, with permission as appropriate. When circumstances change, bring the new information forward rather than assuming the old conclusion continues unchanged.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every physician file a return in every state where they work?

Not necessarily. Filing requirements depend on the states, income, work locations, residence facts, and applicable thresholds and rules. Have a qualified tax professional review each jurisdiction. Do not assume every state requires a return, and do not assume only your home state matters.

### Does employer withholding prove that the correct state tax was paid?

No. Withholding is a payment during the year, not a final determination of liability or filing requirements. Compare payroll records with where you lived and worked, then ask a tax professional whether corrections, additional payments, or returns should be considered.

### What records matter most for locums work?

Keep contracts and amendments, facility addresses, actual work dates, schedules, travel records, invoices or payroll statements, expense documentation, payment forms, and corrected documents. A dated calendar that distinguishes work locations from personal travel can help a preparer evaluate the facts.

### Can a financial planner decide which state taxes a physician’s income?

State tax determinations should be addressed with a qualified tax professional familiar with the relevant jurisdictions. A financial planning discussion can help organize questions and coordinate related decisions, but it is not a substitute for tax preparation or legal advice.

### When should I ask about a move or a new assignment?

When possible, raise the question before the change so you know what records and administrative steps may matter. If the move or assignment already occurred, provide the actual dates and documents promptly. A professional can tell you whether the matter needs immediate attention or can be included in a scheduled review.

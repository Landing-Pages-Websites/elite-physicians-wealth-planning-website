For many dentists, the practice is both a source of income and one of their largest assets. That connection can create opportunity, but it also means a significant part of your financial future may depend on one business. Practice costs, debt, staffing, and patient demand can all affect the wealth you build outside the office. * **Connect practice decisions with personal goals**: Review business cash flow, household spending, debt, and savings together to see how each affects your financial priorities.

## What Does Wealth Management for Dentists Include?

Dentists often manage two connected financial lives: their household finances and the finances of a practice. ## How Can Dentists Assess Their Finances?

Start with a clear view of both your household and your dental practice. Gather current financial records, identify your priorities, and note any areas where you need help from a financial, tax, or legal professional. A regular review can help you understand whether your cash flow supports your goals and where you may need to adjust.

### Review Income, Spending, Cash Flow, Assets, and Debt

Collect recent bank and investment statements, tax returns, loan balances, and practice financial reports. List personal and business income and expenses separately, then compare what comes in with what goes out. Include irregular costs such as equipment purchases, insurance premiums, continuing education, and estimated tax payments.

Next, make a net worth statement by adding your assets, such as cash, investments, real estate, and practice equity, then subtracting your debts. This snapshot can help you track progress over time. Set clear goals, too, such as reducing a loan balance, saving for a home, or increasing retirement contributions. [AMN Healthcare’s guide for new dentists](https://www.amnhealthcare.com/blog/dentist/securing-your-financial-future-a-guide-for-new-dentists) recommends setting financial objectives to guide decisions and keep long-term plans on track.

### Assess Practice Profitability, Debt, and Cash Reserves

If you own a practice, review revenue alongside payroll, rent, supplies, lab fees, insurance, and debt payments. Higher revenue does not automatically mean stronger profitability. Look at what remains after operating costs, taxes, and loan payments, and check whether cash flow changes across the year. Keeping business and household accounts separate can make it easier to understand each budget.

Then assess whether your practice has enough cash to cover expenses if collections slow or an unexpected repair arises. Review personal emergency savings separately, so a practice disruption does not leave your household exposed. [Mercer Advisors’ guidance for dentists](https://www.merceradvisors.com/professions/wealth-management-faqs-for-dentists) highlights budgeting and understanding overhead as important parts of cash-flow management.

### Balance Student Loan Payments With Saving and Investing

List each education and practice loan, including its balance, interest rate, required payment, and repayment terms. With this information in hand, you can weigh extra debt payments against retirement contributions and other savings goals. Your approach may depend on loan rates, cash flow, employer benefits, taxes, and when you expect to need the money.

Debt repayment and investing do not have to be an either-or decision. You might make required loan payments, contribute enough to receive an available employer retirement-plan match, maintain cash reserves, and put extra funds toward higher-interest debt. Revisit the balance as your income and expenses change. [Dental Economics’ article on wealth accumulation](https://www.dentaleconomics.com/money/investments/article/55397492/how-can-a-dentist-accumulate-wealth) explores how education and practice debt can affect wealth-building and retirement timing.

### Review Retirement Savings, Insurance, and Estate Documents

Review your retirement account balances, contribution rates, investment mix, and expected income alongside your preferred retirement timeline. If you own a practice, consider how a future sale might fit into your plan, but avoid relying on practice equity as your only source of retirement income. Think through several possibilities, such as selling, working part-time, or closing the practice.

Check your disability and life insurance, beneficiary designations, and estate documents, including a will or trust. Confirm that they reflect your current family circumstances, debts, ownership arrangements, and wishes. A coordinated [retirement strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help connect retirement savings and practice-transition decisions with broader financial goals. Make a list of any gaps to discuss with qualified financial, tax, and legal professionals.

## How Should Dentists Build an Investment Strategy?

An investment strategy for a dentist should account for both personal goals and the financial demands of running a practice. Choosing investments is only one part of the process. You also need to consider when you’ll need the money, how much risk you can take, and how practice ownership, taxes, and retirement savings affect your overall plan.

Start by reviewing your finances as a whole. Consider your household expenses, student loans, practice debt, savings, and any major costs on the horizon. Then decide which goals should take priority and how much you can invest without leaving yourself short on cash. A practice owner’s income and expenses may vary over time, while an employed dentist may have a more predictable paycheck. Those differences can shape how much liquidity you need and how you invest.

Your strategy should be specific to your circumstances, not based on a one-size-fits-all portfolio. Revisit it when your income, ownership, family needs, or retirement timeline changes. List the goals your investments need to support, such as retirement, a home purchase, a child’s education, or buying a practice. Estimate how much each goal may cost and when you’ll need the money. A clear timeline helps distinguish funds for near-term expenses from savings you can invest for longer.

Your time horizon, comfort with market fluctuations, and ability to absorb losses all matter when deciding how to invest. For example, a dentist preparing to buy a practice may need more accessible funds than someone focused primarily on retirement savings. ## How Can Dentists Protect Their Wealth?

Protecting wealth means preparing for risks that could affect your household, your practice, or both. Insurance, continuity plans, cash reserves, and estate documents each play a role. Review them regularly, particularly after changes to your income, ownership, family, or long-term plans.

### Review Disability, Life, Malpractice, and Business Insurance

Your ability to practice is a major part of your financial picture. Review your disability policy to understand when benefits begin, how much income it may replace, and how the policy defines a qualifying disability. Life insurance may help support dependents or address shared business obligations. Check that malpractice, general liability, and property coverage reflect the services you provide and the assets your practice owns.

Practice owners may also need coverage for property damage, business interruption, or the loss of an essential employee. Your needs can change as your income, staffing, and responsibilities evolve, so review policies with a qualified insurance professional. [Risk management and insurance](https://www.marinerwealthadvisors.com/our-services/dental-professionals/) belong in a broader financial plan alongside savings, investments, and retirement planning.

### Plan for Buy-Sell Agreements, Key-Person Risks, and Continuity

If you share practice ownership, a buy-sell agreement can explain what happens if an owner retires, becomes disabled, dies, or wants to leave. It should address how an ownership interest will be valued, who may purchase it, and how the transaction will be funded. Ask your attorney and tax professional to review the agreement and confirm that it reflects your current ownership structure and intentions.

Consider how the practice would operate if a dentist, manager, or other essential team member could no longer work. Document key responsibilities and identify who could step in. You can also discuss whether key-person insurance fits your situation. [Business succession planning](https://creativeplanning.com/private-wealth-management/dental/) can help protect practice value and support continuity for employees and patients. Revisit your plans as your practice or ownership changes.

### Prepare for Unexpected Costs and Cash-Flow Disruptions

Dental practices can face expenses that are hard to predict, including equipment repairs, technology upgrades, staffing changes, or a temporary decline in patient volume. Consider setting aside a cash reserve for the business and a separate emergency fund for your household. The amounts depend on your regular expenses, debt, insurance, and how predictable your income is. Review your targets periodically as those factors change.

Track cash flow to spot seasonal patterns, rising costs, and delays in insurance reimbursements. If you use a business line of credit, understand its terms and avoid treating it as a replacement for cash reserves. Effective [cash-flow management](https://www.merceradvisors.com/professions/wealth-management-faqs-for-dentists) can help you prepare for disruptions while keeping longer-term financial goals in view.

### Connect Estate and Legacy Plans to Financial Goals

Estate planning should account for your personal assets and your interest in the dental practice. Review your will, powers of attorney, health care directives, and beneficiary designations to confirm they reflect your current wishes. If you co-own a practice, coordinate these documents with your buy-sell agreement. Conflicting instructions about ownership or decision-making can complicate a transition.

Consider how you want your assets to support your family, charitable interests, or other priorities. Your attorney and financial planner can help connect those wishes with your retirement strategy, practice transition, and tax considerations. [Legacy planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/legacy-estate-planning) can help bring your estate documents and financial goals into alignment. ## What Financial Mistakes Should Dentists Avoid?

A strong income does not automatically create financial security. Dentists may be balancing practice expenses, student loans, taxes, retirement savings, and family goals, with decisions in one area affecting the others. An equipment purchase, for example, can change your cash reserves and the amount available for debt payments or retirement contributions.

A coordinated plan can help you see these connections and make informed choices as your income, practice, or priorities change. Keep an eye out for these common financial mistakes.

### Separating Practice and Personal Finances

Mixing practice and personal expenses can make it difficult to track business performance, set a realistic household budget, or prepare accurate tax records. Keep separate bank accounts and credit cards, and review practice cash flow alongside personal spending. Include costs such as payroll, rent, supplies, equipment, and insurance so you can see how overhead affects profits.

It can also help to set a consistent schedule for paying yourself instead of withdrawing money whenever personal expenses arise. If you own a practice, work with your accountant and financial advisor to clarify how you’ll handle compensation, distributions, and expense tracking. Mercer Advisors explains why dentists should [separate practice and personal expenses](https://www.merceradvisors.com/professions/wealth-management-faqs-for-dentists) as part of a clear budgeting process.

### Delaying Tax, Retirement, or Succession Planning

Waiting until year-end to review your finances can leave little time to make changes. Tax decisions, retirement contributions, and business structure may depend on your income and ownership arrangements, so review them throughout the year with qualified professionals. Dentists who own a practice should also give succession planning enough lead time to prepare records, strengthen operations, and explore potential buyers or successors.

Set an annual planning review, and schedule an additional one when you buy into a practice, bring on a partner, or expect a significant change in income. Starting early gives you more time to compare options and coordinate decisions. A [retirement strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help connect savings and retirement goals with the rest of your financial plan.

### Tying Too Much Wealth to the Practice

Your practice may be a major source of income and a valuable asset, but relying on it for most of your wealth can create concentration risk. Your earnings, business value, and retirement plans may all depend on the same practice. If patient demand falls, costs rise, or you need to step away from work, several parts of your financial picture could be affected at once.

Consider building savings and investments outside the practice while maintaining enough capital for business needs. The right balance depends on your cash flow, debt, goals, and time horizon. Review how much of your net worth is tied to practice ownership, then consider whether your personal investments are sufficiently diversified. [Wealth management for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) can help coordinate investment decisions with practice equity and personal goals.

### Making Investment or Insurance Decisions Without a Coordinated Plan

An investment or insurance product should support a clear goal and fit your broader financial picture. Investments can affect your taxes, access to cash, and exposure to risk. Insurance should reflect your income, family responsibilities, debt, and the financial impact of being unable to work. Buying without reviewing what you already own can leave you with coverage gaps, overlapping policies, or costs that do not match your needs.

Before making a decision, ask what the recommendation is meant to accomplish, what it costs, and how it works with your existing accounts and coverage. Share relevant statements and policies with your financial and tax professionals so they can consider the full picture. Review your choices when your practice, family, or financial responsibilities change, rather than assuming an old plan still fits.

### Overlooking Fees, Debt Costs, or Cash-Reserve Needs

Investment and account fees can reduce the money available for long-term goals, while loan interest can make debt more expensive over time. Review fees, interest rates, repayment terms, and potential penalties before deciding where to direct extra cash. Then compare debt payments with retirement contributions and savings needs. The right priorities depend on your loan terms, tax situation, and goals. [Dental Economics outlines how dentists can weigh debt and savings decisions](https://www.dentaleconomics.com/money/investments/article/55397492/how-can-a-dentist-accumulate-wealth).

Practice owners also need cash for payroll, supplies, repairs, and uneven collections. Set a reserve target based on operating costs and how predictable your cash flow is, then adjust it as the practice changes. A [practice and business planning review](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning) can help you account for business needs alongside personal financial goals.

## What Should Dentists Plan Before Retirement or a Practice Sale?

### Set an Exit Timeline and Estimate Retirement Income Needs

Start by choosing a target timeframe for retirement or a practice sale, even if you expect it to change. A timeline helps you identify steps to take before leaving, such as paying down debt, building savings, or preparing the practice for a buyer. Next, estimate your retirement expenses, including health care, housing, travel, and family support. Compare those needs with income you may receive from investments, retirement accounts, and any work you plan to continue after the sale.

A [dentist-focused financial plan](https://www.merceradvisors.com/solutions/professions/dentists/) can help connect your expected retirement income with your exit timeline. Review your estimates regularly, especially after changes to your health, family responsibilities, or business plans. Starting early gives you more time to adjust your savings and make decisions deliberately.

### Assess Practice Value, Readiness, and Succession Options

Your practice may be one of your largest assets, but revenue alone doesn’t determine its value. Profitability, equipment, staffing, patient retention, location, and how much the practice depends on you can all matter to a potential buyer. Consider working with a qualified valuation professional to estimate its worth and identify improvements that could make the practice more ready for a transition.

Then compare possible paths, such as selling to an associate, bringing in a partner, or approaching an outside buyer. Each option can affect your timeline, responsibilities, and proceeds differently. [Practice and business planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning) can help connect ownership decisions with your personal financial goals. Allow time to organize financial records, address operational concerns, and review proposed terms with your legal and tax professionals.

### Coordinate Sale Proceeds With Tax and Retirement Plans

The sale price is not the same as the amount you’ll have available after taxes and transaction costs. Before accepting an offer, ask your tax professional to review how the purchase price is allocated, when payments will arrive, and how the transaction may affect your tax situation. Depending on the terms, an installment sale may be worth discussing with your advisers.

Next, decide how the proceeds fit into your retirement income plan, investment strategy, and cash reserves. [Tax planning for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) and [retirement planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help coordinate these decisions. Avoid committing the funds before you understand the expected tax bill, payment schedule, and how much you may need for near-term expenses.

### Plan for Staff, Patient, and Practice Continuity

A clear transition plan can help maintain the routines and relationships you’ve built with patients and staff. Discuss how the new owner will communicate with patients, handle records, and manage appointment access. Work with the buyer to clarify staff roles, benefits, and the timing of any changes. Decide what employees should be told and when, in coordination with your legal and professional advisers.

If you expect to remain involved after the sale, put your responsibilities, compensation, and end date in writing. These details can affect the practice’s day-to-day stability and help you prepare for your next chapter. Include continuity expectations in the sale discussions rather than leaving them until the final handoff. A well-planned transition can support patients and employees while setting clear expectations for everyone involved.

### Update Estate and Legacy Plans for the Transition

A practice sale can change the value and structure of your assets, so review your estate plan before and after the transaction. Check that your will, trust, powers of attorney, and beneficiary designations reflect your current wishes. Think about how you want to use the proceeds, whether for retirement, family support, charitable giving, or other priorities.

If you have a business succession plan, update it to reflect the sale and remove instructions that no longer apply. Work with an estate attorney and tax professional to prepare or revise documents, particularly if the sale changes asset ownership or your family circumstances.

Choosing a wealth management firm is about more than investment performance. Look for an advisor who understands the financial demands of dentistry and can connect practice decisions with your personal goals. These questions can help you compare firms and decide whether their services fit your needs.

Dentists may be managing practice debt, equipment costs, payroll, personal savings, and plans to buy, grow, or sell a practice. An advisor with experience serving dentists and practice owners may be more familiar with how these demands affect cash flow and long-term planning. Firms vary in the services they provide. Some focus primarily on investments, while others also address taxes, retirement, insurance, practice finances, and succession or sale planning. You want to understand how advice about your personal investments accounts for the financial needs of your practice.

### Understand Fees, Conflicts, and Fiduciary Duties

Ask how the advisor is paid before you sign an agreement. Compensation may come from assets under management, flat or hourly fees, commissions, or a combination. Request a clear breakdown of the costs you may pay, including investment expenses and fees for services provided by outside professionals. Also ask whether the advisor receives compensation for recommending specific investments or products.

Clarify when the advisor acts as a fiduciary and what that means for the services you’re considering. Ask whether the duty applies throughout your relationship or only to certain services, and request the answer in writing. Titles and credentials alone do not explain how an advisor is compensated or what obligations apply. ### Ask How the Advisor Works With Tax and Legal Professionals

Financial decisions can affect several parts of a dental practice and household at once. Your business structure may influence how practice income is taxed and how you manage distributions. Retirement contributions, a practice sale, and estate planning can also require input from tax and legal professionals. Ask whether the advisor coordinates with your CPA and attorney, how information is shared, and who follows up on each recommendation.

Your financial advisor should not replace your tax or legal professionals. Instead, the advisor can help connect their work, identify questions that need specialist input, and flag recommendations that may conflict. If you’re considering a practice transition, ask how early the firm recommends starting financial, tax, and legal planning. A useful financial plan should reflect your goals, responsibilities, and comfort with risk, not just your income or account balance. Ask how the advisor learns about your priorities and how often you’ll review the plan together. Your needs may change as you buy into a practice, pay down debt, start a family, reduce clinical hours, or approach retirement. The firm should explain how it updates recommendations when your circumstances change.

Before choosing an advisor, clarify who your main contact will be, how quickly the team responds, and what you’ll receive after meetings. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**When should a dentist start working with a wealth management professional?**\
You can benefit from financial planning at any career stage. An associate may need help balancing student loans and savings, while a practice owner may be weighing business expenses, retirement options, or a future sale. Starting before a major decision can give you more time to compare choices.

**Should dentists keep practice and personal finances separate?**\
Yes. Separate accounts and clear records make it easier to track practice performance, manage household spending, and prepare tax information. You can still review both sides together to understand how business decisions affect your personal goals.

**How often should dentists review their financial plan?**\
Review it at least once a year, and sooner after a meaningful change, such as buying into a practice, changing your work schedule, or taking on new family responsibilities. Regular check-ins help keep savings, investments, insurance, and business plans relevant to your circumstances.

**Can a financial advisor replace my CPA or attorney?**\
No. A financial advisor can help coordinate financial decisions and identify questions for your tax or legal professionals, but should not replace their specialized advice. Clarify who is responsible for each recommendation and how the professionals will work together.

**What should dentists ask before hiring a wealth management firm?**\
Ask about the firm’s experience with dental professionals, the services and fees included, how it handles potential conflicts, and how often it reviews your plan. If you own a practice, ask how the firm connects business decisions with personal wealth, retirement, and succession planning.

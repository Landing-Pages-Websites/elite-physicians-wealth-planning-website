Retirement income doesn’t come from one place for many physicians. You may draw on a 401(k), 403(b), IRA, taxable investments, Social Security, a pension, or proceeds from selling a practice. Each source has different rules and tax consequences. A large withdrawal may affect your taxable income, while a Roth withdrawal may offer flexibility if you meet the requirements for tax-free treatment. A tax-efficient retirement income strategy for physicians considers how these sources work together over time. This guide explains the key choices, from account withdrawals and Roth conversions to healthcare costs and required minimum distributions, so you can prepare for the income you’ll need.

## Key Takeaways

* **Choose retirement accounts that fit your work**: Review employer plans, IRAs, and practice-owner options, including fees, eligibility, employee requirements, and contribution limits.
* **Coordinate contributions with taxes and other priorities**: Consider pre-tax and Roth savings, multiple income sources, student loans, and IRA balances before making changes.
* **Plan for retirement income, not just savings**: Factor in healthcare, Social Security, practice transitions, family goals, and taxes, then review your withdrawal plan as circumstances change.

## Why Do Physicians Need a Coordinated Retirement Income Strategy?

A physician’s retirement plan needs to account for more than a savings target. Years of training, changing income, practice decisions, family priorities, and taxes all influence how much you can save and how you’ll draw on those savings later. A coordinated strategy connects these decisions, helping you plan for the income and flexibility you want in retirement.

Many physicians spend years in residency or fellowship before reaching their full earning potential. Student loan payments and other expenses can make it difficult to save much during this time, even though starting early gives investments more time to grow. When your income rises, consider how to increase savings while also managing debt and adjusting your spending. Physicians’ extended training and later peak earning years can make the savings window feel shorter, as [financial planning research for physicians](https://curicapital.com/news-and-insights/8-common-physician-tax-considerations) notes. A plan tailored to your career stage can help you set realistic priorities and use higher-earning years intentionally. Physicians may earn income from a hospital salary, consulting, call coverage, or private practice. W-2 and 1099 income can involve different tax and retirement plan considerations, so it’s important to review how contributions fit together. Your current tax bracket matters, but so does the income you expect from investments and retirement accounts later. Pre-tax contributions may lower taxable income today; qualified Roth withdrawals may provide tax-free income in retirement. The right balance depends on your circumstances and future plans. Family priorities also shape your plan, whether you’re supporting a spouse, helping children with education costs, or hoping to leave assets to heirs. Factor in healthcare expenses, especially if you expect to retire before Medicare eligibility. ## Which Retirement Accounts Can Physicians Use?

Physicians can save for retirement through workplace plans, individual accounts, and plans designed for business owners. The right combination depends on your employer, income, practice structure, career stage, and retirement goals. A hospital-employed physician might have access to a 403(b) and a 457(b), while a practice owner may be able to add a Solo 401(k) or cash balance plan.

Compare each account’s tax treatment, contribution rules, fees, and access restrictions before deciding where to save. Limits and eligibility requirements can change, so confirm current rules with the IRS and review your plan documents. A coordinated [retirement strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help you fit these accounts into your broader financial plan.

### 401(k) and 403(b): compare pre-tax and Roth contributions, matches, fees, and plan rules

Private employers commonly offer 401(k) plans, while public schools, hospitals, and some nonprofit organizations offer 403(b) plans. Both may allow pre-tax contributions, which can reduce your taxable income now, and Roth contributions, which are taxed now and may provide tax-free qualified withdrawals later. Your current tax bracket, expected future income, and other retirement savings can help you weigh the options.

Review the employer match, investment choices, fund and administrative fees, vesting schedule, and rules for loans or withdrawals. If your employer offers a match, find out how much you need to contribute to receive the full amount. Also check how much you can contribute across multiple workplace plans, since certain limits are shared. The IRS outlines current 401(k) and 403(b) contribution rules.

### 457(b): compare contribution options and governmental and nongovernmental plans

Some state and local governments, as well as certain tax-exempt organizations, offer 457(b) plans. A 457(b) can provide another payroll-based way to save. If you also contribute to a 401(k) or 403(b), a 457(b) generally has a separate employee contribution limit, subject to plan rules and your eligibility. Check how the plan handles catch-up contributions, distributions, and changes in employment.

The distinction between governmental and nongovernmental plans matters. Governmental 457(b) plans generally offer broader rollover options and stronger protections for plan assets. In a nongovernmental plan, assets are generally part of the employer’s assets and may be subject to its creditors. Distribution options and rollovers may also be limited. Review the plan documents before contributing, especially if you may leave the organization. The IRS explains key 457(b) plan rules.

### Traditional and Roth IRAs: weigh deductions, tax-deferred growth, and qualified tax-free withdrawals

A traditional IRA may offer a tax deduction, depending on your income, tax filing status, and access to a workplace retirement plan. Contributions may grow tax-deferred, and withdrawals are generally taxed as ordinary income. Roth IRA contributions are made with after-tax dollars; qualified withdrawals are generally tax-free. Direct Roth contributions are subject to income limits, so some high-income physicians consider a backdoor Roth IRA.

Before making a contribution or conversion, check the balances in all your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs. The pro-rata rule can make part of a Roth conversion taxable when you hold pre-tax IRA money. IRA contribution limits and deduction eligibility also depend on current IRS rules. Review the IRS’s [IRA contribution and deduction limits](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/ira-deduction-limits), then consider how an IRA fits with your workplace plans and tax strategy.

### SEP-IRAs and Solo 401(k)s: compare contribution limits, flexibility, and administration

Self-employed physicians and practice owners may consider a SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k). A SEP-IRA is often simpler to establish and administer, and contributions are made by the employer. If your practice has eligible employees, you may need to make contributions for them under the plan’s rules. That requirement can affect the cost of the plan, so include staff contributions in your calculations.

A Solo 401(k) may allow an owner with no eligible employees other than a spouse to contribute as both employee and employer. This can provide more flexibility, but the plan may involve added setup, administration, and reporting. If you earn both W-2 and 1099 income, coordinate workplace and self-employed plan contributions to avoid exceeding applicable limits. The IRS provides details on [SEP plan rules](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-sponsor/simplified-employee-pension-plan-sep).

### Cash balance plans: assess added tax-deferred savings for eligible practices and high earners

A cash balance plan is a defined-benefit retirement plan that may let an eligible practice make substantial tax-deductible contributions for participating physicians. Contribution amounts depend on factors such as age, compensation, and plan design. An actuary determines the funding requirements, and older participants may be able to receive larger allocations than younger participants.

A cash balance plan can complement a 401(k) or profit-sharing plan for a profitable practice seeking additional tax-deferred savings. It also comes with ongoing costs and commitments, including actuarial and administrative fees, as well as required contributions for eligible employees. Before adopting one, model its effect on practice cash flow and staff costs. [Practice and business planning for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning) can help connect retirement benefits with staffing, ownership, and long-term business decisions.

### HSAs and taxable brokerage accounts: add healthcare savings and accessible investments

A Health Savings Account (HSA) is available to people enrolled in an eligible high-deductible health plan. Contributions may be tax-deductible or made pre-tax through payroll, investment growth is tax-deferred, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. After age 65, nonmedical withdrawals are generally taxed as income, while qualified medical withdrawals remain tax-free. Confirm your eligibility and current contribution limit before contributing. The IRS explains [HSA rules and qualified expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969).

A taxable brokerage account has no annual contribution limit and can provide access to investments before retirement age. Dividends, interest, and realized gains may create a tax bill, but the account can complement retirement savings when you want flexibility for early retirement, large expenses, or other goals. The best mix of HSA, taxable, and retirement accounts depends on your expected spending and tax picture. A coordinated [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you assess how each account supports your longer-term plan.

## How Does Employment Type Shape Retirement Account Choices?

Your employment structure affects which retirement plans you can use, how contributions are calculated, and whether your employees must be included. It also shapes how workplace savings interact with IRAs and tax planning. Review your options whenever you change roles, add contract work, or hire staff.

### Employed physicians: review employer matches, investment options, fees, and plan features

If you work for a hospital, health system, or private employer, start by reviewing the details of its 401(k) or 403(b). If the plan offers a match, contributing enough to receive the full amount is often a practical first step. Then compare the investment choices, fees, vesting schedule, and availability of pre-tax and Roth contributions. These features affect both your take-home pay today and the taxes you may owe when you withdraw money in retirement.

Some employers also offer a 457(b) plan. Its rules differ depending on whether it’s governmental or nongovernmental, so review the plan documents before contributing. Check current [IRS contribution limits](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-contributions) as you set your savings rate. A physician-focused [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help coordinate workplace savings with your broader financial goals.

### Self-employed physicians: compare Solo 401(k) and SEP-IRA rules and contribution flexibility

Physicians with 1099 income may be able to save through a Solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA. A Solo 401(k) can allow you to contribute as both an employee and an employer. A SEP-IRA is funded through employer contributions, with amounts generally based on eligible compensation. Your choice depends on how much you plan to save, your income, whether you have employees, and how much plan administration you’re prepared to handle.

A Solo 401(k) is generally designed for a business owner with no eligible employees other than a spouse. A SEP-IRA may be simpler to establish, but you must follow plan rules for eligible employees. Review the [IRS guidance on SEP plans](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plans-faqs-regarding-seps), and confirm current limits and calculations before choosing a plan. Include business cash flow and estimated tax payments in your decision.

### Practice owners: consider employee eligibility, plan costs, and staff benefits

If you own a medical practice, evaluate how a retirement plan will work for both you and your staff. Review employee eligibility rules, required contributions, setup and administration costs, and the plan’s effect on employee benefits. A plan that supports your personal savings goals may also require contributions for eligible employees or additional testing and administration.

Some practice owners consider pairing a 401(k) with a cash balance plan to increase tax-deferred savings. Potential contributions depend on factors such as age, compensation, and plan design, and the plan typically involves ongoing administration and costs. Compare the projected benefits for you and your employees before moving forward. Elite Physician Wealth Planning’s [practice and business planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning) can help you consider retirement decisions alongside your practice’s financial needs.

### Physicians with multiple employers or W-2 and 1099 income: coordinate plan participation and contribution limits

Physicians who work for multiple employers, take locum tenens assignments, or combine W-2 work with 1099 income may have access to several retirement plans. Don’t assume each plan gives you a separate employee contribution limit. Employee salary deferrals to 401(k) and 403(b) plans generally count toward a shared annual limit. Employer contributions follow separate rules, and 457(b) plans may have their own limits.

Keep a record of contributions across every employer plan and self-employed plan. Check with each plan administrator about how contributions are classified, and ask a tax professional to confirm how the plans interact. This coordination matters if you change jobs or add contract work during the year, since excess contributions may require correction and can have tax consequences.

### Check how workplace plans affect IRA deductions and Backdoor Roth IRA eligibility

Participation in a workplace retirement plan can affect whether you may deduct traditional IRA contributions. Eligibility depends on factors such as your income and tax filing status. Direct Roth IRA contributions also have income limits, which can change, so check the current [IRS rules for IRA deductions](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/ira-deduction-limits) before contributing.

A Backdoor Roth IRA conversion may create a tax bill if you hold pre-tax money in traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRAs. Under the pro-rata rule, the IRS considers those IRA balances when determining how much of a conversion is taxable. An employer plan may accept eligible rollovers from pre-tax IRAs, but acceptance depends on the plan. Before converting, review your IRA balances, confirm the plan’s rollover rules, and coordinate tax reporting, including Form 8606, with a qualified tax professional.

## How Can Physicians Maximize Contributions and Manage Taxes?

For physicians, saving for retirement can involve more than increasing contributions to one account. You may have access to multiple employer plans, earn both W-2 and 1099 income, or own a practice with employees. Each arrangement can come with different contribution limits, tax rules, and costs. Start by listing your accounts, employer matches, plan fees, deadlines, and any requirements that apply to your staff.

Then consider how each contribution fits your tax picture and other financial priorities. Pre-tax contributions may lower taxable income today, while Roth contributions may offer tax-free qualified withdrawals later. The right mix depends on your current and expected tax rates, retirement income, and goals. Check the [IRS’s current contribution limits](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-contributions) each year, and coordinate decisions with your broader [tax strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians).

### Get the full employer match and know annual plan limits

If your employer matches contributions to a 401(k) or 403(b), consider contributing enough to receive the full match, provided it fits your budget. The match is part of your compensation, though a vesting schedule may determine when you own those employer contributions. Review your plan documents to understand the match formula, vesting rules, and deadlines for enrolling or changing your contribution rate.

Also check the annual limits for your accounts. Employee salary deferrals, employer contributions, and catch-up contributions may be subject to different rules. Limits can change, so avoid relying on figures from a previous year. The [IRS contribution guidance](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-contributions) is a useful reference, but your plan administrator can explain how the rules apply to your specific plan.

### Coordinate employee contributions across employer plans

If you work for more than one hospital, university, or medical group, you may be able to contribute to multiple workplace plans. However, employee salary deferrals to 401(k) and 403(b) plans generally count toward a shared annual limit. Payroll systems may not track what you contribute through another employer, so keep your own records and adjust elections before exceeding the limit. Excess deferrals can create tax and reporting issues.

Some plans, including eligible 457(b) plans, may have separate limits. The rules depend on the plan and employer, and employer contributions follow different rules from employee deferrals. If you also earn 1099 income, review your workplace contributions alongside any self-employed retirement plan. Ask each plan administrator how its rules apply, then coordinate contributions with a tax professional.

### Choose pre-tax or Roth contributions based on current and expected tax rates

Pre-tax contributions generally reduce taxable income in the year you make them, while withdrawals are usually taxed as income. Roth contributions are made with after-tax dollars, but qualified withdrawals are generally tax-free. A physician in a high-income year may value the current tax deduction. Someone early in a career, or expecting substantial taxable income in retirement, may prefer to build more Roth savings. Neither option is right for everyone.

Consider how your tax situation may change over time, including future employment, practice-sale proceeds, investment income, and required distributions. If your plan allows it, splitting contributions between pre-tax and Roth accounts can provide flexibility later. This choice is one part of a coordinated [retirement strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians), not simply a decision based on this year’s paycheck.

### Compare cash balance plans with 401(k) or 403(b) savings

A cash balance plan is a type of defined benefit plan that may allow eligible physicians to save additional tax-deferred funds beyond a 401(k) or 403(b). Contribution amounts depend on factors such as age, compensation, and plan design. The plan also involves setup and administration costs, as well as funding commitments that may continue even when practice revenue changes.

Before adopting one, compare its potential tax advantages with its cost and obligations to eligible employees. A design that suits a solo practice may not fit a group with more staff or variable cash flow. Practice owners should ask a qualified plan professional to review employee eligibility, funding requirements, and plan design. Consider how retirement savings fit into your wider [practice and business plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning).

### Use catch-up contributions when eligible and consider how IRA balances affect Roth strategies

If you meet the age requirements, catch-up contributions may let you save above the standard limit in certain workplace plans and IRAs. Eligibility and limits vary by account, so check the current rules before adjusting your contributions. Catch-up saving can be useful for physicians whose earnings rose later in their careers, but it should still fit with your retirement timeline and near-term financial needs.

High-income physicians may also consider a Backdoor Roth IRA. Before making a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution and converting it to a Roth, review your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances. The pro-rata rule can make part of a conversion taxable. The [IRS instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report nondeductible contributions and conversions. Ask a tax professional to review your situation, including whether an employer plan accepts eligible pre-tax IRA rollovers.

### Balance retirement contributions with emergency savings, debt, and other priorities

Retirement contributions are important, but they are not the only call on your income. Maintain accessible savings for emergencies and irregular expenses, especially if you own a practice or rely on variable 1099 income. Weigh contributions against high-interest debt, student loan payments, insurance needs, and upcoming expenses. Putting every available dollar into an account with withdrawal restrictions may leave you short of cash when you need it.

If you are pursuing Public Service Loan Forgiveness or income-driven repayment, check how your specific retirement contributions may affect the income used to calculate payments. Rules can depend on the repayment plan and contribution type. Set a savings target that accounts for debt, family needs, cash reserves, and retirement goals together. A coordinated financial plan can help align these priorities with your career and practice plans.

## How Does a Backdoor Roth IRA Work for High-Income Physicians?

A Backdoor Roth IRA is a way to fund a Roth IRA indirectly. The process generally involves making a nondeductible contribution to a traditional IRA, then converting that money to a Roth IRA. High income can limit eligibility for direct Roth IRA contributions, but it does not prevent someone from making a Roth conversion. You still need eligible compensation to contribute to an IRA, and annual contribution limits apply.

The steps may be straightforward, but the tax outcome depends on your other accounts and income for the year. Pre-tax IRA balances can make part of a conversion taxable, and a conversion may affect your overall tax bill. Before moving funds, review your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances, your workplace plan rules, and your expected income. A coordinated [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help you assess how the choice fits with your compensation, retirement savings, and other financial priorities.

### Contribute to a traditional IRA without a deduction, then convert it to a Roth IRA

The first step is generally to contribute to a traditional IRA and treat the contribution as nondeductible on your tax return. You can then convert the funds to a Roth IRA. The contribution must follow the annual IRA limit and compensation rules. Unlike direct Roth IRA contributions, Roth conversions are not restricted by income limits.

Any investment growth or pre-tax money included in the conversion may be taxable. To keep the records clear, note the contribution date and amount, and save the conversion confirmation and account statements. The [IRS guidance on traditional IRA contributions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a) explains the rules for contributions and deductions. Before starting, consider how the contribution and conversion fit with your full financial picture and [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians).

### Check the pro-rata rule and traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances

The pro-rata rule can make part of a Roth conversion taxable, even if your latest traditional IRA contribution was nondeductible. For this calculation, the IRS generally considers your combined traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances. You cannot choose to convert only the after-tax dollars while leaving all pre-tax money behind.

Review every account in these categories, including IRAs from former employers or self-employment. The year-end value of those accounts can affect the calculation for that tax year. The [IRS instructions for Form 8606](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain how to report nondeductible contributions and conversions. If your account history is complex, ask your tax professional to review the balances and estimate the taxable amount before you convert.

### Check whether an employer plan accepts eligible pre-tax IRA rollovers

If pre-tax IRA balances could make a Backdoor Roth conversion costly, check whether your employer’s 401(k) or 403(b) plan accepts eligible rollovers from a traditional IRA. Some plans accept them, while others do not. Ask the plan administrator which assets qualify, what documents are required, and how the plan handles after-tax IRA basis.

In some cases, moving eligible pre-tax IRA money into a workplace plan may reduce the pre-tax IRA balance used in the pro-rata calculation. The plan must accept the rollover, and the funds must meet the applicable rules. Do not start a transfer until you confirm the details with the plan administrator and tax professional. The [IRS rollover guidance](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc413) outlines general tax considerations for moving retirement assets.

### Coordinate conversions, tax reporting, and workplace plan contributions

A Backdoor Roth IRA requires accurate tax reporting as well as a transfer between accounts. Nondeductible traditional IRA contributions and Roth conversions are generally reported on IRS Form 8606. Keep account statements and transaction records so your tax preparer can track your after-tax basis and determine how much of the conversion is taxable.

Coordinate the conversion with your other income and retirement contributions for the year. Workplace plan contributions follow separate rules and limits, and a conversion may increase your taxable income. The [Form 8606 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8606) explain the reporting process. A physician-focused [retirement planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help connect these account decisions with employer plans, practice income, and your broader savings goals.

### Weigh Roth savings against your time horizon, current taxes, and expected retirement income

Roth savings can offer tax-free qualified withdrawals, but converting money may create a tax bill now. Consider how long the funds may remain invested, whether you can pay the tax from cash outside the IRA, and how the conversion could affect your taxable income for the year. Your expected retirement income, including investment income and required minimum distributions, also matters.

A resident with decades until retirement may weigh the tradeoffs differently from a late-career physician with significant tax-deferred savings. Compare the potential value of future Roth withdrawals with the immediate tax cost, and consider how each option fits your retirement timeline and family priorities. A [comprehensive wealth management plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) can help you evaluate Roth savings alongside your investments, tax outlook, and other financial goals.

## How Do Age, Income, and Retirement Goals Shape a Physician’s Strategy?

Age and income help determine how much a physician can save, but they are only part of the picture. Student loans, employer benefits, practice ownership, family responsibilities, tax exposure, and the desired retirement date all affect which accounts may fit and when to use them. A resident with a long investment horizon may make different choices from a practice owner nearing retirement, even if both have similar long-term goals.

Treat your strategy as a plan that changes with your career. Early on, you may focus on building savings while managing debt. As income grows, you may have more room to contribute across different account types. Later, you may need to plan carefully for withdrawals, healthcare costs, and the tax impact of converting or inheriting retirement assets. Physicians who own a practice should also consider how retirement plan decisions affect employees and the business.

Reviewing these choices together can help you avoid decisions that work well in isolation but conflict with other priorities. For example, a contribution that reduces taxable income may also affect a student loan payment calculation. A Roth conversion may support future tax flexibility but increase this year’s tax bill. A physician-focused [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can connect these decisions with your broader financial plan and adapt as your goals or circumstances change.

### Residents and early-career physicians: balance debt repayment, Roth savings, and time to invest

Residency and the first years of practice often bring modest earnings alongside substantial student loans. Start by covering essential expenses and building an emergency reserve, then compare debt repayment with retirement contributions. If your employer offers a match, contributing enough to receive the full match can be a practical first step. Roth contributions may also suit some early-career physicians who expect higher earnings later, since qualified withdrawals are generally tax-free.

Starting early gives investments more time to grow, but investing should not automatically take priority over debt repayment. Compare loan interest rates, repayment options, and other financial needs before deciding how to divide your income. [Farther’s physician retirement guidance](https://www.farther.com/foundations/retirement-planning-for-physicians-strategies-best-practices) discusses balancing long-term savings with challenges such as student debt.

### Midcareer physicians: save more as income rises and diversify account tax treatments

As earnings increase, revisit your savings rate instead of allowing every raise to go toward higher spending. Check whether you are using available workplace plans, employer matches, and other eligible accounts. A higher income may also place you in a different tax bracket, so consider how pre-tax, Roth, and taxable investments could work together. The appropriate mix depends on your current tax rate, expected retirement income, and each plan’s rules.

Physicians with both W-2 and 1099 income should review contribution limits across their plans to avoid exceeding applicable caps. Check plan fees and investment options, too. A coordinated [wealth management plan for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) can connect investment decisions with tax planning and your wider financial picture.

### Late-career physicians: assess catch-up contributions, cash balance plans, and Roth conversions

Physicians nearing retirement may have fewer years to save, but peak earnings can make larger contributions possible. Review the current catch-up contribution rules for your age and workplace plan. Practice owners may also assess whether a cash balance plan fits their goals, income, business structure, and employee obligations. These plans may allow substantial tax-deferred contributions, but costs and plan design matter. Compare them with other savings options before making a commitment.

Roth conversions may make sense in some years, though converting pre-tax money generally creates taxable income in the year of conversion. Consider the effect on your tax bracket, other income, and any pre-tax IRA balances. [Waverly Advisors’ overview of cash balance plans](https://waverly-advisors.com/insights/financial-challenges-physicians-face-and-how-to-overcome-them) explains how one might fit a practice owner’s retirement strategy. Coordinate any decision with your tax and [retirement planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians).

### Early retirees: plan for income and health insurance before Medicare eligibility

Retiring before age 65 creates a health insurance planning challenge: Medicare eligibility generally begins at 65, so you will need to plan for coverage in the years before then. Compare options such as coverage through a spouse, a former employer, or the Health Insurance Marketplace. Include premiums and out-of-pocket costs in your budget, since healthcare expenses can affect how much you need to withdraw from savings.

Build an income plan for this period that does not force poorly timed withdrawals. Consider how part-time work, taxable investments, and retirement accounts may fit together, and revisit the plan if your retirement date or health needs change. [Farther’s discussion of healthcare costs in retirement](https://www.farther.com/foundations/6-retirement-planning-mistakes-you-should-avoid) highlights why medical expenses deserve attention before you leave work.

### Physicians pursuing Public Service Loan Forgiveness: consider how contributions affect income-driven repayment calculations

Physicians pursuing Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF) should coordinate retirement contributions with their student loan strategy. Depending on the repayment plan and current rules, income-driven payments may be based on income reported on a federal tax return. Some pre-tax contributions can reduce adjusted gross income, which may affect a payment calculation. The result depends on the borrower’s circumstances, repayment plan, and applicable program requirements.

Before changing contributions or tax filing choices, check how the decision could affect both loan payments and long-term savings. Confirm details with your loan servicer and a tax professional familiar with your situation. [Curic Capital’s physician tax guidance](https://curicapital.com/news-and-insights/8-common-physician-tax-considerations) explains why tax decisions should be coordinated with financial planning. Verify current PSLF and repayment requirements before acting.

### Physicians with legacy goals: account for the tax treatment of inherited retirement assets

If leaving assets to family or other beneficiaries matters to you, include account type in your estate plan. Traditional retirement accounts, Roth accounts, and taxable investments can have different tax consequences for heirs. Beneficiaries may also face distribution deadlines, and the rules can depend on the account, the beneficiary relationship, and current law. Avoid assuming an inherited account can remain invested indefinitely.

Review beneficiary designations regularly, especially after marriage, divorce, a birth, or another major change. Coordinate them with your will and broader estate plan, and make sure your intended beneficiaries know what steps to take. ## How Can Physicians Build a Tax-Efficient Withdrawal Strategy?

A tax-efficient retirement income strategy coordinates withdrawals with your spending needs, account tax treatment, and other income. Rather than drawing from each account in isolation, consider how a withdrawal may affect your total taxable income for the year. This matters for physicians who may also receive pension income, Social Security, practice-sale proceeds, consulting fees, or income from part-time clinical work.

Start by estimating annual expenses and listing the income sources available to cover them. Then review how your income and tax picture may change over time. For example, retiring before Social Security or required minimum distributions begin may create years when your income is lower than usual. Those years could present planning opportunities, but the right choices depend on your accounts, tax situation, health coverage, and goals.

Review your plan at least once a year, and before major decisions such as a large withdrawal or Roth conversion. A coordinated [retirement strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help connect withdrawal decisions with tax planning, investments, and your broader financial goals.

### Match withdrawals to spending needs, account tax treatment, and tax brackets

Begin with a realistic estimate of how much you’ll need each year for essentials, travel, family support, healthcare, and unexpected expenses. Then consider which accounts could cover those costs and how each withdrawal may be taxed. Distributions from traditional 401(k)s and IRAs are generally taxed as ordinary income. Qualified Roth IRA withdrawals are generally tax-free, while selling investments in a taxable brokerage account may create capital gains or losses.

Your total income matters. A large withdrawal from a tax-deferred account could move some income into a higher tax bracket or affect other income-related costs. Estimate your income annually, including work, pension, and investment income, rather than following a fixed withdrawal order. The IRS provides an overview of how [retirement plan and IRA distributions](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc413) are generally taxed.

### Coordinate taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth withdrawals to manage taxable income

Holding taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth assets can give you more choices about where retirement income comes from each year. You might use cash or taxable investments to cover spending in one year, then take a distribution from a traditional retirement account in another. Roth assets may offer flexibility when additional taxable income could affect your tax bracket or other costs.

There isn’t one withdrawal sequence that fits every physician. Selling appreciated investments may create capital gains, while taking more from a traditional IRA may increase ordinary income. Compare the effects of each option against your expected income and expenses before making a large withdrawal. A physician-focused [tax strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help coordinate these choices with your broader financial plan.

### Consider Roth conversions in lower-income years and plan for required minimum distributions

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional retirement account to a Roth account. The converted amount is generally included in your taxable income for that year. If your income temporarily falls, such as after retiring but before Social Security or required minimum distributions begin, a partial conversion may be worth evaluating. Consider the amount carefully, since a large conversion could increase your tax bill or affect income-related costs.

Traditional retirement accounts are generally subject to required minimum distributions (RMDs) once you reach the applicable age, although rules vary by account and circumstances. RMDs can increase taxable income even if you don’t need the money for spending. Check the IRS guidance on RMDs, and plan for them well before distributions begin.

### Consider when to claim Social Security and how taxes may affect benefits

When you claim Social Security affects the size of your monthly benefit. Claiming earlier generally results in a smaller benefit than waiting until full retirement age. Delaying beyond full retirement age can increase benefits up to age 70. Compare the options based on your health, spouse’s benefits, savings, expected retirement length, and income needs. If you keep working, your earnings and claiming age may also affect your benefits.

Social Security benefits may be taxable depending on your combined income, which includes other income sources and a portion of your benefits. That makes the claiming decision part of your overall income plan. Review estimates with the Social Security Administration’s retirement planner, then consider how each claiming age fits with withdrawals from your other accounts.

### Factor in Medicare premiums, healthcare costs, part-time work, and other income

Include healthcare costs in your retirement budget, including premiums, prescriptions, dental and vision care, and services Medicare may not cover. Medicare premiums can vary based on income. Some beneficiaries pay an income-related monthly adjustment amount, or IRMAA, based on tax information from an earlier year. A large withdrawal or Roth conversion may affect that calculation, so consider the timing with your tax professional.

Add all expected income to your estimates. Pensions, consulting, practice-sale proceeds, rental income, and part-time clinical work can affect both your spending plan and taxable income. If you retire before Medicare eligibility, plan for health insurance during the gap. Review [Medicare’s information on costs](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/medicare-costs) and account for healthcare expenses when setting your withdrawal strategy.

### Stay flexible as markets, expenses, and retirement dates change

Your income needs and financial picture may shift in retirement. Market declines, unexpected healthcare costs, family needs, or a change in your retirement date can affect how much you need to withdraw. Review your spending plan and account balances at least once a year, and after major events such as selling a practice, receiving an inheritance, or starting part-time work.

Flexibility doesn’t mean reacting to every market headline. Set a process for deciding when to adjust withdrawals, which accounts to draw from, and how much cash to keep available. If investments fall, you might reduce discretionary spending or use another income source, depending on your circumstances. Regular reviews can keep your [retirement plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) aligned with your goals, expenses, and tax situation.

## Which Retirement Planning Mistakes Should Physicians Avoid?

A high income and steady retirement contributions can create a strong foundation, but they do not guarantee that your savings will support the retirement you want. Physicians may reach peak earnings later than other professionals, carry student debt into their careers, or balance W-2 income with 1099 work or practice revenue. Family responsibilities, a practice transition, and plans to retire early can further shape how much you need and when you can access it.

Start by looking beyond your account balances. Estimate future spending, identify the income sources you may rely on, and consider how taxes and healthcare costs could affect your cash flow. Then review your plan as your career, family, and goals change. Coordinating your [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) with tax planning and investment decisions can help connect today’s savings choices with the income you may need later.

### Assuming high income or maximum contributions guarantee retirement readiness

A large paycheck and maximum retirement contributions are valuable, but they do not show whether your savings will cover future expenses. Physicians may have high fixed costs, support family members, or expect to maintain a lifestyle that requires substantial income after they stop working. A later start to peak earnings can also mean fewer years for savings to grow.

Estimate retirement spending for housing, travel, insurance, taxes, and family support. Compare that estimate with projected income from investments, Social Security, pensions, and other sources. Revisit the numbers when your income, retirement date, or goals change, rather than relying on a generic savings target. [Retirement planning guidance](https://www.farther.com/foundations/6-retirement-planning-mistakes-you-should-avoid) also notes that underestimating expenses can put long-term plans at risk.

### Relying on one account or overlooking taxable and tax-free income sources

Saving in only one type of account can limit your options in retirement. Traditional workplace plans generally provide tax-deferred growth, while qualified Roth withdrawals can be tax-free. A taxable brokerage account may offer access to investments without the same withdrawal rules that apply to many retirement accounts. Each option has different tax treatment, so the mix of accounts matters alongside the total balance.

Consider how each source could support your spending over time. A combination of taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth assets may give you more flexibility to manage taxable income from year to year. Your income plan might also include Social Security, a pension, rental income, or proceeds from selling a practice. [Northwestern Mutual’s overview of retirement income sources](https://www.northwesternmutual.com/life-and-money/5-common-financial-mistakes-to-avoid-in-retirement) explains why it can help to plan for more than one source.

### Underestimating healthcare costs or claiming Social Security without comparing timing options

Healthcare costs can remain significant in retirement, even with Medicare. If you retire before Medicare eligibility, you may need to pay for individual coverage or another temporary option. Later costs can include premiums, deductibles, prescriptions, and long-term care. Add these expenses to your projections instead of assuming they will fit within general living costs.

Review Social Security timing as part of your broader income plan. The age you claim affects your monthly benefit, and the right choice depends on factors such as health, other income, and whether a spouse’s benefits are involved. Compare potential claiming dates and their effect on your household income before deciding. The Social Security Administration’s retirement benefits information can help you review eligibility and estimate benefits.

### Missing required minimum distributions or overlooking their effect on taxable income

Required minimum distributions, or RMDs, affect how much you must withdraw from certain tax-deferred accounts and when. Under current rules, the starting age depends on your birth year and account type. Missing a required distribution can result in a tax penalty, so identify which accounts are subject to RMDs and note when withdrawals must begin.

RMDs may also increase taxable income, even if you do not need the money for spending. That income can affect your tax bracket, Medicare premiums, or the taxation of Social Security benefits. Estimate future RMDs as part of your withdrawal plan, and consider how charitable giving or Roth conversions may fit into your overall tax strategy. The IRS guide to required minimum distributions outlines the rules and exceptions.

### Overlooking plan fees, employee eligibility, account rules, and contribution limits

A plan’s contribution limit is only one part of the decision. Review investment and administrative fees, employer matching, vesting schedules, eligible employees, and rules for loans or withdrawals. For practice owners, employee eligibility and the cost of offering benefits can influence which plan design is suitable. A plan that works for an employed physician may not fit a practice with staff or multiple owners.

If you have more than one employer or combine W-2 and 1099 income, check how your plans work together. Employee salary deferrals may be subject to an overall annual limit across plans, while employer contributions follow separate rules. Confirm current limits and plan documents each year, especially after a job or practice change. [Practice and business planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning) can help you consider retirement benefits alongside staffing and ownership decisions.

### Making Roth conversions without checking tax timing and the pro-rata rule

A Roth conversion moves money from a tax-deferred account into a Roth account and generally creates taxable income in the year of the conversion. Converting during a lower-income year may be worth considering, but the tax cost depends on your full financial picture. A conversion could push income into a higher tax bracket or affect other income-based costs, so estimate the impact before moving funds.

Physicians using a Backdoor Roth IRA strategy should also review their traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances. Under the pro-rata rule, the taxable share of a conversion is calculated using pre-tax and after-tax funds across these IRA accounts, not just the account involved in the conversion. Check whether an eligible employer plan accepts a rollover of pre-tax IRA assets, and coordinate the transaction with tax reporting. This physician-focused Backdoor Roth guide explains how the rule may affect a conversion.

### Delaying withdrawal planning or failing to revisit the strategy as circumstances change

Accumulating retirement assets is only part of the work. Before leaving practice, consider how you will turn those assets into income, which accounts you may draw from first, and how you would respond to market declines or higher expenses. Without a withdrawal plan, you could create more taxable income than necessary, sell investments at an unfavorable time, or hesitate to use savings you have spent years building.

Review your plan when your circumstances change. A practice sale, part-time work, a change in health, or a different retirement date can alter your income needs and tax picture. Recheck your assumptions and confirm that planned withdrawals still align with your spending and investment approach. Regular reviews help keep your income strategy relevant as your retirement unfolds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Which retirement accounts should physicians consider?**  
Your options depend on your employer, income, and practice structure. You may have access to a 401(k), 403(b), or 457(b), while self-employed physicians and practice owners may be eligible for a Solo 401(k), SEP-IRA, or cash balance plan. Compare contribution rules, fees, employee requirements, and access to funds before choosing.

**Should physicians choose pre-tax or Roth contributions?**  
Pre-tax contributions may lower your taxable income now, while qualified Roth withdrawals are generally tax-free. Consider your current tax bracket, expected income in retirement, and other savings. If your plan allows, using both types of accounts may give you more options later.

**Can a high-income physician contribute to a Roth IRA through the backdoor?**  
A Backdoor Roth IRA generally involves making a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution and converting it to a Roth IRA. Before doing so, review your traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances. The pro-rata rule may make some of the conversion taxable, so check the tax impact with a qualified tax professional.

**What should practice owners consider before adding a retirement plan?**  
Review setup and ongoing costs, staff eligibility, required employer contributions, and the effect on practice cash flow. A cash balance plan may allow additional tax-deferred savings for some practices, but its funding commitments and employee obligations need careful review.

**How should physicians coordinate retirement savings across multiple jobs or income sources?**  
Track contributions across all employer and self-employed plans. Some employee contribution limits are shared across 401(k) and 403(b) plans, while other plans may have separate rules. Check plan documents and current IRS limits, especially when combining W-2 and 1099 income or changing employers.

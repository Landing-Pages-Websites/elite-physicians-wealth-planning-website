## When Does a Doctor Need a CPA?

A CPA can help when your income, tax filings, or financial decisions become too complex for tax software or a once-a-year filing appointment. Physicians often manage several income sources, student loans, professional expenses, investment accounts, and changing compensation arrangements at the same time. A CPA with healthcare experience can help you make informed decisions before they affect your tax return.

You may need a CPA when you move from residency into attending practice, begin earning 1099 income, buy into a medical practice, receive partnership distributions, or earn income in more than one state. A CPA can also help you evaluate the tax effects of major personal and professional decisions, such as relocating, purchasing real estate, changing entity structures, or preparing to retire.

The most valuable CPA relationships are proactive. Instead of focusing only on preparing your return, your CPA should help you understand estimated payments, documentation, business expenses, retirement contributions, and opportunities for tax planning throughout the year. This work can complement a broader [tax planning strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians), especially when your personal and professional finances are closely connected.

### Move From Residency to Attending Income

The move from residency or fellowship to attending income can change nearly every part of your financial plan. Your compensation may rise substantially, but so can your tax liability, retirement savings opportunities, insurance needs, and monthly cash flow. A CPA can help you review paycheck withholding, estimate your tax balance, and avoid an unexpected payment at filing time.

This transition is also a useful time to establish a system for tracking professional expenses, charitable gifts, retirement contributions, and income from any side work. A CPA can explain which expenses may qualify for tax treatment and which records you need to retain. Since a large income change can leave little room for error, choose a professional who understands physician compensation and can work with your financial planner as your priorities develop.

### Manage W-2, 1099, Locums, and Moonlighting Income

Many physicians receive a combination of W-2 wages and 1099 income from moonlighting, consulting, telemedicine, expert witness work, or locums assignments. Each source may involve different withholding, reporting, expense, and estimated tax requirements. A CPA can help you separate business income from personal wages and determine how much to set aside during the year.

Keep contracts, invoices, mileage records, equipment receipts, and other business documentation organized from the start. If you pay contractors or hire administrative support through a side business, payroll and reporting requirements add another layer of responsibility.

The [IRS explanation of independent contractors](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-defined) provides general guidance, but classification can depend on the facts of your work arrangement. A CPA can review those details, help you understand your obligations, and coordinate your tax reporting across different forms of compensation.

### Manage Practice, Partnership, and S Corporation Income

Practice ownership introduces financial decisions that do not apply to most employed physicians. You may need to track business revenue, payroll, operating costs, owner distributions, retirement plan contributions, and equipment purchases. A CPA can maintain accurate books, reconcile accounts, and prepare financial statements that show how the practice is performing.

Partnerships and S corporations also involve specific filing and compensation requirements. Your CPA may help coordinate K-1 reporting, reasonable compensation, payroll taxes, owner draws, and quarterly payments. They can also help you understand how a potential entity change may affect your income and administrative responsibilities.

If you are considering a practice purchase or ownership opportunity, involve your CPA before signing an agreement. Reviewing the purchase terms, projected cash flow, debt obligations, and tax treatment early can help you identify issues while there is still time to address them. [Practice and business planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning) can also help connect ownership decisions with your broader financial plan.

### Handle K-1, Investment, Rental, and Multi-State Income

A K-1 from a partnership, medical practice, private investment, or real estate holding can arrive after the end of the calendar year, which may complicate tax preparation. Rental properties, brokerage accounts, restricted stock, and multiple investment accounts can add more forms, basis information, deductions, and filing considerations.

Multi-state work creates additional questions when you practice across state lines, own property elsewhere, or receive income from a partnership operating in several states. Your CPA can review where income was earned, identify possible filing obligations, and coordinate information from each source. This is particularly important for physicians who combine hospital employment with locums work or maintain a practice interest in another state.

Send your CPA contracts, K-1s, closing statements, brokerage tax forms, and rental records as soon as they become available. Organized documents make it easier to identify missing information, estimate your total liability, and avoid unnecessary delays. A CPA experienced in medical accounting can also coordinate with your investment and [wealth management plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

### Plan for Career, Practice, and Family Changes

A CPA becomes especially valuable when your circumstances change. Marriage, divorce, a new child, relocation, a practice purchase, a sabbatical, disability, or an upcoming retirement can affect withholding, deductions, insurance, entity structure, and long-term savings. Bringing your CPA into the conversation early gives you more choices than waiting until tax forms are due.

Ask your CPA to model major decisions before you commit. Selling a practice, changing states, buying real estate, or moving from employed work to independent contracting may create tax consequences that are difficult to reverse. Your CPA can estimate the likely impact and identify records or elections you may need to address in advance.

These decisions should also connect with your retirement strategy, estate plan, insurance coverage, and investment approach. Coordinating [retirement planning for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) with tax advice can help you evaluate both the immediate tax effects and the long-term impact on your financial independence.

### Recognize Reactive Tax Support

Hiring a CPA does not automatically mean every tax-related matter will be handled without your involvement. Many CPA relationships focus primarily on preparing tax returns using information the client provides. That may be appropriate for a straightforward W-2 situation, but it can leave planning opportunities unaddressed for a physician with multiple income sources or a growing practice.

If your CPA contacts you only after year-end, ask whether they offer quarterly or year-round planning. Clarify who is responsible for estimated payments, entity elections, retirement plan deadlines, payroll reviews, and changes in your financial circumstances. You should also know what information your CPA needs and when you need to provide it.

A proactive relationship includes regular communication before major decisions, not just document collection during tax season. Your CPA can handle tax preparation and accounting, while your wealth advisor can help connect those decisions with investments, retirement, and legacy goals. Coordinating [wealth management for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) with your CPA can give you a clearer view of how each financial decision affects the rest of your plan.

## What Does a CPA for Doctors Do?

A CPA for doctors does more than prepare an annual tax return. They help physicians organize financial information, meet tax and reporting obligations, and make informed decisions about income, expenses, compensation, and practice operations.

The work can include individual and business tax returns, bookkeeping, payroll, cash flow reporting, estimated taxes, entity accounting, compliance, and financial forecasting. The right combination depends on your situation. A resident may need help with W-2 income, moonlighting pay, student loan considerations, and estimated taxes. An established physician may have partnership income, investments, rental property, or multiple employers. A practice owner may also need accounting for payroll, distributions, equipment, partners, and multiple business entities.

Healthcare accounting requires close attention because physicians often have high income, several revenue sources, and little time to review every transaction. A CPA who understands medical practices can organize the details and identify tax or cash flow concerns before they become urgent. Their work can also support a broader [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians), coordinated with your financial planner and other professional advisors.

### Prepare Individual and Practice Tax Returns

A CPA may prepare your personal federal and state tax returns, along with returns for your medical practice, partnership, or S corporation. They gather income documents, review deductions, reconcile tax forms, and check that information is consistent across related returns.

For physicians, this may involve W-2 wages, 1099 income, K-1s, rental income, investment activity, and practice revenue. Practice returns may also require a detailed review of payroll, equipment purchases, owner compensation, and deductible operating costs.

Healthcare accounting leaves little room for missed details. High income, complex tax rules, and patient-driven revenue cycles can create issues that general accounting services may overlook. A medical CPA can connect the details before filing deadlines arrive. Learn more about accounting for doctors.

### Plan Taxes Year-Round

Tax planning should not begin when your tax documents arrive. A CPA can review projected income, deductions, retirement contributions, withholding, and estimated payments throughout the year. This gives you time to act before a financial decision becomes difficult to change.

For example, you may need to adjust estimated payments after taking on moonlighting work, changing employers, selling an investment, or receiving a large practice distribution. A practice owner may need to revisit compensation, retirement contributions, or equipment purchases before year-end.

Year-round planning can also reduce surprises. Your CPA may prepare tax projections, identify important deadlines, and explain how specific decisions could affect your tax liability. This forward-looking work is different from simply reporting what happened after the tax year ends.

### Manage Bookkeeping, Financial Statements, and Cash Flow

Accurate bookkeeping gives you a clearer view of how your practice is performing. A CPA or accounting team may reconcile bank accounts, categorize transactions, track accounts receivable, and maintain records for business expenses and owner activity.

They may also prepare financial statements, including a profit and loss statement, balance sheet, and cash flow report. These reports can show whether revenue is growing, expenses are rising, or collections are slowing without requiring you to sort through raw transactions.

Cash flow reporting matters even when a practice is profitable. Insurer and patient payments may arrive at different times, while payroll, rent, supplies, and loan obligations follow fixed schedules. Reliable records help you see how much cash is available, when it is available, and whether your practice can support upcoming expenses.

### Manage Payroll, Compensation, and Benefits

Medical practices often employ a mix of clinical staff, administrative employees, and other physicians. Each role may involve different compensation arrangements, tax reporting, benefits, and documentation requirements.

A CPA may coordinate payroll records, review owner wages, classify payments, and help ensure payroll information matches accounting records and tax filings. They may also work with your payroll provider to address retirement contributions, bonuses, health benefits, and other forms of compensation.

For practice owners, compensation planning can affect both personal taxes and business finances. The right approach depends on your entity, ownership structure, income, and professional goals. A CPA can explain the accounting and tax implications, while your financial planner and attorney address related investment, legal, and risk decisions.

### Calculate Estimated Taxes and Withholding

Physicians with 1099, locums, moonlighting, partnership, rental, or investment income may need to make estimated tax payments. A CPA can project your annual liability and help determine whether current payments or paycheck withholding are on track.

This review becomes especially important when income changes during the year. A new contract, partner distribution, bonus, practice sale, or large investment gain can create a tax obligation that was not reflected in earlier estimates.

A CPA may also compare payroll withholding with accounting records and filed returns. Keeping those figures aligned can help prevent underpayment issues and make cash planning more predictable. Your CPA should explain payment dates and amounts clearly, so you understand what is due and why.

### Manage Entity, Partnership, and Practice Accounting

A physician may operate through more than one legal or tax entity. Examples include a medical practice, management company, real estate entity, or separate business shared with partners. Each entity may require distinct bookkeeping, tax filings, bank accounts, and financial records.

A CPA can maintain accounting for these entities and track how money moves between them. For partnerships and S corporations, that may include reviewing ownership percentages, distributions, basis, payroll, and K-1 information.

Entity accounting also supports major business decisions. If you are considering adding a partner, buying into a practice, changing ownership, or opening another location, accurate records give your advisors better information. Review these choices with your CPA, attorney, and financial planner before making changes.

### Support Multi-State, K-1, and Investment Taxes

Physicians may earn income in more than one state through locums work, telehealth, medical groups, speaking engagements, or ownership interests. A CPA can review where income was earned, which filings may apply, and whether credits are available for taxes paid to another state.

K-1 income from partnerships and S corporations adds another layer. The amount shown on a K-1 may not match the cash you received, so planning based only on distributions can lead to an unexpected tax bill. Your CPA can review the K-1 alongside your other income and estimated payments.

Investment accounts, rental property, private investments, and equity compensation may also create taxable income. Coordinating these sources helps your CPA prepare a more complete picture of your obligations and identify missing documents before filing. This may be especially important for physicians working across state lines or holding interests in several entities.

### Manage Compliance, Documentation, and Audits

A CPA can help your practice meet filing, reporting, and recordkeeping requirements. Depending on your structure, this may include payroll filings, business registrations, local tax obligations, retirement plan reporting, and documentation for deductible expenses.

Strong documentation supports your tax return if the IRS or a state agency asks questions. Your CPA may establish systems for retaining receipts, mileage records, charitable giving documents, accountable plan reimbursements, and records supporting business use.

If an audit or notice occurs, a CPA can help explain what the agency requested, organize relevant records, and communicate with the agency when appropriate. They cannot guarantee a particular outcome, but careful preparation can make the process more orderly and reduce avoidable delays. Clear systems also help your practice respond consistently when records are requested.

### Forecast Finances and Advise on Practice Operations

Once your records are accurate, a CPA can use them to create financial forecasts. A forecast may estimate revenue, expenses, payroll, cash reserves, debt payments, and tax obligations under different scenarios.

This information can support operational decisions, such as hiring staff, changing compensation, purchasing equipment, refinancing debt, expanding a location, or evaluating a new payer contract. It can also show whether your practice has enough liquidity to support a planned investment without placing unnecessary pressure on cash reserves.

A CPA may provide financial reports and recommendations, but they are not a substitute for clinical, legal, or investment advice. The strongest approach brings these professionals together. For practice owners, this coordinated process can fit within a broader [practice and business planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning), connecting day-to-day decisions with long-term goals.

Physicians rarely have a simple financial picture. You may receive income from a hospital position, private practice, moonlighting, consulting, or locum tenens work while also managing student loans, retirement accounts, real estate, and investments. Practice owners face additional decisions involving payroll, employee benefits, business expenses, partner compensation, cash reserves, and entity-level tax planning.

A CPA who understands healthcare can review these moving parts together instead of treating your tax return as a once-a-year filing. That perspective matters because a decision made in your practice can affect your personal taxes, retirement savings, cash flow, and long-term wealth. [Healthcare accounting guidance](https://leichtercpa.com/blog/accountant-for-doctors-do) highlights why medical professionals need careful attention to high income, complex tax rules, and patient-driven revenue cycles.

### Address Complex Income, Student Debt, and Delayed Wealth Building

Many physicians spend their twenties and early thirties in training, earning modest wages while accumulating substantial student debt. Once attending income begins, the financial transition can be sudden. A larger paycheck may arrive before you have a clear system for estimated taxes, loan repayment, retirement contributions, insurance, and lifestyle spending.

A physician-focused CPA can organize income from multiple sources and identify tax considerations that apply to your situation. This support can make the transition from residency to practice more manageable. It may also help you direct attending income toward debt repayment and long-term savings while preserving enough liquidity for current expenses. The objective is not simply to lower this year’s tax bill, but to make your financial decisions work together.

### Connect Practice Finances With Personal Wealth

For a practice owner, business and personal finances are closely connected. Owner compensation, distributions, payroll, business expenses, retirement contributions, and cash reserves can all affect your personal tax position. When these areas are managed separately, inconsistencies and planning opportunities can go unnoticed.

A CPA can help reconcile payroll, accounting records, and tax filings so your financial information remains consistent across the practice and your personal return. This may reduce the risk of avoidable notices, penalties, or reporting problems. It can also give you a clearer view of what the practice produces and how much it can reasonably support in personal spending, investing, and [wealth management for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

### Understand Healthcare Compensation and Practice Models

Physicians may receive a combination of salary, productivity bonuses, partnership income, distributions, call pay, consulting fees, and benefits. The appropriate tax and accounting treatment can vary based on your employment agreement, practice structure, and state. A CPA with healthcare experience is more likely to recognize how these arrangements fit together.

Practice owners may also employ clinical staff, administrative employees, advanced practice providers, and other physicians. Each group can involve different payroll, benefits, classification, and reporting responsibilities. A CPA familiar with medical practices can review compensation and operating systems with those details in mind. This experience becomes especially useful when you are evaluating a buy-in, adding a provider, restructuring ownership, or preparing for a future sale.

### Manage Variable Cash Flow and Limited Time

Medical income is not always as predictable as a standard salary. Collections can vary based on payer mix, season, procedure volume, staffing, and billing performance. Practice owners may also face equipment purchases, unexpected repairs, hiring costs, or delayed reimbursements. Without regular financial reporting, it can be difficult to tell whether a cash shortage is temporary or signals a larger issue.

Your time is limited, too. A CPA can provide recurring bookkeeping, cash flow reporting, payroll coordination, and tax estimates, helping you make decisions with current information. Year-round medical accounting can be more useful than searching for answers during tax season, particularly when your practice is growing or facing an operational change.

### Plan Beyond Tax Season

Tax preparation tells you what happened. Tax planning helps you decide what to do next. Physicians may benefit from advice before accepting a new contract, receiving a large bonus, purchasing a practice, adding a partner, selling property, or changing business entities.

A CPA can model the potential tax impact of these decisions and identify deadlines, documentation requirements, or estimated payment changes. A CPA with relevant experience can help establish reliable processes and review records before problems grow. ## Which Tax Strategies Might a CPA Recommend?

A CPA who works with physicians will usually look beyond the tax return. The right strategy depends on your income sources, practice structure, state residency, benefits, investment accounts, family situation, and long-term goals. A physician with W-2 income may need a different approach than a practice owner receiving K-1 income, 1099 compensation, or rental income.

Tax planning works best when it begins before the end of the tax year. Your CPA may review projected income, retirement contributions, business expenses, estimated payments, and investment activity throughout the year. This gives you time to make informed decisions instead of reacting after the numbers are final.

Tax strategies should also fit into your broader financial plan. For example, a retirement contribution can affect your cash flow today, retirement income later, and the amount you can invest in a taxable account. Coordinating these decisions with [wealth management for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) can help keep your tax strategy connected to your larger financial goals.

The strategies below are common areas a CPA may evaluate. Not every option is appropriate for every physician. Recommendations should account for current federal and state rules, plan documents, business records, and your personal circumstances.

### Use Retirement Plans, 401(k)s, and Cash Balance Plans

Retirement plans can reduce taxable income while helping you build long-term savings. Depending on your employer or practice, a CPA may review your 401(k), 403(b), profit-sharing plan, defined benefit plan, or cash balance plan.

A cash balance plan may allow a practice owner to make larger tax-deferred contributions than a standard 401(k), particularly when the owner is older and has substantial, consistent income. These plans also come with funding requirements, administrative costs, and employee coverage rules. They should be designed with an actuary and benefits professionals.

A CPA may compare pre-tax and Roth contributions, coordinate contributions across multiple employers, and review whether your practice has the right plan design. The goal is not simply to contribute as much as possible. It is to balance current tax savings, employee benefits, future income needs, and practice cash flow. Physicians can connect these decisions to a broader [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians).

### Choose an Entity Structure and Set Owner Compensation

The structure of your medical practice affects taxes, payroll, legal administration, and how money moves between the business and its owners. A CPA may compare a sole proprietorship, partnership, professional corporation, or S corporation based on your state’s rules and your practice’s circumstances.

For an S corporation, owner compensation requires particular attention. The IRS generally expects shareholder-employees to receive reasonable compensation for the services they provide before taking profits as distributions. Paying too little in wages can create compliance concerns, while paying too much may reduce the potential tax benefit.

A CPA may review revenue, specialty, ownership arrangements, employee wages, benefits, and the work you perform in the practice. The right structure is not determined by income alone. It should also reflect your plans for hiring, expansion, admitting partners, selling the practice, or purchasing real estate. Practice owners may benefit from coordinating this work with [practice and business planning](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning).

### Claim Practice Deductions and Use Accountable Plans

Medical practices may have deductible expenses for staff wages, rent, malpractice insurance, software, supplies, continuing education, professional dues, marketing, and business travel. A CPA can help determine whether an expense is ordinary, necessary, properly documented, and connected to the practice.

An accountable plan may allow a practice to reimburse employees, including eligible owners, for certain business expenses without treating those reimbursements as taxable wages. Examples may include mileage, business travel, professional education, and supplies purchased personally for practice use. The plan should require documentation and the return of any excess reimbursement.

Keeping personal and business expenses separate makes this process easier. Use dedicated accounts and cards, retain receipts, and record the business purpose of each expense. Your CPA can compare payroll, bookkeeping records, and tax filings so the numbers remain consistent across the practice.

### Manage Equipment, Depreciation, and Capital Improvements

Medical equipment, technology, furniture, leasehold improvements, and office renovations may receive different tax treatment. A CPA can help determine whether an item is currently deductible, depreciated over time, or subject to special rules.

The timing of a purchase matters. Buying equipment near year-end does not automatically produce a full deduction, and the business may need to meet placed-in-service requirements. A CPA may compare available depreciation methods while considering taxable income, future purchases, financing, and the practice’s long-term needs.

Capital improvements also deserve careful review. A renovation may need to be separated into building improvements, equipment, furnishings, and other categories. Keep invoices, contracts, financing records, and installation dates to support the treatment selected on the return. Your CPA should coordinate depreciation decisions with your practice’s cash flow and [tax strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians).

### Plan Estimated Taxes on W-2 and 1099 Income

Physicians with 1099 income may need to make quarterly estimated tax payments for federal and state taxes. This can apply to locums work, moonlighting, consulting, medical directorships, expert witness services, and other independent contractor arrangements.

A CPA may project total income, deductions, self-employment tax, withholding, and prior-year tax obligations. The calculation should include both W-2 and 1099 income rather than treating each source separately. If your compensation changes during the year, estimated payments may need to be adjusted.

Physicians can also review W-2 withholding with their employer. Increasing withholding may be simpler than making separate quarterly payments, although the right approach depends on your income pattern and cash flow. The purpose is to avoid a large surprise bill and reduce the risk of underpayment penalties without sending more money than necessary before it is due.

### Manage State Taxes, K-1 Income, and Multiple Locations

State tax planning becomes more complex when you live in one state, work in another, own an interest in a partnership, or practice across multiple locations. A CPA may review residency, workdays, income sourcing, entity filings, and state-specific deductions or credits.

K-1 income can make planning less predictable because the final amount may not be available until after the partnership or S corporation completes its books. Your CPA may use prior-year information or interim figures to estimate the current liability, then update the calculation when better data becomes available.

Physicians working across state lines should keep clear records of where services were performed and when. Practice owners may also need to consider composite filings, pass-through entity taxes, and state estimated payments. A CPA with multi-state experience can help identify filing obligations before they become a last-minute problem.

### Use HSAs, Charitable Giving, and Family Tax Planning

If you are eligible for a health savings account, contributions may offer tax advantages while helping cover current and future medical expenses. A CPA can review your health plan eligibility, contribution limits, employer contributions, and whether HSA funding fits your broader savings strategy.

Charitable giving may also receive more thoughtful treatment than a simple cash donation. Depending on your goals, your CPA may discuss appreciated securities, donor-advised funds, qualified charitable distributions after reaching the applicable age, or grouping gifts into selected tax years. Each option has its own rules and should be coordinated with your investment plan.

Family tax planning may include dependent care benefits, education savings, gifting, family employment in a legitimate business, and the tax treatment of a spouse’s income. Any arrangement involving family members should reflect real services, reasonable compensation, and complete records. A financial planner can help connect these choices with your [legacy planning goals](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/legacy-estate-planning).

### Manage Rental Real Estate and Investment Income

Rental properties can create deductions for interest, repairs, insurance, taxes, management fees, and depreciation. A CPA may also review passive activity rules, loss limitations, material participation, and the distinction between repairs and capital improvements.

The tax treatment can become more complicated when a physician owns several properties, participates in short-term rentals, or holds real estate through partnerships or limited liability companies. K-1 forms, refinancing, property sales, and suspended losses may all affect the return.

Investment income requires a separate review. Your CPA may account for dividends, interest, capital gains, distributions, and investment expenses while considering how those items interact with your other income. Look for an advisor who understands both physician compensation and real estate taxation, particularly if rental property is part of your wealth-building plan.

### Coordinate Tax-Loss Harvesting With Your Investment Plan

Tax-loss harvesting involves selling an investment that has declined in value and using the realized loss to offset eligible gains, subject to applicable rules and limitations. A CPA can help estimate whether realizing a loss makes sense based on your current gains, income, and expected transactions.

The decision should not be based on taxes alone. Selling may change your asset allocation, create a replacement investment issue, or interfere with your investment strategy. The wash-sale rules also require attention when you or a related account purchases a substantially identical security within the relevant period.

Your investment advisor and CPA should communicate before trades are made. Together, they can consider portfolio risk, investment costs, charitable giving, and future liquidity needs. Tax-loss harvesting is most useful when it supports the investment plan rather than driving it.

### Maintain Documentation and Track Tax-Law Changes

Good documentation supports nearly every tax strategy. Keep receipts, mileage logs, reimbursement records, charitable acknowledgments, property statements, entity documents, payroll reports, and records showing the business purpose of expenses. Digital records can work well when they are organized, readable, and backed up.

Your CPA may also review changes to federal and state tax rules, contribution limits, depreciation provisions, reporting requirements, and filing deadlines. This matters for physicians because compensation, practice ownership, retirement plans, and investment income can all be affected by rule changes.

Ask your CPA how recommendations will be documented and implemented. A written suggestion is useful only when someone confirms the next steps, deadlines, required records, and responsible parties. When tax planning is coordinated with your financial team, it can support [tax-aware wealth management](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) while keeping your practice and personal priorities in view.

CPA fees may cover much more than annual tax preparation. Depending on the engagement, you may pay for bookkeeping, payroll, estimated tax calculations, entity setup, year-round tax planning, and practice consulting. ## How Do You Choose the Right CPA for Your Medical Practice?

Choosing a CPA for your medical practice requires more than comparing tax preparation fees. Your accountant may manage physician income, payroll, entity returns, estimated taxes, practice expenses, retirement contributions, and investment or rental activity. You should also decide whether you need tax preparation, year-round tax planning, bookkeeping, practice consulting, or coordination with your financial planner and attorney. An employed physician may need a different level of support than a practice owner managing payroll, multiple entities, and a partner buy-in.

Before signing an engagement letter, interview at least two or three firms. Ask for specific examples of how they support physicians with similar income, practice structures, and financial goals. A CPA who understands your work can ask more relevant questions and identify planning opportunities earlier.

### Look for Physician and Healthcare Practice Experience

Look for a CPA who regularly works with physicians, dentists, CRNAs, NPs, PAs, surgeons, and medical practices. Healthcare experience matters because compensation models, ownership arrangements, billing cycles, and practice expenses can differ from those of a typical small business.

Ask how many medical professionals the firm serves and whether it works with your type of practice. An accountant who understands private practices may be better prepared to address partner buy-ins, collections, physician compensation, associate agreements, and payroll.

A generalist may still be a good fit, but do not assume experience with other small businesses translates to medical accounting. ### Confirm Expertise With W-2, 1099, K-1, and Practice Taxes

Physicians often receive income from several sources. Your CPA should be comfortable handling W-2 wages, 1099 compensation, locums work, moonlighting, partnership income, S corporation distributions, and K-1 reporting. Ask how the firm reconciles these sources and calculates estimated payments.

The CPA should understand the difference between preparing your return and planning for the tax impact of each income stream. A practice owner, for example, may need guidance about reasonable compensation, payroll taxes, retirement plan contributions, and the timing of distributions.

Request a clear explanation of which returns the engagement covers. Your personal return may not include the practice’s partnership, S corporation, payroll, or state filings. ### Assess Real Estate and Multi-State Tax Expertise

Real estate can create planning opportunities, but it also introduces depreciation, passive activity rules, basis tracking, and recordkeeping requirements. If you own rental property or invest in real estate, ask whether the CPA has worked with physician clients who hold property personally, through an LLC, or through a separate partnership.

Multi-state income adds another layer of complexity. You may owe tax in more than one state if you practice across state lines, perform locums work, own property elsewhere, or receive income from a pass-through entity operating in multiple jurisdictions.

Ask how the CPA determines filing obligations, handles state tax credits, and tracks income earned in each location. The firm should also explain which records you need to maintain throughout the year. Choose a professional who can coordinate these details instead of treating each return as an isolated task.

### Verify Credentials, Licensing, and Engagement Terms

Confirm that the CPA holds an active license in the relevant state. You can check licensing information through your state’s accountancy board and ask about the firm’s experience with clients who have similar financial and practice structures.

Read the engagement letter before hiring anyone. It should identify the services included, deadlines, responsible contacts, communication expectations, and fee structure. Check whether the quoted fee covers individual returns, entity returns, tax projections, estimated payments, bookkeeping, payroll, amended returns, and audit support.

### Match Services to Your Personal, Practice, and Multi-Entity Needs

Your CPA’s services should reflect the full structure of your financial life. An employed physician with one W-2 may need annual filing and periodic planning. A practice owner may need bookkeeping, payroll, entity accounting, partner reporting, cash flow analysis, and personal tax preparation.

Create a list of the entities and accounts connected to your finances. Include your medical practice, real estate LLCs, investment partnerships, charitable entities, and retirement plans. Then ask which responsibilities the CPA can handle directly and which will require another professional.

Price matters, but the lowest fee may not represent the lowest total cost. Compare what each CPA includes and how much support you receive between tax filings. A higher fee may be reasonable if it covers projections, entity returns, planning meetings, bookkeeping oversight, and prompt answers.

Be cautious if a CPA cannot explain their experience with physician income, practice entities, or your specific tax situation. Vague answers may indicate that the firm relies on a high-volume process instead of tailored advice.

Other warning signs include unclear fees, no written engagement letter, limited availability outside tax season, slow communication, weak document security, and pressure to approve returns without reviewing them. ## What Misconceptions Do Doctors Have About Hiring a CPA?

### CPAs Only Work During Tax Season

Tax filing is one visible part of a CPA’s work, but it should not be the entire relationship. Many important decisions need attention months before a return is due. Retirement contributions, estimated payments, owner compensation, equipment purchases, charitable gifts, and entity elections may all require advance planning.

Year-round accounting can also help you monitor cash flow, maintain organized records, and prepare for changes such as opening a practice or becoming a partner. As Leichter CPA explains, medical accounting is a year-round function, not a task limited to tax season.

Ask prospective CPAs how often they meet with clients, what information they review throughout the year, and whether tax planning is included in their engagement. A professional who only contacts you when documents are due may not provide the proactive support your finances require.

### Any Accountant Can Manage Medical Practice Finances

A general accountant may be capable of preparing a basic return, but medical practices often involve details that require industry knowledge. Revenue may come from insurance payments, patient collections, procedures, ancillary services, or several related entities. Compensation may also depend on productivity formulas, call coverage, partnership distributions, or ownership percentages.

Healthcare accounting leaves little room for overlooked details. Specialized accounting guidance for doctors notes that high income, complex tax rules, and patient-driven revenue cycles can create challenges that generic services may miss.

Look for experience with physician compensation, practice accounting, K-1 income, payroll, multi-state filings, and common medical business structures. A CPA who understands these areas can ask more useful questions and identify potential issues earlier than someone who mainly handles straightforward individual returns.

### Hiring a CPA Costs Too Much

Cost matters, but the lowest fee is not always the least expensive option over time. A CPA may help you avoid missed deductions, inaccurate estimated payments, payroll mistakes, or poorly timed business decisions. Professional support may also save time, which matters when you are balancing patient care, call schedules, administrative work, and family responsibilities.

Tax software can work well for simple returns, particularly when your income comes from one employer and your finances are fairly straightforward. It cannot, however, understand your goals or assess the full impact of a practice, investment, compensation, or family decision.

Software may calculate information you enter, but it will not usually ask whether your entity structure still fits, whether your retirement plan is appropriate, or whether a planned purchase belongs in the current tax year. A CPA, financial planner, and attorney may all contribute to your financial decisions, but they do not perform the same job. Your CPA generally focuses on tax compliance, accounting, and tax planning. A financial planner may coordinate investments, insurance, cash flow, retirement income, and broader financial priorities. An attorney handles legal matters such as contracts, business structures, asset protection, and estate documents.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When should a physician consider hiring a CPA?**\
Consider hiring a CPA when you have multiple income sources, partnership or practice income, rental property, investments, or tax obligations in more than one state. A CPA can also provide valuable guidance during career changes, practice purchases, relocations, and retirement planning.

**What is the difference between a CPA and a financial planner?**\
A CPA generally handles tax preparation, accounting, estimated payments, payroll, and business records. A financial planner focuses on investments, retirement, cash flow, insurance, and long-term goals. Physicians may benefit from using both professionals and allowing them to share relevant information.

**How much does a CPA for a doctor usually cost?**\
Fees depend on the complexity of your finances and the services included. A straightforward W-2 return usually costs less than a package covering practice returns, bookkeeping, payroll, K-1s, rental properties, and year-round planning. Ask for a written proposal that explains included services and additional charges.

**What should a doctor ask before hiring a CPA?**\
Ask about experience with physicians, your compensation structure, practice entities, multi-state income, K-1s, and real estate. Confirm who prepares and reviews your returns, whether planning is available throughout the year, how communication works, and what the engagement fee covers.

**How can Elite Physician Wealth Planning work with my CPA?**\
Elite Physician Wealth Planning can help connect your CPA’s tax guidance with investment management, retirement decisions, practice planning, insurance, and legacy goals. With your permission, the professionals can coordinate around major decisions so your personal and professional finances follow one clear strategy.

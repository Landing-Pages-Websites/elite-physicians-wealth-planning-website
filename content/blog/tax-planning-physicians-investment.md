A high income can make small tax details matter. For physicians, wages, practice profits, investment gains, and rental income may all affect the same return, even though they are not necessarily taxed the same way. A planned sale of appreciated stock, for example, may have a different effect than interest from a savings account or income from a medical practice. Tax planning for physicians with investment income means reviewing those sources together and understanding how timing, account type, and state rules may affect your tax picture. We’ll explain the key considerations and share steps to help you prepare for conversations with your tax and financial professionals.

## Key Takeaways

* **Track each income source separately**: Wages, practice earnings, investments, and rental income follow different tax rules, so keep records organized by category.
* **Coordinate tax decisions across your financial plan**: Consider investment sales, retirement contributions, practice structure, charitable giving, and state filing obligations together.
* **Review your tax strategy during the year**: Check in with tax and financial professionals before major transactions or income changes, and adjust estimated payments when needed.

## Why Is Tax Planning More Complex for Physicians With Investment Income?

A physician’s tax picture may include wages, practice earnings, investment returns, and rental income, each with different tax rules. These sources can also affect one another: a large capital gain in a year with high clinical or practice income may change your total tax liability and estimated payments. Coordinating them takes more than reviewing a brokerage statement at tax time. A physician-focused [tax planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can help connect investment decisions with compensation, practice finances, and other parts of your financial plan.

### Coordinate wages, practice income, and portfolio income

Your salary, practice income, and portfolio income may all contribute to your taxable income, but they may not receive the same tax treatment. Wages and many types of business income are generally taxed at ordinary income rates. Interest, dividends, and gains from selling investments can be taxed differently, depending on the type of income and, for gains, how long you held the asset.

This makes timing important. A substantial bonus, a profitable year at your practice, or a planned investment sale can affect your total income for the year. Before selling assets or making a major financial move, review how it fits with your expected earnings and other income. The Tax Adviser’s [overview of tax planning for physicians](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2024/dec/tax-planning-for-physicians) explains why coordinating income, benefits, and tax decisions matters throughout a medical career.

### Account for high tax brackets, the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax, and state taxes

High earnings can make the tax treatment of investment income especially important. Ordinary income is subject to federal tax brackets, while qualified dividends and long-term capital gains may qualify for separate rates. Some taxpayers also owe the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT). It generally applies to the lesser of net investment income or the amount by which modified adjusted gross income exceeds the applicable threshold. The threshold depends on filing status and is not indexed for inflation. The [IRS NIIT guidance](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/net-investment-income-tax) outlines the rules and exceptions.

State taxes add another consideration. States differ in how they tax investment income, and a taxable sale may affect your federal and state returns differently. Review both before realizing a significant gain, especially if you expect income to vary from year to year.

### Review multistate income from locum tenens work, practice locations, and investment properties

Working or owning property in more than one state can create additional filing responsibilities. Locum tenens assignments, a practice with locations across state lines, or a move during the year may require you to determine which state can tax particular income. Rental property outside your home state may also trigger a filing requirement. The rules vary by state, so don’t assume that filing a federal return or a resident-state return covers every obligation.

Keep clear records of where and when you worked, how you were paid, and which expenses relate to each location. For rental property, track income and expenses by property. These details help a tax professional allocate income correctly and assess whether a credit may reduce double taxation. [Guidance on choosing a medical practice CPA](https://taxstra.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-cpa-for-your-medical-practice) highlights the value of coordinating tax work when assignments or business activity cross state lines.

### Distinguish portfolio, business, and passive rental income

Income connected to investments can fall into different tax categories. Interest, dividends, and gains from securities are generally reported as portfolio income. Earnings from a medical practice are business income, with tax treatment shaped by the practice’s structure and other circumstances. Rental income is typically reported separately, and special rules may limit whether rental losses can offset other income. Your level of participation and the type of activity can affect how those rules apply.

These distinctions matter when calculating taxable income, evaluating deductions, and determining whether income may be subject to the NIIT. A partnership distribution, rent payment, and sale of a long-held investment are not equivalent tax events. Keep statements and supporting records organized by income source, and ask your tax professional how each activity should be reported before making decisions based on an estimate.

### Separate investment taxes from medical practice taxes

Your practice and your investments may ultimately appear on the same individual return, but they involve different tax questions. Practice planning can include payroll, deductible business expenses, retirement plan contributions, and entity filings. Investment planning may focus on interest, dividends, capital gains, and the tax treatment of different account types. Separating these categories makes it easier to understand what is driving your tax bill.

At the same time, plan them together. A change in compensation, a practice sale, or a large investment gain can affect your overall income and estimated tax payments. Coordinating with professionals who understand both your practice and personal finances can help identify how one decision may affect the other. A comprehensive [wealth management approach for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) considers investments alongside the broader financial picture.

## How Do Taxes Apply to Physicians’ Investment Income?

Investment income can be taxed differently from salary or practice earnings. The tax treatment depends on the type of income, how long you held an asset, and where you file. For physicians with several income sources, knowing these differences can help you plan sales, distributions, and estimated payments before tax season.

### Compare taxes on interest, qualified dividends, and short- and long-term gains

Interest from savings accounts, certificates of deposit, and many bonds is generally taxed as ordinary income. Qualified dividends and long-term capital gains may receive lower federal tax rates, depending on your taxable income and filing status. In most cases, an investment must be held for more than one year to qualify for long-term capital gains treatment. Gains from assets held for one year or less are generally taxed at ordinary income rates. The [IRS explains how capital gains and losses are taxed](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409), and [Ameriprise outlines tax considerations for investors](https://www.ameriprise.com/financial-goals-priorities/taxes/common-tax-mistakes-for-investors-to-avoid).

For physicians with significant wages or practice income, the difference between ordinary and preferential tax rates may be meaningful. The 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax may also apply when modified adjusted gross income exceeds the applicable threshold. Consider the tax effect of a planned sale or dividend income alongside your full-year income, rather than assessing each investment in isolation.

### Track cost basis and taxable sales; understand why unrealized gains generally aren’t taxed

Cost basis is generally what you paid for an investment, adjusted for certain events such as reinvested dividends, stock splits, or return-of-capital distributions. When you sell, your taxable gain or loss is typically calculated by comparing the sale proceeds with your adjusted basis. Keep trade confirmations and account statements, and verify that your brokerage has accurate basis information, especially after transferring assets or inheriting investments.

An increase in an investment’s value generally does not create a tax bill until you sell or otherwise realize the gain. The Congressional Research Service explains the role of realization in capital gains taxation. However, you may owe tax on fund distributions even when you have not sold your shares. Before selling appreciated investments, review the timing and likely tax impact with your tax professional.

### Review distributions from mutual funds, ETFs, REITs, and partnerships

Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds may distribute dividends or capital gains to shareholders. These distributions can be taxable in a regular brokerage account even if you reinvest them instead of taking them as cash. Review year-end tax forms and fund notices to identify the type of income and keep your basis records current. The [IRS provides guidance on investment income](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550).

REIT distributions may include ordinary dividends, qualified dividends, capital gains, or return of capital, each with different tax treatment. Partnerships generally report your share of income, deductions, and other items on Schedule K-1. You may owe tax on partnership income even if you did not receive a cash distribution. If you invest in real estate or private funds, account for K-1 delivery timelines and coordinate the investment’s tax treatment with your broader portfolio plan.

### Assess real estate taxes, depreciation, and potential depreciation recapture

Rental property may generate taxable income, while eligible expenses and depreciation can reduce that income. Depreciation generally allows an owner to deduct part of a property’s cost over time, subject to tax rules and limits. Keep records of purchase costs, improvements, operating expenses, and depreciation claimed or allowable. How you own and use the property can affect which deductions apply.

When you sell, depreciation may affect both the amount and character of your taxable gain. For some real property, depreciation-related gain may be treated as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain and taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25%; other property or circumstances may lead to different treatment. Review the details with a tax professional before completing a sale. Physicians with rental properties can include these decisions in a coordinated [tax planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians).

### Consider municipal bond interest and state-tax differences

Interest from many municipal bonds is generally exempt from federal income tax. It may also be exempt from state income tax when the bonds are issued in your state, though rules vary. Interest from certain private-activity municipal bonds may affect the alternative minimum tax, so tax-exempt interest does not always mean there is no tax consequence.

Compare a municipal bond’s after-tax yield with that of taxable alternatives rather than focusing only on its stated interest rate. Your income, filing status, state of residence, and the bond’s source can all affect the comparison. This is worth reviewing if you earn income in multiple states through locum tenens work, practice locations, or investment properties. Ask your tax professional to consider state filing obligations alongside your investment choices.

### Plan for distributions, estimated payments, and accurate records

Investment income can increase your tax bill without changing paycheck withholding. This may happen when you receive substantial dividends, sell appreciated assets, earn rental income, or receive partnership income. Depending on your full-year tax picture, you may need to adjust withholding or make estimated payments to avoid an underpayment penalty. The [IRS explains estimated tax rules and payment requirements](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes).

Keep brokerage statements, Forms 1099, partnership K-1s, property records, and receipts for investment-related expenses in an organized system. Track acquisition dates, cost basis, and any events that change it. A regular review with your tax professional can help you prepare for upcoming distributions and sales, coordinate portfolio income with practice earnings, and keep the records you need for filing.

## How Can Physicians Manage Taxes on Investment Income?

Investment tax planning works best when it reflects your full financial picture. Portfolio income may sit alongside physician compensation, practice distributions, rental income, or locum tenens earnings. Together, these sources can affect your tax bracket, your exposure to the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT), and the estimated payments you may need to make during the year.

Start by reviewing expected income and planned investment activity before selling assets or making large gifts. A year with a practice sale, a substantial bonus, or significant capital gains may call for a different approach than a year with lower income. State taxes can add another layer, particularly if you work across state lines or own property elsewhere.

The goal is not to avoid every taxable event. It is to understand the potential tax impact and weigh your options without losing sight of your investment strategy. A tax professional can help assess tax rules, while a financial planner can connect those decisions to your portfolio, cash flow, retirement goals, and charitable plans. Regular reviews can help keep these moving parts coordinated. A physician-focused [tax planning strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians) can bring investment decisions into the context of your broader financial plan.

### Harvest losses, avoid wash sales, and carry forward unused losses

Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment in a taxable account for less than its tax basis to realize a capital loss. You may be able to use that loss to offset capital gains, reducing the tax impact of selling investments that have appreciated. Before placing a trade, consider whether the investment still fits your plan and what you would hold instead.

Be mindful of the wash-sale rule. The IRS generally disallows a loss if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale. Purchases in other accounts may also affect the result, so coordinate trades across your household and check with a tax professional. If you cannot use all your losses in the current year, unused amounts may generally carry forward. Review the IRS rules and common [tax mistakes investors can avoid](https://www.ameriprise.com/financial-goals-priorities/taxes/common-tax-mistakes-for-investors-to-avoid) before making a harvesting plan.

### Use capital losses to offset gains and, within limits, ordinary income

Capital losses generally offset capital gains. If your losses exceed your gains, you may usually deduct up to $3,000 of the remaining net loss against ordinary income each year, or $1,500 if you are married filing separately. Unused losses can generally carry forward to future tax years. Your specific tax benefit depends on your filing status, income, and the type of gains and losses involved.

Holding period matters, too. Investments held for one year or less generally produce short-term gains or losses; those held for more than a year generally produce long-term gains or losses. Short-term gains are typically taxed at ordinary income rates, while long-term gains may qualify for lower rates. Specific netting rules apply, so check how gains and losses interact before estimating your tax bill. The [IRS overview of capital gains and losses](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409) explains the basic rules.

### Place investments in suitable taxable and tax-advantaged accounts

Where you hold an investment can affect when and how its income is taxed. In a taxable brokerage account, interest, dividends, and realized gains may create a tax bill in the year they occur. Traditional retirement accounts generally defer taxes on investment growth until you take distributions, while Roth accounts may allow tax-free qualified withdrawals if requirements are met.

Some income-producing investments may be more suitable for tax-advantaged accounts, but asset location should reflect your complete plan. Consider liquidity needs, investment choices, fees, and withdrawal rules, not just the tax treatment. Physicians may have several accounts through an employer, a practice, and personal savings, so review them together before changing where you hold an investment. The [Tax Adviser’s physician tax planning overview](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2024/dec/tax-planning-for-physicians) discusses investment location as one element of broader tax planning.

### Coordinate sales with income, deductions, charitable giving, and NIIT thresholds

Before selling an appreciated investment, estimate how the gain could affect your total income for the year. A large sale may coincide with a bonus, practice income, or another financial event. Combined, these may affect your marginal tax rate and whether you owe the 3.8% NIIT. The tax applies to certain investment income when modified adjusted gross income exceeds the applicable threshold.

You may want to compare the tax impact of selling in different years, but timing should not outweigh your investment goals or expose your portfolio to unnecessary risk. Review planned deductions, charitable gifts, and other income events before deciding when to sell. Because NIIT calculations depend on your income and tax circumstances, ask your tax professional to model the potential result. The [IRS NIIT guidance](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/net-investment-income-tax) can help you understand the general rules.

### Consider donating appreciated securities as part of a broader giving plan

If charitable giving is part of your financial plan, donating appreciated securities held in a taxable account may be worth discussing. When a gift meets the applicable requirements, you may avoid realizing capital gains on the appreciation and may qualify for a deduction based on the asset’s fair market value. Eligibility and deduction limits can depend on how long you held the investment, the recipient organization, your income, and whether you itemize deductions.

A securities donation may be more tax-efficient than selling an investment, paying tax on the gain, and giving the remaining cash. First, confirm that the charity can accept the asset and that the gift fits your portfolio and giving priorities. Coordinate the transfer with your financial institution and tax professional, since processing may take time. Physicians with concentrated stock or a highly appreciated portfolio can consider charitable gifts as one part of a broader [legacy and estate plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/legacy-estate-planning).

### Weigh after-tax returns without letting taxes drive investment decisions

Tax efficiency matters, but it should support your investment plan rather than dictate it. Holding an investment only to delay a tax bill could leave your portfolio too concentrated or misaligned with your goals. Selling an asset solely to claim a loss may also be unhelpful if the trade disrupts your strategy or creates costs elsewhere.

Compare investments based on their role in your portfolio, risk, fees, liquidity, and potential after-tax return. Tax treatment is one factor, but future returns and tax rules can change. A diversified portfolio that reflects your time horizon and financial needs is generally more useful than one built only to reduce this year’s tax bill. Consider the full picture with your advisor, including how [wealth management for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians) can coordinate investment decisions with your broader financial priorities.

## How Does Practice Structure Affect a Physician’s Taxes?

Your practice structure can affect how you report income, pay employment taxes, and handle tax filings. It may also shape your ownership and operating options. But changing entities does not guarantee a lower tax bill. The right fit depends on your practice’s income, state rules, administrative costs, and plans for the business. A coordinated [practice and business plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/practice-owner-planning) can help you compare those factors with your broader [tax strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians).

### Compare sole proprietorship and single-member LLC tax treatment

A physician who operates a business alone without forming a separate entity is generally treated as a sole proprietor for federal tax purposes. A single-member LLC typically receives the same default tax treatment: the IRS treats it as separate from its owner for legal purposes, but not as a separate entity for federal income tax. The owner usually reports the practice’s income and expenses on an individual return, and net earnings may be subject to self-employment tax.

An LLC can offer legal protections under state law, but forming one does not automatically change the practice’s federal tax treatment. An eligible owner can elect a different tax classification, such as S-corporation treatment. Consider that option only after reviewing the tax, legal, and administrative effects with qualified professionals.

### Evaluate S-corporation elections, reasonable compensation, payroll taxes, and administrative costs

An eligible practice may elect S-corporation tax treatment. A physician who works in the practice must generally receive reasonable compensation as an employee. The practice runs payroll and pays applicable payroll taxes on that salary. Remaining profit may be distributed to owners, and those distributions generally are not subject to Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes. They still count as income for federal tax purposes.

The potential tax difference depends on practice profits and a defensible salary for the physician’s work. There is no universal salary formula or guaranteed savings. S-corporations also bring payroll costs, separate tax filings, and recordkeeping responsibilities. The IRS outlines its [reasonable compensation rules](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporation-compensation-and-medical-insurance-issues). Compare expected tax savings with the costs and responsibilities of maintaining the election.

### Weigh C-corporation taxes and potential double taxation

A C corporation files its own tax return and pays corporate income tax on its profits. If it distributes after-tax profits to shareholders as dividends, those shareholders may owe personal income tax on the dividends as well. This is known as double taxation. The federal corporate income tax rate is 21%, but the combined tax cost depends on the practice’s profits, how much it distributes or retains, and the owners’ personal tax situations.

A C corporation may suit certain ownership or business goals, but it is not automatically the best structure for a medical practice. Consider how you expect to use practice profits, whether you plan to bring in investors, and how a future sale or transfer could be taxed. Ask a tax professional to model the corporate and shareholder impact, including state taxes, before deciding.

### Consider state rules, practice needs, and compliance costs before changing entities

State law can affect which structures physicians may use and how those entities must operate. Some states require medical practices to use a professional corporation or professional LLC, or limit who may own one. States may also impose annual fees, franchise taxes, extra filings, or specific payroll requirements. If you work or own a practice in more than one state, you may have additional filing obligations.

Taxes are only one part of the decision. Weigh liability protections, ownership plans, hiring needs, and the time and expense of maintaining the entity. Before making a change, consult a tax professional and an attorney who understand healthcare practices in the states where you work. Revisit the structure when your practice, income, or state requirements change.

### Remember that practice structure doesn’t automatically reduce taxes on personal investments

Your practice’s entity type does not, by itself, change how your personally held investments are taxed. Interest, dividends, and capital gains are generally reported according to the account or asset that generated them and your individual tax situation. For example, electing S-corporation treatment for your practice does not make gains in your personal brokerage account tax-free.

Business income can still affect your overall tax picture, including your tax bracket and potential exposure to the net investment income tax. Investment income held by an entity may receive different treatment depending on its structure and circumstances. Keep practice and personal accounts clearly documented, and review both as part of your broader [physician wealth management plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/wealth-management-for-physicians).

### Ask how specified service business rules may affect the qualified business income deduction

The qualified business income (QBI) deduction may let eligible owners of pass-through businesses deduct part of their qualifying business income. However, medical practices are generally classified as specified service trades or businesses. For physician owners, eligibility may depend on taxable income, filing status, and other rules. The deduction can be reduced or unavailable when income exceeds applicable limits.

Choosing S-corporation treatment does not remove a medical practice from the specified-service rules or guarantee that its owners qualify for the deduction. Ask a tax professional to review your eligibility under the rules for the relevant tax year, along with your household income and other deductions. The IRS provides an [overview of the QBI deduction](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/qualified-business-income-deduction), including how specified service businesses are treated.

## How Can Retirement Plans Support Tax Planning?

Retirement plans can affect both your current tax bill and the taxes you may owe later. For physicians, the right mix depends on employer benefits, practice ownership, investment income, and retirement goals. Review the options together, rather than choosing an account based on one tax feature.

### Compare 401(k), 403(b), 457(b), and IRA options

A 401(k) or 403(b) may allow pre-tax contributions, which can reduce taxable income in the year you contribute. If your employer offers a Roth option, contributions are made with after-tax dollars, and qualified withdrawals may be tax-free. Some hospitals and government employers also offer a 457(b), but its rules depend on whether the plan is governmental or nongovernmental.

An IRA may provide another way to save, though income and workplace plan coverage affect whether traditional IRA contributions are deductible or direct Roth IRA contributions are allowed. Compare employer matches, fees, access, and withdrawal rules along with tax treatment. The [IRS overview of retirement plans](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans) explains common plan types. A tax professional can help determine which options fit your income and investment picture.

### Evaluate profit-sharing and cash balance plans for eligible practice owners

Practice owners may be able to save more through a profit-sharing plan or a cash balance plan, in addition to a standard retirement plan. Profit-sharing plans can let a practice contribute to eligible employees’ accounts, subject to plan terms and annual limits. A cash balance plan is a type of defined benefit plan that may allow larger tax-deferred contributions for some owners, though it can involve actuarial work, administration, and ongoing funding commitments.

These plans may be worth considering when practice income is strong and predictable. But employee eligibility, staffing costs, cash flow, and required contributions all affect whether a plan makes sense. Review the potential deduction alongside the plan’s long-term obligations and your practice’s needs. A coordinated [retirement strategy for physicians](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians) can help you assess how a plan fits your broader financial goals.

### Coordinate plan eligibility, contribution limits, and employer plans

Before contributing, check which plans you can use and how they interact. A physician may have access to a workplace 401(k) or 403(b), a 457(b), and an IRA, but eligibility and contribution limits vary. If you own a practice and also work for another employer, review the plans together. The details can affect how much you may contribute and whether a strategy is available.

A backdoor Roth IRA strategy also requires coordination. The nondeductible IRA contribution and Roth conversion must be reported correctly, and existing pre-tax IRA balances may affect the tax result under the pro-rata rule. The [Tax Adviser’s discussion of tax planning for physicians](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2024/dec/tax-planning-for-physicians) highlights why retirement contributions and tax preparation should be considered together. Confirm your eligibility and reporting requirements with a tax professional before acting.

### Weigh current deductions against taxes on future withdrawals

Pre-tax contributions may reduce taxable income now, but withdrawals from tax-deferred accounts are generally taxed as ordinary income. That tradeoff matters for physicians who currently earn a high income and expect their financial picture to change in retirement. A deduction today can be valuable, but it does not necessarily mean you will pay less tax over your lifetime.

Consider expected retirement income, other savings, required distributions, and how your investments are taxed. Taxable accounts, Roth accounts, and tax-deferred plans each have different rules, so holding a mix may give you more flexibility later. Morgan Stanley’s guidance on tax-efficient investing recommends weighing current tax benefits against taxes that may apply in the future. Revisit your projections when your practice income, investment gains, or retirement timeline changes.

### Assess Roth contributions and conversions alongside income, tax brackets, and investment income

Roth contributions or conversions may suit you if paying taxes now is preferable to paying taxes on future withdrawals. A conversion moves money from a tax-deferred account to a Roth account, and the converted amount is generally included in taxable income for that year. For physicians with significant investment income, the added income may affect tax brackets or exposure to the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax, depending on overall income and applicable thresholds.

Before converting, estimate the tax cost and decide how you would pay it. Consider salary, practice income, realized capital gains, deductions, and expected changes in income. Direct Roth IRA contributions also have income limits, and backdoor Roth contributions involve additional tax considerations. Ask a tax professional to compare scenarios before making a conversion, especially if you expect investment gains or other income changes in the same year.

## What Should Physicians Ask a Tax Professional?

A productive tax conversation looks beyond the return you’re filing now. W-2 wages, practice income, investments, rental property, and retirement accounts can all affect your tax picture. Ask your tax professional to consider how these pieces work together, then explain which decisions may need attention before year-end.

Bring a current summary of your income, investments, practice structure, and goals. A resident weighing retirement contributions may have different questions from a practice owner planning a sale or managing employees. The [Tax Adviser’s guide to physician tax planning](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2024/dec/tax-planning-for-physicians) outlines how benefits, retirement contributions, and other choices can shape a tax strategy. Coordinate tax guidance with your broader [financial plan](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/tax-planning-for-physicians), especially when a tax decision could affect retirement, practice ownership, or long-term investments.

### Which strategies fit my investments, income, practice structure, and state tax situation?

Ask your tax professional to review your income sources together: W-2 wages, practice earnings, investment income, rental income, and compensation from locum tenens work. These sources may be taxed differently, and your practice structure can affect how business income and payroll taxes are reported. Share your goals, too, such as contributing to retirement accounts, buying into a practice, or selling an investment property.

If you work or own property in more than one state, ask where you may need to file and whether you could claim a credit for taxes paid elsewhere. Residency rules and state tax treatment vary, so a strategy that works in one location may not work in another. Ask your tax professional to assess these details alongside your investments and practice structure, and to coordinate with your financial planner when decisions affect your long-term goals.

### How might capital gains, NIIT, or estimated payments affect my tax bill?

Before selling investments, ask how the sale could affect your total tax bill. Your tax professional can explain whether a gain is short-term or long-term, whether your income may subject you to the Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT), and how these taxes may interact. The [IRS overview of the NIIT](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc559) explains which types of income and taxpayers may be affected.

If you receive income without enough tax withheld, including practice or investment income, ask whether estimated payments are appropriate and how much to pay. Bring information about expected income, withholding, and planned transactions so your tax professional can estimate your liability. Review the plan during the year if your income changes. That can help you avoid a surprise bill and discuss payment adjustments before a deadline.

### Could loss harvesting, charitable giving, or account placement improve tax efficiency?

Ask whether selling investments at a loss could offset realized gains, and how wash-sale rules may affect the timing of a sale and repurchase. If your losses exceed your gains, ask how much may be used against ordinary income and whether unused losses can carry forward. The [IRS guidance on capital gains and losses](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409) is a helpful starting point for that discussion.

You can also ask whether donating appreciated securities fits your charitable and financial goals. Confirm the tax treatment, eligibility, and documentation requirements before making a gift. Finally, review which investments belong in taxable accounts and which may be suitable for tax-advantaged accounts. Account placement can affect when investment income is taxed, but taxes should be one consideration alongside your risk tolerance, investment plan, and need for access to funds.

### Should I consider a Roth conversion, and how would it affect my overall taxes?

Ask your tax professional to estimate the tax impact before converting assets from a traditional retirement account to a Roth account. The converted amount is generally included in taxable income for that year. Depending on your circumstances, the added income could affect your tax bracket, state tax bill, NIIT exposure, or other income-based calculations.

Rather than treating a conversion as an all-or-nothing decision, ask your advisor to compare different amounts and timing. A lower-income year may present an opportunity, but the conversion should also fit your retirement timeline, expected future income, and ability to pay the tax bill. Review the [IRS comparison of Roth and traditional accounts](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/roth-comparison-chart), and coordinate the decision with your [retirement strategy](https://elitephysicianwealthplanning.com/retirement-planning-for-physicians). A tax projection can help you weigh the current cost against potential future benefits.

### Which records should I keep, and how often should we review my plan?

Ask your tax professional which documents to keep and for how long. Depending on your finances, useful records may include investment statements, purchase confirmations, cost-basis details, charitable gift receipts, rental expenses, estimated tax payments, and documents related to practice income. Organizing records throughout the year makes it easier to verify transactions and answer questions when preparing your return.

Before signing, review your tax return and ask about any figures or assumptions you do not understand. The [National Society of Accountants’ questions for tax preparers](https://connect.nsacct.org/findataxpro/consumer/questions) encourages taxpayers to confirm they are comfortable with the return’s accuracy. Ask whether a midyear review makes sense, particularly if your income, investments, practice, or work location changes. Regular check-ins give you time to discuss the tax effects of a decision before you make it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do physicians pay a different tax rate on investment income than on salary?**\
It depends on the income type. Interest and short-term capital gains are generally taxed as ordinary income, while qualified dividends and long-term gains may receive different federal rates. Your total income, filing status, and state tax rules also matter.

**Can I owe taxes on investments without selling them?**\
Yes. Mutual funds and ETFs may distribute taxable dividends or capital gains even if you reinvest the money. Partnership income reported on a Schedule K-1 may also be taxable even when you receive no cash distribution.

**When should I review the tax impact of selling an investment?**\
Review it before placing the sale, especially if you expect a bonus, strong practice earnings, or another large income event. A tax professional can estimate how the gain may affect your federal and state taxes, including potential NIIT.

**Can investment losses reduce my tax bill?**\
Capital losses can generally offset capital gains. If your losses exceed your gains, you may be able to deduct a limited amount against ordinary income and carry unused losses forward. Wash-sale rules can affect whether a loss is allowed, so check the timing before buying the same or a substantially identical investment.

**What records should I keep for investment and rental income?**\
Keep brokerage statements, Forms 1099, partnership K-1s, purchase records, cost-basis details, and documentation for rental income and expenses. Clear records help your tax professional report each income source accurately and assess estimated tax payments.

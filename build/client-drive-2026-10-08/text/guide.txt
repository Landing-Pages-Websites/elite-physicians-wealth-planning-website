1. Executive Summary for Physicians
Physicians often earn high income, but high income alone does not create financial independence. The difference comes from
coordinated decisions: how income is taxed, how retirement plans are designed, how cash flow is allocated, how student loans are
handled, how risk is protected, and how wealth is eventually distributed.
The central planning challenge is not simply finding deductions. It is building a repeatable annual process that connects tax planning,
retirement planning, investment strategy, debt management, practice planning, and legacy planning.

The physician planning gap
      Tax planning is often reactive and happens after the year is over.
      Retirement contributions are sometimes maximized without evaluating tax brackets, Roth opportunities, after-tax contributions, or
       practice-owner plan design.
      High-income W-2 physicians may miss benefit, withholding, 457(b), HSA, and backdoor Roth coordination opportunities.
      Practice owners may miss larger opportunities in cash balance plans, defined benefit plans, profit sharing, accountable plans, entity
       structure, and succession planning.
      Retirement-income planning often starts too late, creating avoidable RMD, Medicare IRMAA, and legacy-tax issues.

The Elite Physician Planning Objective
    Planning Objective
    Help physicians keep more of what they earn, build more for the future, and retire with greater confidence through a coordinated annual
    process that converts current law into actionable planning decisions.

3. The Elite Physician Tax Strategy Map
The strongest physician planning engagements start by mapping the tax surface before recommending products or tactics. A doctor
with $600,000 of W-2 income needs a different planning sequence than a surgeon with K-1 practice income, a dentist with an S
corporation, or a physician executive approaching Medicare age.




Core diagnostic questions
1.   What type of income is being earned, and who controls its timing?
2.   Which tax surfaces are triggered: ordinary income, payroll tax, NIIT, SALT, AMT, QBI/SSTB, capital gains, Medicare IRMAA, or estate
     tax?
3.   What planning levers exist before year-end?
4.   Which strategies require a CPA, TPA, attorney, payroll provider, custodian, or employer approval?
5.   What should be reviewed annually to prevent tax drift?

 Advisor Positioning
 The planning process should lead with diagnosis. The client should see the relationship among taxes, retirement savings, benefits, and
 implementation before any investment or insurance recommendation is discussed.

4. Federal Income Tax Planning for High-Income
Physicians
Most physicians are affected by a compressed set of planning variables: high ordinary income, limited time, concentrated career risk,
substantial payroll taxes, and delayed retirement planning. Federal tax planning should focus on what can be controlled before
December 31 and what can be positioned for future years.

Deep dive: bracket management
The 2026 federal rate schedule keeps seven individual income tax brackets, with the highest marginal rate of 37% beginning at
income above $640,600 for single filers and $768,700 for married couples filing jointly. A high-income physician often cannot avoid
the top bracket in a high-income year, but may still manage taxable income through retirement contributions, business deductions,
charitable giving, loss harvesting, and timing of bonuses, distributions, and Roth conversions. [S1]
Planning moves to consider
          Compare current-year marginal rate with projected retirement marginal rate before choosing traditional vs Roth contributions.
          Model bunching of charitable deductions when the standard deduction would otherwise reduce the value of annual giving.
          Use multi-year capital gain and tax-loss harvesting rather than waiting until December.
          For practice owners, coordinate major equipment purchases, retirement plan contributions, entity distributions, and owner compensation
           before year-end.
          For physicians near retirement, model Roth conversions before RMDs, Social Security, and Medicare IRMAA exposure increase.

Deep dive: SALT and itemized deductions
For 2026, the overall deduction limit for state and local income, sales, and property taxes is $40,400, reduced when modified AGI
exceeds $505,000. The deduction cannot be reduced below $10,000. Married filing separately amounts are lower. This is especially
relevant for high-income physicians in states with higher income and property taxes. [S4]

    SALT planning questions
    • Will the physician itemize after accounting for SALT, mortgage interest, and charitable giving?
    • Will income push the taxpayer into the SALT phase-down range?
    • Should charitable gifts be bunched into a donor-advised fund year?
    • Does the physician own a pass-through business that may have an entity-level state tax election? State-specific review is required.

Deep dive: AMT awareness
For 2026, the AMT exemption amount is $90,100 for unmarried individuals and $140,200 for married couples filing jointly, with
phaseout beginning at $500,000 and $1,000,000 respectively. AMT review is especially relevant when a physician has incentive stock
options, large state tax deductions, unusual itemized deductions, or complex K-1 income. [S1]

Many employed physicians assume they have limited planning opportunities because they receive a W-2. The planning tools are

Deep dive: governmental vs non-governmental 457(b)
A governmental 457(b) can often be rolled to an IRA or another eligible plan after separation, while a non-governmental 457(b) may
remain subject to the employer’s creditors and have more restrictive distribution elections. The financial value of a 457(b) contribution
depends on tax deferral, investment options, distribution risk, and the physician’s future tax rate.

Deep dive: employer student-loan assistance
Under current IRS guidance, employer-provided educational assistance can include payments of principal or interest on a qualified
education loan for the employee’s own education. For 2025 and 2026, the first $5,250 of qualifying educational assistance benefits
can be excluded from income if provided under a qualifying plan. [S16]

 W-2 physician action items
 • Request a benefits summary and plan documents, not just payroll screenshots.
 • Ask whether the employer offers 457(b), HSA, supplemental disability, legal plans, or student-loan assistance.
 • Review whether Roth, pre-tax, or mixed salary deferrals fit the physician’s career stage and projected retirement tax bracket.
 • Track basis and conversions carefully if using backdoor Roth strategies.

6. Practice Owners: Entity, Payroll, QBI/SSTB, and
Deduction Strategy
Practice owners have additional planning levers because they may influence entity structure, compensation, retirement plan design,
employee benefits, depreciation, and succession planning. The opportunity is larger, but so is the implementation risk.

Entity structure is not tax planning by itself
A medical practice may operate as an LLC, partnership, professional corporation, S corporation, C corporation, or another
professional entity depending on state law, ownership, licensing, and legal requirements. The entity choice affects payroll tax,
qualified plan design, fringe benefits, basis, distributions, buy-sell agreements, and eventual sale or succession planning.

Deep dive: S corporation planning
S corporation planning should never be reduced to simply “take less salary.” A shareholder-employee must receive reasonable
compensation for services. The right compensation level depends on specialty, clinical hours, administrative duties, practice profits,
market compensation, retirement plan design, and payroll-tax exposure.

Deep dive: QBI and medical practices
The qualified business income deduction may allow eligible taxpayers to deduct up to 20% of QBI from certain domestic pass-through
businesses. However, physicians are typically in health-related specified service trade or business territory, and income limits,
wage/property limitations, and form-specific rules can materially reduce or eliminate the deduction for high-income owners. [S7]
For 2026 planning, use current IRS forms and instructions when available. Draft 2026 QBI forms indicate that taxpayers above
income thresholds may need Form 8995-A rather than simplified Form 8995, with threshold figures around $201,750 single and
$403,500 married filing jointly in draft materials. Confirm final forms before relying on exact thresholds. [S8]

Deep dive: equipment, depreciation, and Section 179
Practice owners purchasing equipment should coordinate business need, financing, depreciation, state conformity, and income
timing. The IRS has issued guidance under the Working Families Tax Cuts for permanent 100% additional first-year depreciation for
eligible depreciable property acquired after January 19, 2025. [S18]
IRS Publication 946 states that for tax years beginning in 2026, the maximum Section 179 expense deduction is $2,560,000 and
begins to phase out when Section 179 property placed in service exceeds $4,090,000. Certain SUVs have a separate $32,000 limit.
[S19]

Practice owner planning checklist
   Review entity structure annually with CPA and legal counsel.
   Confirm reasonable compensation methodology for S-corp owners.
   Evaluate whether retirement plan design should move beyond basic deferrals into profit sharing, new comparability, cash balance, or
    defined benefit design.
   Coordinate major equipment purchases with actual clinical need and tax benefit timing.
   Review accountable plan reimbursements, business insurance, lease arrangements, buy-sell agreements, and succession planning.
   Do not implement deductions without documentation, business purpose, and CPA review.

8. Roth Planning, Backdoor Roth, After-Tax Contributions,
and Asset Location
Roth planning is less about one contribution and more about managing tax flexibility. A physician may benefit from having assets in
three tax buckets: taxable accounts, tax-deferred accounts, and tax-free Roth accounts. This flexibility becomes valuable in retirement
when managing RMDs, capital gains, Medicare IRMAA, and survivor taxes.

Backdoor Roth IRA planning
Many high-income physicians earn too much for direct Roth IRA contributions. A backdoor Roth strategy may still be possible, but it
requires careful review of pre-tax IRA balances and the pro-rata rule. The IRA contribution limit for 2026 is $7,500, with a $1,100
catch-up contribution for individuals age 50 or older. [S2]

 Backdoor Roth checklist
 • Check all traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances before conversion.
 • Confirm whether rollover to an employer plan can isolate basis and reduce pro-rata complications.
 • Track nondeductible IRA basis on Form 8606.
 • Coordinate both spouses separately.
 • Avoid assuming the conversion is tax-free without reviewing prior IRA balances.

Roth vs traditional decision framework
                                       Situation                                                             Likely Planning Direction

 Current tax rate is unusually high                                                Traditional may be more attractive if future rates are lower.

 Current tax rate is temporarily low                                               Roth contribution or conversion may be attractive.

 Early-career physician with rising income                                         Roth strategies may be more compelling before peak earning years.

 Practice owner seeking cash-flow deduction                                        Traditional/cash balance contributions may create immediate tax leverage.

 Near-retirement physician before RMDs                                             Partial Roth conversions may reduce future RMD/IRMAA pressure.

 Legacy-focused physician                                                          Roth assets may provide estate and beneficiary planning flexibility.

Asset location
Asset location means coordinating which assets are held in taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth accounts. For example, tax-inefficient
assets may be more appropriate in tax-deferred accounts, while broad-market equity strategies with favorable capital-gain treatment
may be efficient in taxable accounts. The exact answer depends on the portfolio, tax bracket, withdrawal plan, and estate objective.

Practice owners should review group health coverage, disability benefits, life insurance, long-term care planning, employee benefits,
educational assistance programs, and retirement plan benefits together. The objective is not merely to add benefits, but to create a
compensation structure that supports recruitment, retention, owner planning, and tax efficiency.

10. Charitable Giving, SALT, AMT, and Itemized Deduction
Strategy
Many physicians are charitably inclined, but tax-efficient giving requires coordination with the standard deduction, itemized
deductions, appreciated assets, donor-advised funds, QCDs, capital gains, and estate goals.

Current law charitable giving rule for non-itemizers
Beginning with tax year 2026, taxpayers who do not itemize may deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 if filing jointly) of cash contributions to
certain qualified organizations. Taxpayers who itemize generally use Schedule A subject to applicable limits and substantiation rules.
[S6]

Bunching strategy
When annual charitable gifts do not generate an itemized deduction because the standard deduction is higher, physicians may
consider bunching multiple years of giving into one year. A donor-advised fund may allow a larger deduction in the contribution year
while grants to charities can occur over time. This strategy requires cash-flow review, charitable intent, and documentation.

Appreciated securities
A physician with taxable investments may consider donating appreciated securities held long-term instead of cash. The potential
benefit is avoiding capital gain on the donated asset while receiving a charitable deduction if itemizing and otherwise eligible. This is
especially relevant after a bull market, concentrated stock position, or practice sale liquidity event.

Qualified charitable distributions
For physicians age 70 1/2 or older, QCDs can be used to send IRA assets directly to eligible charities. QCDs may count toward
RMDs and can reduce adjusted gross income when properly executed. Current IRS retirement guidance reflects a 2026 aggregate
QCD exclusion limit of $111,000. [S15]

 Charitable planning questions
 • Will the client itemize in the current year?
 • Are gifts being made in cash, appreciated securities, or retirement assets?
 • Will a QCD reduce AGI, RMD exposure, or IRMAA pressure?
 • Are substantiation requirements satisfied before the return is filed?
 • Does the giving strategy align with estate and family objectives?

11. Student Loan and PSLF Planning for Physicians
Student-loan strategy can materially affect physician cash flow, career decisions, filing status, and retirement savings capacity. The
right approach depends on loan type, employer type, income trajectory, specialty, family status, and whether the physician is pursuing
forgiveness.

PSLF current framework
Federal Student Aid describes Public Service Loan Forgiveness as forgiving the remaining balance on eligible Direct Loans after 120
qualifying monthly payments under a qualifying repayment plan while working full-time for a qualifying employer. Many physicians
employed by government hospitals, academic medical centers, and certain nonprofit hospitals may need to evaluate PSLF eligibility.
[S14]

IDR and current court-action awareness
Federal Student Aid reports that a March 10, 2026 court order prevents the Department of Education from implementing the SAVE
Plan and parts of other IDR plans, while eligible borrowers can apply for or recertify under IBR, ICR, and PAYE as described by
StudentAid.gov. This area is especially fluid and should be checked before making repayment decisions. [S17]

Physician student-loan decision tree
                                    Situation                                                                    Planning Focus

 Works for nonprofit/government employer                                        Evaluate PSLF, employer eligibility, Direct Loan status, repayment plan, and
                                                                                annual certification.

 Private practice or for-profit employer                                        Compare refinancing, standard repayment, aggressive payoff, and investing
                                                                                tradeoffs.

 Resident/fellow                                                                Coordinate income certification, PSLF count, interest, disability coverage, and
                                                                                early retirement savings.

 High-income attending                                                          Model after-tax cash flow before refinancing or accelerating payoff.

 Dual-income household                                                          Review filing status, IDR payment effect, spouse loans, and tax cost.

 Practice owner                                                                 Consider employer educational assistance plan design when recruiting or
                                                                                retaining physicians.

 Student-loan action items
 • Download loan details from StudentAid.gov before modeling.
 • Confirm employer eligibility using the PSLF Help Tool or official employer search.
 • Do not refinance federal loans privately until forgiveness, IDR, deferment, and protection tradeoffs are reviewed.
 • Coordinate student-loan payments with retirement plan contributions and emergency reserves.

12. Retirement Income, RMDs, Social Security, Medicare,
and IRMAA
Retirement-income planning for physicians is tax planning. Decisions around Roth conversions, taxable-account withdrawals, pension
elections, Social Security, RMDs, charitable giving, and Medicare premiums can interact over decades.

RMD rules
IRS retirement guidance states that account owners generally must begin taking required minimum distributions from traditional IRAs,
SEP IRAs, SIMPLE IRAs, and retirement plan accounts when they reach age 73. Roth IRAs and designated Roth accounts in 401(k)
or 403(b) plans generally do not require lifetime RMDs for the owner, though beneficiary rules apply. [S11]

Medicare and IRMAA
For 2026, CMS reports the standard Medicare Part B monthly premium is $202.90 and the annual Part B deductible is $283. High-
income beneficiaries pay income-related monthly adjustment amounts. In 2026, the first Part B IRMAA tier begins above MAGI of
$109,000 for individual tax returns and $218,000 for joint returns. [S12]

                      2026 Part B Full-Coverage MAGI Tier                                                   Total Monthly Part B Premium

 Individual MAGI <= $109,000 / Joint <= $218,000                                   $202.90

 Individual > $109,000 to $137,000 / Joint > $218,000 to $274,000                  $284.10

 Individual > $137,000 to $171,000 / Joint > $274,000 to $342,000                  $405.80

 Individual > $171,000 to $205,000 / Joint > $342,000 to $410,000                  $527.50

 Individual > $205,000 to < $500,000 / Joint > $410,000 to < $750,000              $649.20

Roth conversion window
Physicians often have a conversion window after retirement but before RMDs and Social Security. During this period, partial Roth
conversions may convert future taxable RMDs into Roth assets. The analysis should include current bracket, future RMDs, survivor
tax filing status, IRMAA thresholds, charitable intent, and estate objectives.

Social Security planning
Social Security should be evaluated as part of a retirement-income plan, not in isolation. Physicians with long high-income careers
often qualify for significant benefits, but timing decisions should reflect longevity, spouse benefits, survivor benefits, work plans, tax
cost, investment withdrawals, and Medicare premiums.

 Retirement-income planning sequence
 • Project after-tax retirement spending need.
 • Map tax-deferred, Roth, taxable, HSA, real estate, pension, and business-sale assets.
 • Model Roth conversions before RMDs and Medicare IRMAA where appropriate.
 • Coordinate charitable giving, QCDs, and estate goals.
 • Review annually because income, brackets, premiums, and health needs change.

13. Estate, Gift, Asset Protection, and Legacy Planning
Estate planning for physicians should protect family decision-making, preserve assets, prepare for incapacity, coordinate beneficiary
designations, and transfer wealth efficiently. The legal documents are necessary, but the ongoing coordination is where planning
often breaks down.

2026 federal estate planning context
For estates of decedents dying in 2026, the federal basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000. High-net-worth physicians, practice
owners, and clients with concentrated real estate, business value, or large retirement accounts should review estate planning while
the exemption is known. [S1]

Essential documents to review
   Will and/or revocable living trust.
   Durable financial power of attorney.
   Health care power of attorney and advance directive.
   HIPAA authorization.
   Guardian provisions for minor children.
   Beneficiary designations for retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities, and transfer-on-death accounts.
   Buy-sell agreement for practice owners.
   Entity documents, operating agreements, shareholder agreements, and employment agreements.

Asset protection
Physicians face professional risk, business risk, creditor exposure, cyber/privacy risk, and lifestyle-related liability. Asset protection
planning should be done prospectively, while solvent, and with legal counsel. Core planning may include malpractice insurance,
umbrella liability coverage, entity separation, retirement accounts, trust planning, titling review, and contractual risk management.

Practice transition and exit planning
A physician practice may represent a major component of net worth. Exit planning should address valuation, buyer readiness,
associate agreements, succession, real estate, tax structure of sale, goodwill, restrictive covenants, retention incentives, and post-
sale income planning.

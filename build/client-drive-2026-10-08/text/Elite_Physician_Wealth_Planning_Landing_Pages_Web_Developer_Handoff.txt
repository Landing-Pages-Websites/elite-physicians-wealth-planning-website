Financing Designed Around the Physician and the Practice
Physicians frequently operate in two financial worlds at the same time: the medical practice and the personal financial plan. A financing decision made inside the practice can affect liquidity, retirement contributions, investment strategy, insurance needs, and the physician's personal balance sheet.
Our approach is designed to connect those decisions. We help evaluate the financing need first, then identify lending options that may fit the transaction and the broader financial plan.

How the Physician Financing Process Works
1. Define the Objective
Determine what the physician is trying to accomplish: acquire a practice, purchase real estate, expand, buy equipment, improve liquidity, refinance debt, or complete an ownership transaction.
2. Review the Financial Picture
Assess the relevant practice financials, existing debt, cash flow, use of proceeds, ownership structure, collateral, and other lender considerations.
3. Evaluate Financing Alternatives
Identify conventional commercial and SBA financing alternatives that may fit the transaction.
4. Prepare for the Lending Process
Organize the financial information and documentation commonly requested by lenders, including tax returns, financial statements, debt schedules, projections, purchase agreements, and property information as applicable.
5. Coordinate the Transaction
Work with the physician, lender, CPA, attorney, practice consultant, and other professionals when appropriate.
6. Integrate the Financing Into the Financial Plan
After financing is placed, evaluate the effect on practice cash flow, personal liquidity, retirement funding, insurance, investments, and long-term wealth goals.

Your Practice May Be One of Your Most Valuable Assets
The medical practice may provide current income, retirement-plan contributions, employment for staff, equity value, real estate opportunities, future sale value, and family financial security. Protecting the practice is therefore part of protecting the physician's personal wealth.
Business Insurance Solutions for Medical Practices
Commercial General Liability
Protection for certain third-party claims involving bodily injury, property damage, and other covered liabilities. Needs vary based on patient traffic, premises, contracts, staffing, and operations.
Professional Liability
Professional liability exposure should be evaluated based on the physician's specialty, practice structure, services, contractual obligations, and existing coverage. This coverage is distinct from general business liability.
Commercial Property
Coverage for buildings, office contents, medical equipment, computers, furniture, tenant improvements, inventory, and other covered property depending on the policy.
Business Interruption / Business Income
Coverage that may help address eligible lost income and continuing expenses when a covered event prevents the practice from operating.
Cyber Liability & Data Breach
Coverage designed to address certain financial exposures associated with cyber events, privacy incidents, ransomware, system interruption, and data-breach response. Insurance complements, but does not replace, cybersecurity controls.
Employment Practices Liability - EPLI
Coverage that may respond to certain employment-related allegations such as discrimination, harassment, retaliation, or wrongful termination, subject to policy terms.
Workers' Compensation
Coverage addressing employee work-related injuries or illnesses as required or applicable under state law and policy terms.
Commercial Auto
Coverage for vehicles owned, leased, or used in connection with business operations, depending on how the practice uses vehicles.
Business Owner Policy - BOP
For eligible businesses, a BOP may package core coverages such as general liability, commercial property, and business income into one policy.
Commercial Umbrella / Excess Liability
Additional liability limits that may sit above certain underlying commercial policies, subject to policy structure and eligibility.
Key Person Life Insurance
Life insurance designed to help address the financial impact that the death of an owner, physician, or other key person could have on the business.
Buy-Sell & Business Continuity Funding
Insurance strategies that may help fund ownership transition obligations and support continuity when a partner or owner dies or becomes disabled, depending on the arrangement.
Why Physicians Need an Integrated Risk Review
A medical practice may have several policies purchased at different times, from different carriers, for different reasons. The risk is not always a lack of insurance; sometimes it is a lack of coordination.
A physician practice risk review should consider how the policies work together, where meaningful gaps may exist, whether limits reflect the current size of the practice, and how business protection connects with personal financial and succession planning.

The Physician Practice Risk Review
1. Understand the Practice
Review ownership, specialty, locations, employees, property, vehicles, technology, contracts, revenue concentration, and business structure.
2. Identify Major Exposures
Consider liability, property, cyber, employment, business interruption, key-person, vehicle, and other material exposures.
3. Review Current Coverage
Examine existing policies, limits, deductibles, exclusions, endorsements, renewal dates, and overlapping or missing protection.
4. Prioritize the Risks
Separate critical exposures from lower-priority issues so recommendations remain practical and financially disciplined.
5. Coordinate Coverage
Where appropriate, coordinate with insurance carriers, brokers, attorneys, CPAs, lenders, and other professionals.
6. Connect Risk Management to the Financial Plan
Consider how insurance decisions affect practice continuity, debt obligations, retirement strategy, buy-sell planning, succession, and the physician's personal financial security.
Common Physician Practice Risk Questions
If the practice is forced to close temporarily, how long can it continue paying payroll and fixed expenses?
Are cyber limits appropriate for the amount and sensitivity of patient and employee information maintained by the practice?
Does the practice have insurance appropriate for its employees, vehicles, property, and contractual obligations?
Would the business remain financially stable if a key physician died unexpectedly?
Is there a funded plan for the death, disability, retirement, or departure of an owner?
Have insurance limits kept pace with practice growth, equipment purchases, new locations, or increased payroll?
Are the physician's personal wealth plan and practice risk-management plan coordinated?
